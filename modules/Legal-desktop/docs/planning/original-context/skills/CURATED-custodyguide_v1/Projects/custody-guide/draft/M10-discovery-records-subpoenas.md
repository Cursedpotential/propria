# Why this module exists

Once a case is filed, **discovery** is the formal, rules-governed process for getting information — from the other party, and from third parties like schools, clinics, employers, and banks. It is the engine that turns "I know she does this" into a record a court can look at.

Two ideas carry the whole module, and both cut against what television and the internet suggest:

1. **Discovery is court-controlled, and narrower than you think — especially in a custody case, and especially for someone without a lawyer.** There is no automatic right to rummage through the other parent's life. A domestic-relations case is *exempt* from the ordinary civil disclosures, the court can rein in the scope, and a self-represented party cannot issue a subpoena to a third party the way a lawyer can.
2. **Getting a record is not the same as getting it admitted.** A stack of subpoenaed school records is not evidence until someone lays the foundation for it. Discovery that ignores the admissibility rules in **Modules 6 and 7** produces a binder the judge never reads.

Underneath both is a discipline the rest of this book keeps returning to: **plan discovery by the disputed element, not by curiosity.** In a custody or support case, the genuine disputes are usually about income, specific records, and third-party accounts — not about re-litigating the whole relationship. Every request you make should trace back to a row in the **Best-Interest Evidence Matrix** you built in **Module 2**: *which factor does this prove, and how would it get admitted?* If a request does not answer both questions, it is curiosity, and curiosity is where discovery goes wrong.

And a warning that runs through every section below, because it is the law of this module: **every tool here can be aimed back at you.** The same interrogatories you serve, the other parent serves on you — under oath. The same medical and mental-health records you reach for can be reached for against you (**factor (g)**, Module 2.4). A motion to compel that you lose can cost *you* the other side's fees. Discovery is symmetrical by design, and a parent who treats it as a weapon usually gets cut by it.

## 10.1 What discovery is for — and the proportionality rule that bounds it

The scope of discovery is one rule, and it is worth reading slowly, because it is both the grant of power and the leash on it:

> Parties may obtain discovery regarding any non-privileged matter that is relevant to any party's claims or defenses and proportional to the needs of the case, taking into account all pertinent factors, including whether the burden or expense of the proposed discovery outweighs its likely benefit, the complexity of the case, the importance of the issues at stake in the action, the amount in controversy, and the parties' resources and access to relevant information. Information within the scope of discovery need not be admissible in evidence to be discoverable.
> — MCR 2.302(B)(1)

Three things follow:

- **"Relevant to any party's claims or defenses."** Not "everything about the other parent." Relevant here means relevant to a disputed custody, parenting-time, or support element — a factor in **Module 2**, an income figure, a specific incident already in play.
- **"Proportional to the needs of the case."** This is the word that defeats the fishing expedition. A demand for "all text messages, emails, and social-media activity for the last five years" is not proportional to a dispute about who picks the child up on Fridays. The burden outweighs the benefit, and a court can say so.
- **"Need not be admissible … to be discoverable."** You can *discover* something you could not yet *admit* — a lead, a source, a name. That is the bridge to Section 10.6: discoverable is the low bar; admissible is the one you actually have to clear at the hearing.

> **The in-section limiter (this is the symmetry rule at work).** Proportionality is not advice — it is enforceable *against you*. Overbroad or harassing discovery invites the other side to move for a **protective order** under MCR 2.302(C) (Section 10.7), and every request you sign carries a certification that it is "not interposed for any improper purpose, such as to harass or to cause unnecessary delay or needless increase in the cost of litigation" (MCR 2.302(G)(3)(b)(ii), quoted in 10.7). A parent who buries the other side in requests is not building a case; they are building the other side's sanctions motion.

## 10.2 ⚠ What is different about a custody case: the domestic-relations exemption and the court-control rule

Most of what people "know" about discovery comes from ordinary civil or commercial cases, where each side must volunteer a package of **initial disclosures** at the start. **A domestic-relations case is carved out of that regime.** Discovery here is turned on and off differently, and the court holds the switch.

**First: there is no automatic duty to hand over the ordinary civil disclosures.** The rule lists what is exempt:

> Unless otherwise stipulated or ordered, the following are exempt from initial disclosure under subrule (A)(1)-(3): … (c) an action under subchapter 3.200 …
> — MCR 2.302(A)(4)(c)

Subchapter 3.200 is the domestic-relations subchapter — divorce, custody, parenting time, support, paternity. So you **cannot** assume the other side must volunteer a factual-basis statement, a witness list, or a document description the way a defendant in a car-crash case must. If you want it, you generally have to ask for it. (This corrects a common and expensive misreading — see the trap box below.)

**Second: discovery is available "after commencement," but the court controls it.** Because a custody case does not require initial disclosures, the timing rule routes you to its second sentence:

> In a case where initial disclosures are required, a party may seek discovery only after the party serves its initial disclosures under MCR 2.302(A). Otherwise, a party may seek discovery after commencement of the action when authorized by these rules, by stipulation, or by court order.
> — MCR 2.301(A)(1)

And over all of it sits a one-sentence grant of control to the judge:

> The court may control the scope, order, and amount of discovery, consistent with these rules.
> — MCR 2.301(C)

**Third — and this is the piece that surprises postjudgment litigants: after a judgment, the discovery track is closed until you re-open a case.** You do not get to serve interrogatories about your ex years after the divorce just because you are curious. The rule unlocks discovery only when a postjudgment *motion* is on file:

> After a post judgment motion is filed in a domestic relations action as defined by subchapter 3.200 of these rules, parties may obtain discovery by any means provided in subchapter 2.300 of these rules.
> — MCR 2.301(A)(4)

Putting those together, here is the accurate picture for a Genesee custody case — neither "you can discover anything" nor "you need permission for everything":

| Situation | Is discovery open? | Do you need leave (court permission)? |
|---|---|---|
| **Pending (pre-judgment) case**, party-to-party written discovery (interrogatories, RFP, RFA — 10.3) | Yes, after the case is commenced | **No leave** to serve them — the rules authorize service "without leave of court" (MCR 2.309(A)(1), 2.310(C)(1)) |
| **Any stage**, subpoena to a **third party** for discovery, and you are self-represented | Only through the court | **Yes — you must file a motion** (MCR 2.305(A)(1); Section 10.4) |
| **Postjudgment**, before any motion is filed | **No** — the track is closed | You must first file a proper postjudgment motion; that filing is what opens discovery (MCR 2.301(A)(4)) |
| **Depositions** in some circumstances (e.g., before the other side has had time to get a lawyer; a person confined or hospitalized) | Conditioned | **Yes — leave required** (MCR 2.306(A)(1)–(2)) |
| **Any stage**, and the request is oppressive or overbroad | The court can shut it down | The other side moves for a protective order (MCR 2.302(C)) |

> ### ⚠ TRAP — "She'll have to turn it all over." No, she won't.
> A false expectation here wrecks a discovery plan before it starts. Because a domestic-relations case is exempt from initial disclosures (MCR 2.302(A)(4)(c)), **nothing is produced automatically.** If you want the other parent's pay records, their calendar, their messages with a third party, you must serve a proper request for them — and if you want a *non-party's* records, you (as a self-represented party) must **ask the court** to issue the subpoena (10.4). Plan for what you will *request*, not for what you assume will arrive. `ℹ PROVISIONAL — LOCAL PRACTICE:` many domestic-relations judges enter a scheduling order under MCR 2.401 that sets a discovery cutoff, and some expect discovery in a custody matter to be modest and focused; confirm your judge's scheduling order and any standing expectations with the assigned chambers or the clerk before you plan a large discovery effort.

## 10.3 Party discovery: the three written tools (and a word on depositions)

Between the parties, three written tools do almost all the useful work in a custody or support case. Each is available in a pending case **without leave of court**, each has a response clock, and each has a limiter that points back at you.

### Interrogatories — written questions answered under oath (MCR 2.309)

Interrogatories are written questions the other party must answer in writing, under oath. They are best for locked-down facts: dates, addresses, employers, account identities, the names of people with knowledge, the basis for a specific claim.

There is a hard cap, and it was verified for this draft against the archived rule text (resolving an earlier open question in the outline about the numeric limit):

> Each separately represented party may serve no more than twenty interrogatories upon each party. A discrete subpart of an interrogatory counts as a separate interrogatory.
> — MCR 2.309(A)(2)

Twenty is not many. A "discrete subpart counts as a separate interrogatory," so you cannot smuggle in fifty questions under ten numbers. Spend them on what only the other party can answer. The answer clock:

> The party on whom the interrogatories are served must serve the answers and objections, if any, on all other parties within 28 days after the interrogatories are served, except that a defendant may serve answers within 42 days after being served with the summons and complaint.
> — MCR 2.309(B)(4) `⚠ TIME-CRITICAL — this period also runs against you when you are served; calendar it and add it to Appendix A.`

> **In-section limiter.** Interrogatories are symmetrical: the twenty questions you can ask, the other parent can ask you, and **you answer under oath.** Draft nothing you would not want to answer yourself, and remember that a sworn interrogatory answer can be used against its author later. Use the narrow interrogatory drafting aid (Templates) rather than a scattershot list.

### Requests for production — documents and ESI (MCR 2.310)

A **request for production** (RFP) asks the other party to produce and let you inspect and copy documents and electronically stored information ("ESI") — pay records, calendars, messages, photographs, app exports. In a pending case it may be served without leave, and the response clock mirrors interrogatories:

> The party on whom the request is served must serve a written response within 28 days after service of the request, except that a defendant may serve a response within 42 days after being served with the summons and complaint.
> — MCR 2.310(C)(2) `⚠ TIME-CRITICAL`

> **In-section limiter — and a bright line.** An RFP is a request that the other party *produce* material; it is **not** authority to log into anyone's account, inspect anyone's device, or use remembered or shared credentials. Reaching for the other parent's email, phone, or social-media account yourself is not discovery — it is potentially unlawful access that can destroy your credibility and taint the evidence (**Module 9**, the Do-Not-Do list). Ask for the *export or the copy*; never self-help your way into an account. And keep it proportional: "every document you have touched in five years" is the kind of request that draws a protective order (10.7). The toolkit's fill-in aid at `templates/requests-for-production.md` structures a narrow request by category, date range, and form of production.

### Requests for admission — pinning down what is not really disputed (MCR 2.312)

A **request for admission** (RFA) asks the other party to admit the truth of a specific fact or the genuineness of a specific document, so you do not have to prove the obvious at the hearing ("Admit that Exhibit 3 is a true copy of the parties' text thread of March 4"). RFAs are powerful precisely because silence is fatal:

> Each matter as to which a request is made is deemed admitted unless, within 28 days after service of the request, or within a shorter or longer time as the court may allow, the party to whom the request is directed serves on the party requesting the admission a written answer or objection addressed to the matter.
> — MCR 2.312(B)(1) `⚠ TIME-CRITICAL — miss the 28 days and the matter is admitted by default.`

And an admission is not a soft concession — it locks:

> A matter admitted under this rule is conclusively established unless the court on motion permits withdrawal or amendment of an admission.
> — MCR 2.312(D)(1)

> **In-section limiter — this one bites hard in both directions.** The deemed-admitted rule is the sharpest double edge in discovery. If *you* are served with requests for admission and let 28 days pass, **every one of them is admitted against you, conclusively.** Treat an incoming RFA like a deadline with your case attached. And do not deny things just to be difficult: if you deny a request and the other side later *proves* the matter, you can be ordered to pay the cost of that proof, including attorney fees (MCR 2.313(C)(2), quoted in 10.7). The fill-in aid at `templates/requests-for-admission.md` keeps each request to a single, concrete, provable fact.

### Depositions — available, but rarely the right tool for a pro se parent

A **deposition** is live, sworn, out-of-court testimony taken on the record. It is the most powerful and the most expensive tool in the box, and for a self-represented parent it is usually the wrong one: you pay the court reporter, you must know how to question and how to handle objections in real time, and there are procedural traps (a deposition "may not exceed one day of seven hours," MCR 2.306(A)(3); leave of court is required in some situations, MCR 2.306(A)(1)–(2); and discovering an opponent's *expert* can shift that expert's fees onto you, MCR 2.302(B)(4)(c)).

> **In-section limiter.** Deposition practice has its own sanctions: a party who conducts or obstructs a deposition "in bad faith or in a manner unreasonably to annoy, embarrass, or oppress the deponent" can be sanctioned, including the other side's expenses and attorney fees (MCR 2.306(D)). If a deposition genuinely matters to your case, that is a strong signal to spend limited-scope attorney money here (**Module 4.7**) rather than improvise.

## 10.4 ⚠ Subpoenas — the two-track rule a self-represented party must not get wrong

This is the single most common structural mistake in pro se discovery, and it rests on a false premise: that "you can just subpoena the records." You can compel third-party records — but **there are two different subpoenas, governed by two different rules, and a self-represented party's power is different from an attorney's.** Choosing the wrong track produces an invalid subpoena.

### Track 1 — the trial/hearing subpoena, to bring a witness or records *to the hearing* (MCR 2.506)

This is the subpoena that commands a person — including a records custodian — to appear at your hearing or trial and testify, and/or to bring documents to that proceeding. The key rule is who can issue it, and here a self-represented party is *not* shut out:

> A subpoena signed by an attorney of record in the action or by the clerk of the court in which the matter is pending has the force and effect of an order signed by the judge of that court.
> — MCR 2.506(B)(1)

You are not an "attorney of record," so **the clerk signs and issues the subpoena for you.** In practice that is the SCAO subpoena form (**MC 11**); you do not recreate it — you obtain and complete the official form and have the **clerk issue it**. `ℹ PROVISIONAL — LOCAL PRACTICE:` confirm with the Genesee County Circuit Court clerk where and how a self-represented party gets an MC 11 issued, and the current form revision, before relying on the mechanics. The service clock:

> Unless the court orders otherwise, the subpoena must be served at least 2 days before the appearance or 14 days before the appearance when documents are requested.
> — MCR 2.506(C)(1) `⚠ TIME-CRITICAL — 14 days when you are asking for documents.`

Two boundaries on this track. First, it is for the *hearing*, not for pretrial discovery: "This subrule does not apply to discovery subpoenas (MCR 2.305) or requests for documents to a party where discovery is available (MCR 2.310)" — MCR 2.506(A)(1). Second, you must pay the witness: "The fee for attendance and mileage provided by law must be tendered to the person on whom the subpoena is served at the time of service" — MCR 2.506(G)(1). `ℹ verify the current statutory attendance-fee and mileage amounts; this draft does not state a dollar figure.`

### Track 2 — the non-party discovery subpoena, to get records *during discovery* (MCR 2.305)

This is the subpoena you use to obtain documents or a deposition **from a third party before the hearing**, as part of building your case. And here is the two-track rule — the sentence a pro se litigant must not miss:

> A represented party may issue a subpoena to a non-party for a deposition, production or inspection of documents, inspection of tangible things, or entry to land upon court order or after all parties have had a reasonable opportunity to obtain an attorney, as determined under MCR 2.306(A). **An unrepresented party may move the court for issuance of non-party discovery subpoenas.**
> — MCR 2.305(A)(1)

Read those two sentences against each other. **A lawyer can self-issue a discovery subpoena to a non-party. You cannot.** As an unrepresented party, you **move the court** — you file a motion asking the judge to issue the non-party discovery subpoena. This is the trap: guides and forms written for attorneys show a self-issued subpoena, and copying that as a pro se party produces an invalid one. The toolkit's annotated drafting aid — a **motion for issuance of non-party discovery subpoenas under MCR 2.305(A)(1)** — exists precisely for this step (Templates), and the cover aid is at `templates/subpoena-cover.md`.

The non-party gets time and protection:

> A subpoena shall provide a minimum of 14 days after service of the subpoena (or a shorter time if the court directs) for the requested act.
> — MCR 2.305(A)(3)

> **In-section limiter.** A non-party discovery subpoena is not a free pass. The recipient — or any party — can move to "quash or modify the subpoena if it is unreasonable or oppressive" (MCR 2.305(A)(4)(a)), and a subpoena is subject to the same protective-order power in MCR 2.302(C). If you overreach, the non-party's obligation is *stayed* until the court rules, and you can end up paying costs. Ask for the narrow, dated category you can tie to a Module 2 factor — not "the entire file."

## 10.5 Getting third-party records — school, medical, financial — with the privilege caution

Most of the records a custody case actually needs are held by third parties: the school, a clinic or therapist, an employer, a bank, sometimes an agency. Two rules govern *how* you reach them (10.4, above) — but a separate question governs *whether* you can have them at all: **privilege and confidentiality.**

> **Read this with Module 6, which owns the privilege doctrine.** The core distinction — that **confidential does not mean privileged**, and that **a subpoena does not by itself defeat a privilege** — is developed in **Module 6** (therapist–patient, physician–patient, spousal, and the child's records). This section does not restate that doctrine; it maps the practical record types onto the discovery mechanics and flags where the privilege wall stops you.

**School records — FERPA.** A child's education records are governed by the federal Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (**FERPA**). `ℹ PROVISIONAL — FERPA is a federal statute not quoted here from an archived primary source; confirm the current access and release mechanics.` As a general matter a parent has a right of access to their child's education records, and records like attendance and report cards are the least privilege-encumbered category — often obtainable by a signed parental request, and if not, by subpoena to the records custodian (10.4). This is the record type used in the worked example below.

**Medical and mental-health records — HIPAA, privilege, and a rule built for custody.** These are the most protected records, and the court rule that governs a *party's* medical information is worth quoting because it contains two traps.

First, the outer wall — **a non-party's medical information is off-limits under this rule:**

> Medical information concerning persons not parties to the action is not discoverable under this rule.
> — MCR 2.314(E)

The **child is not a party.** So the child's medical and therapy records are *not* reachable through the party-medical-discovery rule; getting them generally requires a different route — a signed release from the appropriate holder, the involvement of the child's **LGAL/GAL or evaluator** (**Module 19**), or a court order — and the child's therapy privilege is the child's, not yours to waive. `ℹ PROVISIONAL:` HIPAA and Michigan's mental-health confidentiality statutes layer on top of the court rule (**Module 6**); treat the child's therapy file as presumptively out of your reach without counsel or a court order.

Second — and this is the symmetry rule in statutory form — **if your own medical or mental-health condition is in play and you claim privilege to keep the records out, you cannot later use those records yourself:**

> Unless the court orders otherwise, if a party asserts that the medical information is subject to a privilege and the assertion has the effect of preventing discovery of medical information that must be disclosed or is otherwise discoverable under MCR 2.302(B), the party may not thereafter present or introduce any physical, documentary, or testimonial evidence relating to the party's medical history or mental or physical condition.
> — MCR 2.314(B)(2)

That is the **factor (g)** exposure (Module 2.4) made concrete: if the other side puts your mental or physical health in controversy, you face a genuine choice — assert the privilege and lose the ability to put on your own favorable treatment records, or waive and open the file. That is a decision to make with counsel, not reflexively.

When a custodian sits on an authorized request, the rule points you back to the subpoena track:

> If a custodian does not respond within the time permitted by subrule (D)(1) to a party's authorized request for medical information, a subpoena may be issued under MCR 2.305(A)(1) …
> — MCR 2.314(D)(6)

**Police reports and CAD logs.** These are public records (reached at the hearing as public records under MRE 803(8) — **Module 6**, including the law-enforcement limitation on that exception), and are often obtainable by a public-records (FOIA) request outside the discovery process entirely. `ℹ PROVISIONAL` on any specific agency's process and timing.

**Employment, payroll, and bank records.** These are the workhorses of a *support* dispute and of **factor (c)** (capacity to provide material needs). Reach the other *party's* own copies by RFP (10.3); reach the *employer's* or *bank's* records by non-party discovery subpoena (10.4 — motion for a pro se party) or by the party's signed authorization.

**MDHHS / CPS records.** Child-protective records have their own statutory process and generally contemplate an **attorney-signed subpoena with proof of representation**, not a pro se self-help request ([MDHHS subpoena page](https://www.michigan.gov/mdhhs/inside-mdhhs/legal/subpoena)). `ℹ PROVISIONAL` and, more importantly, a stop-gate: if there is any live CPS or criminal matter, this is a **`DO NOT USE THIS GUIDE ALONE`** situation — see **Module 17** and the gates below.

**Phone records and platform / social-media records.** Carrier *call-detail* records can be subpoenaed from the carrier (10.4). But the *content* of messages held by a platform (the text of private messages, post history) is frequently **not** produced by the platform in a civil case — federal law (the Stored Communications Act) commonly blocks that, and platforms routinely object. `ℹ PROVISIONAL — the Stored Communications Act is not quoted here from an archived primary source; confirm before relying on it.` The practical, lawful route is almost always the *party* who holds the account: an RFP for their own export (10.3), authenticated later under **Module 7** — not a subpoena to the platform, and never self-help into the account (**Module 9**).

**The quiet alternative to all of it: a signed release.** The rule expressly lets a party respond to a medical-records request by furnishing "signed authorizations in the form approved by the state court administrator" (MCR 2.314(C)(1)(d)). More broadly, where the other parent or a record-holder will *consent*, a signed release gets records faster and cheaper than any subpoena, with no motion and no fight. It will not work for hostile or privileged material — but for a cooperative school or a jointly held account, ask before you compel.

> **In-section limiter (records).** Every route in this section runs both ways. The subpoena that reaches the other parent's therapist can reach *yours*; the FOIA request that surfaces their police contact can surface *yours*; asserting privilege to protect yourself (2.314(B)(2)) forfeits your own use of that evidence. And reaching for a *child's* records to build an adult dispute is both usually barred (2.314(E)) and a red flag to a court. Take the narrowest record that proves the specific factor, by the least intrusive lawful route, and stop.

## 10.6 Connect every request backward to admissibility (Modules 6 and 7)

Here is the mistake that wastes the most effort: a parent spends weeks getting records produced, then discovers at the hearing that produced is not the same as admitted. The court rule says so in as many words:

> The admissibility of the contents of medical records produced under this rule or under MCR 2.314 is not affected or altered by these procedures and remains subject to the same objections as if the original records were personally produced by the custodian at the trial or hearing.
> — MCR 2.506(I)(4)

In other words: a subpoena gets the paper into the room; it does **nothing** for the hearsay and authentication objections waiting for it. So plan every records subpoena **backward from the foundation you will need**:

- **Which admissibility door?** School and clinic files usually come in, if at all, as **records of a regularly conducted activity** (MRE 803(6)) or **public records** (MRE 803(8)) — the doors are in **Module 6**. And remember the hearsay-within-hearsay problem: a business record admitted under 803(6) does not admit every statement quoted inside it.
- **Who authenticates it?** **Module 7** owns this. Michigan has **no self-authentication certificate for electronic records**, so for anything you captured yourself you plan on a live witness. For a *business or public office's* records there **is** a certificate route — a **records-custodian certification** under MRE 902(11) (Module 7, §7.3) — and the court rules provide the matching sworn-certificate mechanics (a custodian's "sworn certificate … verifying that it is a complete and accurate reproduction of the original record," MCR 2.506(I)(1)(b); the medical-records version at MCR 2.314(D)(2)(b)).
- **So subpoena the *custodian*, not just the records.** If you cannot get a certification, you subpoena the **custodian of records** to the hearing (Track 1, MC 11) to lay the 803(6)/803(8) foundation live. Build that into the plan from the start, or you will have records no one can sponsor.

The **records-custodian certification request** (Templates) is the small document that can save you from dragging a custodian into court — request it at the same time you request the records.

## 10.7 When the other side won't comply — meet-and-confer, motion to compel, sanctions (MCR 2.313)

Discovery in a high-conflict case will be resisted. The path when it is has three steps, and skipping the first one is how a parent turns a winnable motion into a fee award *against themselves*.

**Step 1 — Meet and confer, in good faith, first.** This is not optional politeness; it is a stated element of the motion. Every compel provision carries the same requirement:

> The motion must state that the movant has in good faith conferred or attempted to confer with the party not making the disclosure in an effort to secure the disclosure without court action.
> — MCR 2.310(C)(3) (identical language at MCR 2.309(C) for interrogatories and MCR 2.312(C) for admissions)

In practice, this is a dated **deficiency letter**: it quotes the request and the deficient response, identifies the concrete problem, asks for a cure by a reasonable date, and keeps a neutral tone. The toolkit aid is at `templates/deficiency-letter.md`. Keep the letter — you attach or describe it in the motion to show the conferral happened.

**Step 2 — Move to compel (MCR 2.313(A)).** If conferral fails, you may "apply for an order compelling disclosure or discovery." For a *party's* failure, the mechanics live in the tool that governs the request (interrogatories, RFP, RFA each point to 2.313(A)). For a **non-party's** failure to obey a discovery subpoena, there is a dedicated provision: "If a recipient of a non-party discovery subpoena under MCR 2.305 fails to comply, the issuing party may move to compel compliance… The motion must include a copy of the subpoena and proof of service of the subpoena" (MCR 2.313(A)(2)(c)). The fill-in aid is at `templates/motion-to-compel.md`.

**Step 3 — Expenses, and the symmetry.** If you win the motion, the court *may* shift the cost of making it:

> If the motion is granted—or if the disclosure or requested discovery is provided after the motion was filed—the court may, after opportunity for hearing, require the party or deponent whose conduct necessitated the motion or the party or attorney advising such conduct, or both, to pay to the moving party the reasonable expenses incurred in making the motion, including attorney fees, unless the court finds that the moving party filed the motion before attempting in good faith to obtain the disclosure or discovery without court action, the opposition to the motion was substantially justified, or other circumstances make an award unjust.
> — MCR 2.313(A)(5)(a)

> ### ⚠ The limiter that makes this a two-edged motion
> Read the *next* subrule, because it is the reason not to file a weak or premature motion to compel:
>
> > If the motion is denied, the court may, after opportunity for hearing, require the moving party or the attorney advising the motion, or both, to pay to the person who opposed the motion the reasonable expenses incurred in opposing the motion, including attorney fees, unless the court finds that the making of the motion was substantially justified or that other circumstances make an award of expenses unjust.
> > — MCR 2.313(A)(5)(b)
>
> **Lose the motion, and you can pay *their* fees.** And note the first exception in 2.313(A)(5)(a): a court can deny you fees you would otherwise win if you "filed the motion before attempting in good faith to obtain the disclosure or discovery without court action" — i.e., if you skipped Step 1. This is the symmetry rule (GUARDRAILS §7) written into the compel process itself: the motion to compel is not a free swing.

**If a party defies an actual court order**, the sanctions escalate sharply — the court "may order such sanctions as are just," including that designated facts be "taken to be established," "refusing to allow the disobedient party to support or oppose designated claims or defenses," "striking pleadings," "dismissing the action," "rendering a judgment by default," or treating the defiance as "contempt of court" (MCR 2.313(B)(2)(a)–(d)). Those are the teeth. They are also aimed at *whoever* disobeys — including you.

**Two more symmetry hooks that discipline your own conduct:**

- **The certification you sign.** Every request, response, and objection you sign certifies, after "reasonable inquiry," that it is "not interposed for any improper purpose, such as to harass or to cause unnecessary delay or needless increase in the cost of litigation" and "not unreasonable or unduly burdensome or expensive" (MCR 2.302(G)(3)(b)(ii)–(iii)). A certification "made in violation of this rule" exposes you to "an appropriate sanction, which may include … reasonable expenses … including reasonable attorney fees" (MCR 2.302(G)(4)).
- **Refusing to admit the obvious.** If you deny a request for admission and the other side later proves the matter, you can be ordered to "pay the expenses incurred in making that proof, including attorney fees" (MCR 2.313(C)(2)).
- **The other side's shield against *you*.** The target of overbroad discovery can move for a **protective order** — the court "may issue any order that justice requires to protect a party or person from annoyance, embarrassment, oppression, or undue burden or expense," up to and including "that the discovery not be had" (MCR 2.302(C)).

Finally, the fee-shifting hook that runs across many family-law motions — **MCR 3.206(D)(2)(b)**, which can shift fees *either direction* for conduct that causes the other side expense — is developed in **Module 24.6** and is not restated here.

> **The whole-module limiter, stated once plainly.** Discovery abuse cuts back at the person who commits it. Overbroad requests draw protective orders and 2.302(G) sanctions; premature or losing compel motions draw fee awards under 2.313(A)(5)(b); reflexive denials draw proof-cost awards under 2.313(C)(2). The disciplined approach is also the safest one: narrow requests tied to a Module 2 factor, a good-faith letter before any motion, and a plan for how each record gets admitted.

## Templates and tools

None of these is an official form, and none is filing-ready. Each is a drafting aid to be completed only after checking the current rule, the assigned judge's policies, and any scheduling order — and each carries its own "do not use if" condition.

1. **Discovery plan (the master worksheet for this module).** One row per disputed fact: *fact to prove → Module 2 factor → source (party or which third party) → mechanism (interrogatory / RFP / RFA / hearing subpoena / discovery subpoena / signed release) → admissibility route (803(6)/803(8) + authentication witness, per Modules 6–7).* **Done when** no row is blank — especially the last column. *Do not use if* a row's only answer to "which factor" is "it makes them look bad": that is a curiosity request, and it comes out of the plan.

2. **Narrow interrogatory / RFP / RFA drafting aids.** Point to the toolkit fill-ins: `templates/requests-for-production.md`, `templates/requests-for-admission.md`. Keep each interrogatory within the twenty-question cap; keep each RFP a dated, bounded category; keep each RFA a single provable fact. *Do not use to* demand account access, credentials, or device inspection.

3. **Non-party records flowchart.** *Do I want this record in discovery, or at the hearing?* → **Discovery** ⇒ non-party discovery subpoena ⇒ **you must move the court** (MCR 2.305(A)(1)). **At the hearing** ⇒ trial subpoena ⇒ **clerk-issued MC 11**, served 14 days ahead for documents (MCR 2.506(C)(1)). *Is it privileged or a child's record?* ⇒ stop; release, LGAL/evaluator, or court order (Module 6, Module 19).

4. **Motion for issuance of non-party discovery subpoenas (MCR 2.305(A)(1))** — annotated drafting aid, plus the cover aid at `templates/subpoena-cover.md`. *Do not use if* an attorney is of record for you — the mechanics change.

5. **MC 11 hearing-subpoena checklist.** Obtain the official form; have the **clerk** issue it; serve at least 2 days before testimony, 14 days before when documents are requested; **tender the statutory attendance and mileage fee at service** (MCR 2.506(G)(1)). `ℹ verify the current fee/mileage amount and the Genesee issuance procedure.` *Do not* recreate the SCAO form.

6. **Records-custodian certification request** (paired with any records subpoena) — asks for the MRE 902(11) / MCR 2.506(I)(1)(b) sworn certificate so you may not need the custodian live (Module 7, §7.3).

7. **Preservation ("litigation hold") letter** — `templates/preservation-letter.md`; and cross-reference **Module 8**'s litigation-hold discipline on *your own* data.

8. **Discovery log** — every request served/received, date served, response due (calendared), response received, deficiency, next step.

9. **Meet-and-confer / deficiency letter** — `templates/deficiency-letter.md`; the Step-1 document that both fixes problems and protects your fee position.

10. **Privilege log** — `templates/privilege-log.md`; the structure for withholding a document on privilege while telling the other side enough to test the claim (the doctrine is in Module 6).

## Exercises

1. **Draft one proportional request and one valid objection *(fill-in).*** Write one request (interrogatory, RFP, or RFA) tied to a specific Module 2 factor, and one objection to a request that is overbroad or seeks privileged material. **Done when** the request names its factor and its admissibility route, and the objection names the specific ground (proportionality under MCR 2.302(B)(1), privilege, or improper purpose) rather than just "I object."

2. **Build a subpoena plan for school attendance records — without assuming admissibility *(scenario exercise).*** Plan how you would obtain the child's attendance records and get them *in front of the court*. **Done when** the plan names (a) the **custodian** (the school's records custodian), (b) the **foundation rule** you will use (MRE 803(6) or 803(8), per Module 6), and (c) the **sponsoring witness or certification** (a records-custodian certification under MRE 902(11), or the custodian live under a hearing subpoena — Module 7).

3. **Worked walkthrough — an MC 11 for school attendance records *(worked example).*** You want the child's attendance record showing a pattern of tardies on the other parent's days.
   - **Track decision.** Do you want it *during discovery* or *at the hearing*? If you want it produced pretrial, that is a **non-party discovery subpoena**, and as a self-represented party you **move the court** to issue it (MCR 2.305(A)(1)) — you do **not** self-issue. If you want the custodian to bring the certified record to the hearing, that is a **trial subpoena on MC 11**, which the **clerk issues** for you (MCR 2.506(B)(1)), served **14 days** ahead because documents are requested (MCR 2.506(C)(1)), with the **statutory fee tendered at service** (MCR 2.506(G)(1)).
   - **Scope.** Command *the attendance record for [child], [school year]* — a narrow, dated category tied to **factor (h)** (the child's school record) and, if you are arguing a compliance problem, **factor (g)** of the parenting-time factors (Module 2.5). Not "the entire student file."
   - **Admissibility.** Request a **records-custodian certification** so the record can come in under MRE 803(8)/803(6) without live testimony (Module 7); if the school will not certify, the same MC 11 brings the **custodian** to lay the foundation. This is *not* a filled-in form and is not filing-ready — it is the reasoning you complete on the official MC 11.

## Stop and seek counsel 🛑

- **Any live CPS or police matter, or MDHHS/CPS records.** Subpoenaing that material, or discovering into it, is outside self-help. **`DO NOT USE THIS GUIDE ALONE`** — **Module 17**.
- **A child's medical or therapy records.** Presumptively barred to you under MCR 2.314(E) and layered privilege; route through the LGAL/GAL or evaluator (**Module 19**) or counsel, not a self-issued request.
- **Any privilege-waiver question** — including whether to assert or waive privilege over *your own* medical or mental-health records under MCR 2.314(B)(2) (factor (g), Module 2.4). This is a stop-and-seek gate (outline §0.4).
- **Depositions and expert discovery** beyond a signed release or a simple records subpoena — the cost, the fee-shifting, and the real-time skill make this the place to spend limited-scope attorney money (**Module 4.7**).
- **Anything touching a PPO or no-contact order.** `🛑 SAFETY OVERRIDE` — coordinate with FM.5 and Module 17 before serving anything on the other parent or their household.
