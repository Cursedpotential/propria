# Why this module exists, and what it can and cannot do

In a high-conflict case, a parent being cut out of a child's life spends far more hours on denied exchanges than on any trial. This is the module you are most likely to actually use. It is also the one where the law most often disappoints, so it is worth being precise up front about what enforcement *is*.

**Enforcement means: make the order that already exists happen.** You are not asking the court to change anything. You accept the order as written; you want compliance with it. That is a different job from *modification* (asking for a different order), which has its own gate — proper cause or a change of circumstances (Module 2.3) — and its own home in Module 27. Trying to enforce your way to a *better* schedule, or filing to modify when what you needed was enforcement, is one of the most common and most expensive mistakes in this posture.

Three tools do the enforcement work, all from the Support and Parenting Time Enforcement Act:

- the **Friend of the Court (FOC) complaint** — MCL 552.641;
- **make-up parenting time** — MCL 552.642; and
- **civil contempt** — MCL 552.644.

And one hard limit governs all three, so it comes first.

---

## 26.1 First question: is your order actually enforceable?

Before you reach for any of the three tools, look at what your order actually says — because the FOC is allowed to decline your complaint if the order has nothing specific in it to enforce.

The statute lets the FOC turn a complaint away when the order —

> "does not include an enforceable provision that is relevant to the … violation alleged."
> — MCL 552.641(2)(c) (ellipsis in the recorded text)

Read that against a typical vague order. If your judgment says parenting time is **"reasonable and liberal"** or **"as the parties agree,"** there is no specific term to breach — no day, no time, no place, no protocol — and the FOC has a lawful basis to say there is nothing here to enforce. A generous-sounding phrase is not an enforceable one. (Michigan courts have held that "reasonable and liberal parenting time" is *plainly not* a grant of parenting time in specific terms — Module 2.5, Module 12.)

**This is why the campaign runs in order.** The specific-parenting-time motion — converting "as the parties agree" into named days, times, exchange location, and a written communication and make-up protocol — is **step one**, and it lives in **Module 12** and the P1 packet. Enforcement is **step two**. You cannot skip step one. An order that a stranger cannot put on a calendar is an order the FOC can decline to enforce.

> ### ⚠ The precondition, stated plainly
> Enforcement bites only on a **specific, enforceable term**. If your order is vague, the honest first move is **not** a contempt filing that may fail on 552.641(2)(c) — it is a motion to make the order specific (Module 12), or in a narrower case a motion to *clarify* an ambiguous term. Filing contempt on a vague order can lose, and losing teaches the other side that the order is toothless. Get the specific term first; enforce it second. `⚠ TIME-CRITICAL` *(the sequencing, not a clock — but getting it wrong costs you months.)*

If your order **is** specific — it names the schedule, the exchange point, the notice rules — then you have something to enforce, and the rest of this module is for you.

## 26.2 Two doors (and a few smaller ones)

There are two main ways to enforce, and they trade off differently:

| | **The FOC complaint** (26.3) | **Your own motion to show cause** (26.5) |
|---|---|---|
| Who drives it | The FOC, once you file a written complaint | You, as the moving party |
| Cost | Generally no motion fee to file the complaint `ℹ PROVISIONAL` | Motion fee; your own time drafting and appearing |
| Speed / control | The FOC controls timing and which response it chooses | You control timing and what relief you request |
| Best when | You want the make-up-time machinery, or you want a buffer between you and the other parent | The FOC has stalled, the violation is serious, or you want a contempt finding on the record |

You do not have to choose only one, and enforcement is not the only response to a denial. The smaller doors — **documentation only** (log it, build the pattern, do not file yet), **direct resolution** (a dated written request for make-up time before any filing), a **motion to clarify** an ambiguous term, and a **motion to enforce** a clear one — are often the right first step, especially early in a pattern when the record is still thin. The decision tree in the Tools section walks the choice.

## 26.3 The FOC route and the 56-day window — MCL 552.641

You start the FOC route by filing a **written parenting-time complaint**. (The FOC has a form for this; `⚠ VERIFY` the current SCAO/Genesee form number and where Genesee wants it filed — see 26.9 and the checklist.) A phone call or a verbal grievance is not a complaint; the statute and the make-up machinery run on a *written* complaint.

**What the FOC must do.** Once a written complaint alleging a parenting-time violation comes in, the FOC is not free to ignore it. Under MCL 552.641(1), the FOC **shall** take one or more of these steps: apply the make-up-parenting-time policy (26.4); begin civil-contempt proceedings; file a motion to modify parenting time to ensure it happens, unless that would be contrary to the child's best interests; schedule mediation; or schedule a joint meeting. The verb is mandatory — *shall* do one or more — which is the leverage a written complaint gives you.

**What the FOC may decline.** The same section, MCL 552.641(2), lets the FOC **decline** to act in three situations:

- **(2)(a)** — the complaining parent has already submitted **two or more** complaints found to be unwarranted, costs were assessed for them, and **those costs are unpaid**;
- **(2)(b)** — the violation happened **more than 56 days** before the complaint was submitted; or
- **(2)(c)** — the order has no enforceable, relevant provision to enforce (26.1).

> ### ⚠ The 56-day window — treat it as fatal, but know what it really is
> The statute says the FOC **"may decline"** a complaint about a violation more than 56 days old. That is **discretionary, not jurisdictional** — it does not, by its terms, extinguish your right. But you should plan as if it is fatal, because the FOC is permitted to treat it that way and often will. The safe practice: **file the complaint promptly after each denial**, within 56 days, one denial at a time, rather than saving up a season of them.
>
> This is also where the **"past months" myth** gets corrected. A stack of denials from a year ago is not a bank of hours the court hands back. It is **evidence of the pattern** — proof for the best-interest facilitation factor (Module 2.4(j)) and the compliance factors (MCL 722.27a(7)(f)–(g)) that support asking for a better-secured order (Module 27). The recoverable remedy — make-up time — reaches the denials you documented and complained about in time. **The record of your timely requests is the case; the un-asked-for months are the diagnosis, not the cure.** `⚠ TIME-CRITICAL` — the 56-day period belongs in Appendix A with its trigger (each denial).

> ### 🛑 The same door can close on you — MCL 552.641(2)(a)
> Every tool in this module points both ways. If **you** file repeated complaints that the FOC finds unwarranted, and costs get assessed and go unpaid, the FOC can stop responding to you under (2)(a). That is not a footnote; it is the statute deliberately protecting the process from over-filing. File when you have a documented, specific-term denial — not to apply pressure. A complaint you cannot back with a dated breach of a named term is the kind that gets found unwarranted. `🛑`

**Genesee's local intake** for a parenting-time complaint — which form, whether it goes through the FOC office or an online portal, and any local screening — is `ℹ PROVISIONAL`. Confirm it with the Genesee County FOC before you rely on it (contact card in FM.4; free-help resources in Module 4.7). Do not assume a general Michigan description matches Genesee's counter.

## 26.4 Make-up parenting time — the mechanics that give you leverage — MCL 552.642

Make-up time is the FOC route's most efficient remedy, and it has a built-in feature that does a lot of work for a denied parent: **silence counts as agreement.** MCL 552.642 requires every circuit to maintain a make-up-time policy, and the recorded statutory mechanics are these:

- **Same type and duration.** Make-up time matches what was denied — weekend for a weekend, holiday for a holiday, weekday for a weekday, summer for summer. You do not trade a lost holiday for a random Tuesday.
- **Within one year.** The make-up time must be taken within **one year** of the denied time.
- **The wrongfully denied parent chooses when.** You pick the make-up dates, subject to the notice rules below.
- **Notice.** You give **at least one week's** notice for make-up weekend or weekday time, and **at least 28 days'** notice for make-up holiday or summer time.

Those four periods belong in Appendix A. But the provision that changes behavior is the response clock.

> ### 🔴 THE 21-DAY DEEMED-AGREEMENT CLOCK — silence = agreement
> When the FOC sends the other parent notice of a make-up-time claim, that notice must carry, in boldface, the statutory warning — quoted verbatim:
>
> > "FAILURE TO RESPOND IN WRITING TO THE OFFICE OF THE FRIEND OF THE COURT WITHIN 21 DAYS AFTER THIS NOTICE WAS SENT SHALL BE CONSIDERED AS AN AGREEMENT THAT PARENTING TIME WAS WRONGFULLY DENIED AND THAT THE MAKEUP PARENTING TIME POLICY … WILL BE APPLIED."
> > — MCL 552.642 (ellipsis in the recorded text)
>
> **Read the trigger carefully: the 21 days run from the date the notice was *sent*, not the date it was received.** A parent who ignores FOC mail for three weeks is deemed to have agreed that the time was wrongfully denied and that make-up time applies. That is the leverage — a non-responsive other parent loses by default.
>
> **And it runs the other way with equal force.** If a make-up-time notice is sent to **you** — because the other parent filed a complaint — the same clock is running against you from the date it was *sent*. Ignoring FOC mail is how a parent agrees to something without meaning to. **Open everything from the FOC the day it arrives, and calendar 21 days from the date on the notice, not the day you read it.** This clock belongs in Appendix A and the FM.0 triage router with its trigger named: *notice sent.* `🔴 ACT TODAY if a make-up notice has been sent`

This section is **step two of the campaign** described in the CHEAT-SHEET: after step one builds a specific, enforceable schedule (Module 12), each documented denial becomes a make-up-time claim, and the other parent's own silence builds your record. It only works, though, on denials of **specific ordered time** that you **documented and complained about in time** — which is the whole reason the interim documentation protocol (P1) and the 56-day discipline (26.3) matter.

## 26.5 Contempt — the teeth — MCL 552.644

Contempt is the heavier tool. It asks the court to find that the other parent violated a valid order and to impose a consequence. In the parenting-time setting it is **civil** contempt — coercive and remedial, aimed at getting compliance, with the possibility of a purge condition (the contemnor can end the sanction by complying) — as distinct from **criminal** contempt, which punishes past defiance and carries greater procedural protection. (Your reference materials flag *Dailey v Kloenhamer* on the civil-contempt procedure; its holding is **not yet verified** in this project, so do not rely on any summary of it until the opinion is read. `⚠`)

**What you have to show.** The ordinary elements are a **valid order**, **notice** of it to the other parent, the other parent's **ability to comply**, and **willful noncompliance**. Ability to comply matters in both directions (see 26.8).

**The finding is mandatory once willful violation without good cause is shown** — quoted verbatim:

> "If the court finds that either parent has violated a parenting time order without good cause, the court shall find that parent in contempt"
> — MCL 552.644(2)

**The menu of sanctions.** After that finding, the court **may** order one or more of the options in MCL 552.644(2)(a)–(i): additional terms and conditions consistent with the parenting-time order; after notice and a hearing if requested, a modification of the order to meet the child's best interests; **make-up parenting time** for the wrongfully denied parent; a **fine of not more than $100**; commitment to **jail** or an alternative, with or without work-release; **conditional suspension of an occupational, driver's, or recreational/sporting license** tied to noncompliance with a make-up-and-ongoing-parenting-time order; a community-corrections program; and **FOC supervision** with conditions (a parenting program, counseling, an arrearage plan, facilitating make-up time, and the like).

**Jail is capped, and release is built in.** Commitment is **not more than 45 days for a first finding** and **not more than 90 days for each subsequent finding**, and the parent must be released — quoted verbatim —

> "if the court has reasonable cause to believe that the parent will comply."
> — MCL 552.644 (recorded release clause)

That release clause is what makes it *civil* contempt: the contemnor holds the key.

Three provisions a pro se reader would almost never find on their own — and one of them is a gift:

> ### ⚠ The court must explain a do-nothing ruling — MCL 552.644(3)
> If the court finds contempt and then imposes **no** sanction, it does not get to do that silently. Quoted verbatim:
>
> > "The court shall state on the record the reason the court is not ordering a sanction listed in subsection (2)."
> > — MCL 552.644(3)
>
> That is a preserved issue handed to you by statute. A finding of contempt followed by nothing, with no stated reason, is an error you can point to on review. `⚠`

**Bad faith runs both ways, and so does the cost-shifting.** For **bad-faith** conduct in a parenting-time dispute, the recorded escalation is **not more than $250** for a first finding, **$500** for a second, and **$1,000** for a third or subsequent finding (MCL 552.644(6)); and separately — quoted verbatim —

> "If the court finds that a party to a parenting time dispute has acted in bad faith, the court shall order the party to pay the other party's costs."
> — MCL 552.644(8)

Note the mandatory *shall*, and note that "a party" is either party. A reader who files in bad faith is exposed to the same cost order.

**One more clock inside the contempt notice.** The contempt notice must tell the parent of a right to a hearing on a proposed **modification of parenting time if requested within 21 days after the date of the notice** (MCL 552.644(1)(b)) — another 21-day period triggered by the *notice date*, and one for Appendix A.

> ### ⚠ VERIFY — the license-suspension window and any separate escalation
> The outline cites **MCL 552.645** for a 21-day window to request a modification hearing after a license suspension, and lumps the $250/$500/$1,000 escalation under that section too. This project's verbatim read placed the escalation and the cost order in **MCL 552.644(6) and (8)** (above). The two documents do not fully agree on which section carries what. Until **MCL 552.645** is read from primary text, treat the separate license-suspension 21-day modification-hearing window as `⚠ VERIFY` — real enough to plan around, not yet quotable in a filing. ## 26.6 Proving a denial

Whichever door you use, the proof is the same, and it is ordinary evidence assembled in advance — not a story told at the podium. The enforcement evidence packet:

- **the order** — the operative page, with the specific term you say was breached highlighted (26.1);
- **the schedule** — what was supposed to happen, by date and time;
- **the exchange log** — a dated, same-day record of each denial: date, scheduled time, place, what happened, who was present (the interim documentation protocol in P1 is exactly this);
- **the messages** — texts, emails, and app messages in which the other parent cancelled, refused, or moved the exchange. Anything the other parent wrote is **not hearsay** when you offer it against them — MRE 801(d)(2), the party-opponent rule, which Module 6 owns. A "mail only" reply, a "he doesn't want to come" text, a unilateral cancellation — those are your best exhibits;
- **a third-party witness** — an adult who was present at the exchange point (a neutral relative, a friend, an exchange-center staffer), not the child;
- **the "keep the peace" report** — if you called non-emergency police to a failed exchange and they made a report, that report documents the date and the fact of the standoff (26.7 on what police will and will not do).

Every one of these still has to get **into** evidence. A screenshot is not admissible just because it is a screenshot; a text thread needs a live foundation under MRE 901 (there is no certificate shortcut in Michigan). The foundation scripts are in **Module 7**, and the exhibit-by-exhibit admissibility work is in **Modules 5–7**. Build the packet so each item has an authentication route before you file.

> ### 🛑 The child proves nothing here
> No exhibit in an enforcement packet comes from the child, and no step asks the child to carry, report, record, or confirm anything. The child is never your witness, your courier, or your evidence-gatherer — this is absolute (GUARDRAILS §8, Module 8). You prove a denial with your own log, the other parent's own words, and an adult witness. If the only "proof" of a denial is something the child would have to say, you do not yet have admissible proof, and the answer is not to involve the child. `🛑`

## 26.7 ⚠ Enforcement myths that lose cases

SCAO runs a training for judges and referees literally titled *Custody and Parenting Time Enforcement Myths*. The myths it corrects are the ones a self-help reader is most likely to believe:

- **"Support and parenting time are linked."** They are not. You may **not** withhold the child because support is unpaid, and you may **not** withhold support because parenting time is denied. They are independent obligations with independent enforcement. Doing either turns you from the complaining party into a violator (26.8).
- **"The police will make the exchange happen."** Usually not. Police at the curb will generally keep the peace and document the standoff; most will not haul a child out of one home and into your car on the strength of a family-court order. Their report is useful evidence; it is not the remedy.
- **"If the child refuses to go, I'm in the clear" / "the other parent is automatically in contempt."** A child "refusing" is **not** an automatic defense for the parent who is supposed to produce the child — but it is also **not** a simple contempt case for you to win, and it is a serious factual problem that calls for a different response than a contempt motion. Where a child's refusal is real and persistent, the question shifts toward the *why*, which is best-interest and possibly therapeutic terrain, not a curbside enforcement problem. Do not build your whole case on "the child refused."

`⚠` Treating enforcement as an afterthought is exactly how a guide propagates these myths. They are here on purpose.

## 26.8 If an enforcement allegation is aimed at you

Because every tool in this module is symmetrical, you may be the one served with a complaint, a make-up-time notice, or a show-cause motion. The statute gives the responding parent real defenses — and the most important one is written into the contempt statute itself.

**"Good cause" is defined to include safety** — quoted verbatim:

> "'good cause' includes, but is not limited to, consideration of the safety of a child or party who is governed by the parenting time order."
> — MCL 552.644(3) (recorded definition)

So a parent who did not produce the child has a statutory defense if the reason was the safety of the child or a party. This is the same protective-action principle that runs through best-interest factor (j) (Module 2.4) and the DV-shelter carve-out in the parenting-time factors (Module 2.5): reasonable protective action is not contempt. It also means a reader who files enforcement should **anticipate** a safety defense from the other side and be ready to meet it with the record.

The other defensive fronts, in plain terms:

- **Ability to comply.** Willfulness is an element. A denial caused by something genuinely outside your control (illness, a true emergency, the child's own medical situation) is different from a chosen refusal — and it is your burden to document it, contemporaneously, the same way a denied parent documents a denial.
- **Documentation beats adjectives.** The parent with the dated, boring, contemporaneous record wins the credibility contest. If you had good cause, the time to build the proof of it is *when it happens*, not at the hearing.
- **What not to say.** Do not argue that you withheld time because support was unpaid, or to punish the other parent, or because you disagree with the order — those are admissions of a willful violation, not defenses (26.7). And nothing you say should route through the child.

> ### ⚠ Respond to the paper, on time
> The single most common self-inflicted wound on the receiving end is **not responding** — and 26.4 shows why: a make-up-time notice ignored for 21 days from the date it was *sent* is deemed an agreement. If something from the FOC or a show-cause motion arrives, the response clock is already running. Answer in writing, within the stated period, even if the answer is "here is my good cause, with dates." `⚠ TIME-CRITICAL`

## 26.9 Safety overlay

`🛑 SAFETY OVERRIDE.` Enforcement is confrontation, and confrontation can trigger retaliation. Two practical consequences:

- **Route through the FOC, not the driveway, when contact is unsafe.** The FOC complaint and the make-up-time machinery exist precisely so a denied parent does not have to negotiate face-to-face. If direct contact with the other parent is unsafe, that is a reason to prefer the FOC route (26.3) over a self-driven confrontation, and to use neutral or supervised exchange options.
- **A safety order outranks a parenting-time order.** If there is a PPO or no-contact order, it controls how and whether exchanges happen until a court changes it. You do not enforce a parenting-time order in a way that violates a safety order, and you do not use an enforcement filing as a channel to re-open contact a safety order closed. That coordination is Module 17 and counsel. `🛑`

## Templates and tools

Enforcement route decision tree.** *Is your order specific and enforceable?* → **No** → this is not an enforcement problem yet; go to Module 12 (make it specific) or clarify an ambiguous term. → **Yes** → *Is the violation within 56 days and documented?* → **No** → log it as pattern evidence; do not stake a complaint on it. → **Yes** → *Do you want make-up-time machinery and a buffer, or a contempt finding and control?* → **make-up/buffer** → FOC written complaint (26.3–26.4). → **contempt/control** → motion to show cause (26.5). *Overlay at every branch: is contact safe? |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

*You are done with a row when the order term is quoted, the denial has a date and time, and at least one admissible exhibit or adult witness corroborates it.*

**4. Make-up-time notice calendar.** For each granted or deemed-agreed make-up claim: type of time denied (weekend / weekday / holiday / summer); the matching make-up type; the notice you must give (≥1 week or ≥28 days); the date you gave it; the one-year outer limit. *And, defensively:* every FOC notice received, with **21 days from the date it was sent** calendared as a response deadline.

**5. Motion-to-show-cause drafting aid.** A blank narrative attachment that quotes the breached term verbatim, lists each dated denial, ties each to its exhibit, and states the relief sought — **not** labeled filing-ready, and carrying a "do not use if" list (do not use if the order is vague — clarify or make it specific first; do not use if the violation is stale and undocumented; do not use if a safety order controls the exchange).

**6. Contempt-response worksheet.** For a parent on the receiving end: the specific term alleged breached; the date; your good-cause facts *with contemporaneous documentation*; your ability-to-comply facts; the response deadline (from the paper served) with its trigger named.

**7. Judgment compliance log.** The running, same-day record every other tool draws on — exercised time, missed time, late exchanges, channel messages — kept whether or not you ever file, because it is your evidence in both directions.

## Stop and seek counsel 🛑

- **Any PPO, no-contact order, or safety issue interacting with an exchange.** A safety order outranks a parenting-time order until a court changes it; do not enforce into a violation of one. Route through Module 17 and a DV advocate. `🛑 SAFETY OVERRIDE`
- **Any criminal or CPS overlay** — a "custodial interference" or parental-kidnapping question, a CPS matter, or police involvement beyond a keep-the-peace report. Enforcement self-help stops here. `🛑 DO NOT USE THIS GUIDE ALONE`
- **A child persistently refusing exchanges.** This is not a simple contempt case and not something to press through the child. It calls for advice and, often, a different kind of order — not a curbside showdown (26.7). `🛑`
- **You are the one facing contempt with jail or a license suspension on the table.** The sanctions in 26.5 are real; a response with genuine exposure warrants counsel, and the free resources in Module 4.7 exist for exactly this. `🛑`
