```
     BRIEF IN SUPPORT OF THE MOTION FOR ENTRY OF PARENTING
     TIME IN SPECIFIC TERMS UNDER MCL 722.27a(8)

I.   INTRODUCTION

     The order entered March 15, 2023 states that parenting
time shall be "as the parties agree." It states no date, no
time, and no location. The moving party asks the Court to state
parenting time in specific terms and to set the conditions that
allow those terms to function. The relief requested does not
change where the minor child primarily lives or who provides
her day-to-day care.

II.  FACTS

     1. The order entered March 15, 2023 provides that
parenting time shall be "as the parties agree." Its
communication provision provides that "the parties shall
communicate regarding parenting time."

     2. The moving party requested parenting time in writing,
by text message, on November 2, 2024, December 14, 2024, and
January 20, 2025 (Exhibit A). No parenting time occurred on or
following any of those requests.

     3. By letter dated January 8, 2025, signed by J. Marsh,
attorney for the other party, the moving party was directed to
communicate only by United States mail (Exhibit B). That
direction does not appear in the Court's order.

     4. The existing child support order attributes 104
overnights to the moving party. The regular schedule requested
in the proposed order — alternating weekends and one weekday
evening — is approximately 52 overnights per year.

III. THE COURT SHOULD GRANT PARENTING TIME IN SPECIFIC TERMS
     UNDER MCL 722.27a(8)

     MCL 722.27a(8) provides:

          "Parenting time shall be granted in specific terms
          if requested by either party at any time."

     The moving party is a party to this case and requests
specific terms. The subsection is mandatory ("shall"), states
no threshold, and applies "at any time."

     The Child Custody Act contemplates both forms of order.
MCL 722.27(1)(b) permits a court to provide for parenting time
"by general or specific terms and conditions." MCL 722.27a(8)
is the provision by which either parent obtains the specific
form.

     The Court of Appeals has applied this provision to a
generally worded parenting-time award. In Pickering v
Pickering, 268 Mich App 1 (2005), the trial court had awarded
"reasonable and liberal parenting time." The Court of Appeals
held that "'[r]easonable and liberal parenting time' is plainly
not a grant of parenting time in 'specific' terms," and that
"requests for specific parenting time terms must be considered
by the court regardless of the timing of the request." The
Court vacated the parenting-time provision and remanded "with
the direction to consider a specific parenting time order."
[Bracketed note for the Court: Pickering quotes the
specific-terms sentence as subsection (7); the 2016 amendment
renumbered it, and it is now subsection (8). The text is
identical.]

     A provision that parenting time shall occur "as the
parties agree" is, if anything, less definite than the
"reasonable and liberal" award held insufficient in Pickering.
The moving party has requested specific terms; under
MCL 722.27a(8) and Pickering, that request must be considered
and the specific terms granted.

     The moving party does not contend that MCL 722.27a(8)
displaces any threshold the Court finds applicable to
modification of an existing order. Section IV addresses that
question in the alternative.

IV.  IN THE ALTERNATIVE, THE THRESHOLD IS SATISFIED

     A. The requested order does not change the established
        custodial environment.

     MCL 722.27(1)(c) provides that "[t]he custodial
environment of a child is established if over an appreciable
time the child naturally looks to the custodian in that
environment for guidance, discipline, the necessities of life,
and parental comfort."

     The minor child has an established custodial environment
with [the other parent]. The child lives primarily with [the
other parent] and looks to that parent for day-to-day care. The
moving party does not ask the Court to disturb any of that. The
proposed order changes no residence, no school or childcare
arrangement, and no day-to-day care. It states times, dates,
and locations that the current order leaves to agreement.

     The point is confirmed by the numbers. The existing child
support order already attributes 104 overnights to the moving
party. The regular schedule requested is approximately 52
overnights per year — less than the overnight level the support
order itself recognizes. The requested order cannot fairly be
characterized as enlarging the moving party's role beyond the
existing arrangement when it asks for fewer overnights than that
arrangement already reflects.

     B. Because the established custodial environment is not
        changed, the parenting-time standard governs and the
        burden is a preponderance.

     In Shade v Wright, 291 Mich App 17 (2010), the Court of
Appeals held:

          "We discern nothing in the Vodvarka opinion that
          requires the standards used to determine the
          existence of proper cause or change of circumstances
          for custody determinations to apply to
          determinations regarding parenting time, absent a
          conclusion that a change in parenting time will
          result in a change in an established custodial
          environment. . . . If a change in parenting time
          results in a change in the established custodial
          environment, then the Vodvarka framework is
          appropriate."

     Shade limited its own holding. In footnote 4 the Court
stated: "With our holding today, we do not seek to precisely
define the proper cause or change of circumstances necessary to
change parenting time. Our holding is limited to our conclusion
that the normal life changes that occurred with the minor child
in this case are sufficient to modify parenting time." The
moving party cites Shade for the established-custodial-
environment distinction it drew, not for a general test.

     In Pierron v Pierron, 486 Mich 81, 92-93 (2010), the
Supreme Court allocated the burden: where the proposed change
alters the established custodial environment, the standard is
clear and convincing evidence; where it does not, the standard
is a preponderance of the evidence, and the burden rests on the
parent proposing the change. Because the requested order does
not alter the established custodial environment, the standard is
a preponderance, and the moving party accepts that burden.

     C. Proper cause and change of circumstances, if required.

     Vodvarka v Grasmeyer, 259 Mich App 499, 511, 513-514
(2003), defines the showing. "[P]roper cause means one or more
appropriate grounds that have or could have a significant effect
on the child's life to the extent that a reevaluation of the
child's custodial situation should be undertaken." A change of
circumstances requires proof that "since the entry of the last
custody order, the conditions surrounding custody of the child,
which have or could have a significant effect on the child's
well-being, have materially changed," and "something more than
the normal life changes."

     The facts are these. A provision that operates only on
agreement has, since March 15, 2023, produced no specified
parenting time. Since that order, the moving party has requested
parenting time in writing on the dates shown in Exhibit A, and
no parenting time has followed. The communication contemplated
by the order — "the parties shall communicate regarding
parenting time" — has been narrowed by counsel's January 8,
2025 letter to United States mail only, a medium that cannot
carry the timely logistics that a working schedule requires.
Whether those facts clear the threshold is for the Court; the
moving party pleads them as facts, with dates and exhibits.

     D. A flexible standard governs any change to a condition.

     To the extent the relief touches a condition on parenting
time rather than the schedule itself — for example, the method
of communication — Kaeb v Kaeb, 309 Mich App 556, 571-572
(2015), holds that neither Vodvarka nor Shade is directly on
point, and that a "lesser, more flexible understanding" of
proper cause applies: "proper cause" carries its "normal
meaning," namely "an appropriate ground for taking legal
action," and the movant must show that the condition "in its
current form no longer serves the child's best interests." The
court "may modify, revoke, or add conditions once it is shown
that there is proper cause or a change of circumstances." [Bracketed
note for the Court: Kaeb cites the terms-and-conditions menu as
subsection (8); the current statute places it at subsection (9)
after the 2016 renumbering.]

V.   THE STATUTORY FRAMING SUPPORTS SPECIFIC TERMS

     MCL 722.27a(1) provides: "It is presumed to be in the best
interests of a child for the child to have a strong relationship
with both of his or her parents," and that parenting time "shall
be granted to a parent in a frequency, duration, and type
reasonably calculated to promote a strong relationship between
the child and the parent granted parenting time." A provision
that has produced no specified parenting time is not, in
operation, reasonably calculated to promote that relationship.

     MCL 722.27a(3) provides: "A child has a right to parenting
time with a parent unless it is shown on the record by clear and
convincing evidence that it would endanger the child's physical,
mental, or emotional health." No such showing has been made or
attempted in this case.

VI.  THE PARENTING-TIME FACTORS

     MCL 722.27a(7) provides that "[t]he court may consider the
following factors when determining the frequency, duration, and
type of parenting time to be granted." Two are directly engaged
by this record; a third is addressed to confirm it does not
apply.

     Factor (f) — "[w]hether a parent can reasonably be expected
to exercise parenting time in accordance with the court order."
The moving party requested parenting time in writing on three
dated occasions, has kept a dated log of that contact, has not
violated the order, is current on support, and has missed no
exchange. That record answers the factor.

     Factor (g) — "[w]hether a parent has frequently failed to
exercise reasonable parenting time." Exhibit A shows the moving
party's written requests and their dates; the record is one of
requests made, not of parenting time declined by the moving
party.

     Factor (b) — whether the child is "a nursing child less
than 6 months of age, or less than 1 year of age if the child
receives substantial nutrition through nursing" — does not
apply. The minor child is 6 years old. The schedule requested —
alternating weekends and one weekday evening — is age-
appropriate for a school-age child.

     [Bracketed note for the Court: older opinions cite these
     factors as MCL 722.27a(6)(a)-(i); the 2016 amendment
     renumbered them, and the current statute places them at
     (7)(a)-(i).]

VII. THE REQUESTED CONDITIONS ARE AUTHORIZED BY STATUTE

     MCL 722.27a(9) provides that "[a] parenting time order may
contain any reasonable terms or conditions that facilitate the
orderly and meaningful exercise of parenting time by a parent,"
including division of the responsibility and cost of
transporting the child, subsections (9)(a) and (b); requirements
that the child be ready at a specific time, subsection (9)(d);
requirements that the parent arrive and return the child at
specific times, subsection (9)(e); requirements of reasonable
notice when parenting time will not occur, subsection (9)(h);
and "[a]ny other reasonable condition determined to be
appropriate in the particular case," subsection (9)(i).

     The proposed order tracks those subsections paragraph by
paragraph. Paragraphs 3 and 4 address transportation and cost.
Paragraph 5 requires the child to be ready at the stated time.
Paragraph 6 requires arrival and return at stated times.
Paragraph 7 requires written notice when a period will not
occur. Paragraph 8 requests a written, date-stamped method of
communication under subsection (9)(i), so that the notice
required by paragraph 7 can be given and proved. The moving
party does not request direct contact with the other party and
does not ask the Court to authorize any contact beyond written
communication through the ordered method.

     MCL 722.27a(10) requires that a parenting time order "shall
contain a prohibition on exercising parenting time in a country
that is not a party to the Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects
of International Child Abduction," absent both parents' written
consent. Paragraph 10 of the proposed order contains that
provision.

     MCL 722.27a(11) provides that "[d]uring the time a child is
with a parent to whom parenting time has been awarded, that
parent shall decide all routine matters concerning the child."
Paragraph 9 of the proposed order states that rule.

VIII. AN ENFORCEABLE ORDER IS THE OBJECT OF THIS MOTION

     MCL 552.641(2) permits the office of the friend of the
court to decline to respond to an alleged parenting time
violation where "[t]he custody or parenting time order does not
include an enforceable provision that is relevant to the custody
or parenting time order violation alleged in the complaint."
MCL 552.641(2)(c).

     An order providing that parenting time shall occur "as the
parties agree" contains no term that can be violated, and no
enforceable provision on which the friend of the court can act.
The relief requested would give both parents terms that can be
followed, and, if not followed, enforced.

IX.  RELIEF REQUESTED

     The moving party requests entry of the attached proposed
order granting parenting time in specific terms under
MCL 722.27a(8), with the conditions authorized by
MCL 722.27a(9) and the provision required by MCL 722.27a(10).
```

## What to notice about how this brief is built

- **Section III leads with the statute and one published case, then gives the threshold question away.** "The moving party does not contend that MCL 722.27a(8) displaces any threshold" is the most important sentence in the brief. No court has resolved how subsection (8) interacts with the modification threshold; claiming it settles the question would be a claim you cannot back. Conceding it openly and answering it in Section IV costs nothing and removes the easiest way to lose.
- **Section IV.A uses your numbers.** The 104-overnight fact in your support order is doing real work: your requested ~52 overnights is *below* it, which is the cleanest possible answer to "this is really a custody grab."
- **Section IV.B carries *Shade*'s footnote 4** and says plainly what *Shade* is cited for. That is how you use a narrow holding without overstating it.
- **Section VI walks two factors and disposes of a third — not all nine.** Walking every factor reads as padding. Factor (b) is addressed only to confirm your daughter's age takes it off the table.
- **Every pre-2016 case citation carries a bracketed renumbering note.** *Pickering*'s "(7)," *Kaeb*'s "(8)," and the factor list's "(6)" all shifted down one subsection in 2016. A brief that quotes the old numbers without the note points the Court at the wrong provision.
- **No unpublished opinion is cited, and no case is cited whose holding was not read.** MCR 7.215(C)(1) would require you to explain why you cite an unpublished opinion where published authority exists; the simplest safe course is not to.
