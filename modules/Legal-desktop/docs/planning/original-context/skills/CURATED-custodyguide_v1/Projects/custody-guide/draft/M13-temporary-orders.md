# Why this module matters

Module 2 taught the rule. This is the module where that rule reaches into your case and either helps you or hurts you — usually within the first few weeks, often on a piece of paper that says "temporary" at the top and feels low-stakes because of it.

A **temporary order** is the order that governs custody, parenting time, and support *while the case is pending* — the months (sometimes more than a year) between filing and the final judgment. It feels provisional. The word invites you to relax. That is exactly the danger. The single most valuable lesson in this whole book — the "just for now" trap from **Module 2.1** — does its damage *here*, because a temporary arrangement quietly builds the child's real, lived environment while everyone waits for the "real" hearing. By the time the real hearing arrives, that environment may have set the burden of proof against you.

This module covers four things: how a temporary order is obtained, how long it actually lasts, what to ask for and what to be careful about agreeing to, and how the temporary order interacts with the final best-interest hearing. Throughout, the frame is the same: **"temporary" describes the order, not its effect on the child's life.**

## 13.1 What a temporary order is, and how you get one

The court's power to enter temporary orders comes from **MCR 3.207**. The scope is broad:

> "(A) Scope of Relief. The court may issue ex parte and temporary orders with regard to any matter within its jurisdiction, and may issue protective orders against domestic violence as provided in subchapter 3.700."
> — MCR 3.207(A)

There are three ways a temporary order comes into being. Knowing which road you are on tells you what protections you have.

**Road 1 — a contested motion.** A party files a verified motion asking for temporary relief, the other side can object, and the court decides after a hearing.

> "(1) A request for a temporary order may be made at any time during the pendency of the case by filing a verified motion that sets forth facts sufficient to support the relief requested.
> (2) A temporary order may not be issued without a hearing, unless the parties agree otherwise or fail to file a written objection or motion as provided in subrules (B)(5)."
> — MCR 3.207(C)(1)–(2)

Read subrule (2) twice. A temporary order *"may not be issued without a hearing"* — **unless the parties agree otherwise.** That "unless" is the hinge this whole module turns on (see 13.4). The mechanics of drafting and filing that verified motion — the numbered facts, the proposed order, service, and Genesee's concurrence-certificate and proposed-order local rule — belong to **Module 12**; this module is about *what the order does to your case*, not how to file it.

**Road 2 — a stipulation (the parties agree).** Most temporary orders in ordinary cases are *stipulated* or *consent* orders: the two parties (or their lawyers) negotiate terms and submit a signed proposed order, and the judge enters it without a contested hearing. This is the fastest road and the most common — and, because of subrule (C)(2)'s "unless the parties agree," it is the road on which the reader has the fewest built-in protections. A signature does the work a contested hearing would otherwise require.

**Road 3 — an ex parte order that nobody objects to.** An **ex parte** order is one the court enters based on one side's papers, before the other side is heard (the getting-and-fighting mechanics, the specific-facts affidavit, and the **14-day** objection clock are **Module 14**'s subject — this module does not restate them). What matters here is what happens if that order is *not* timely challenged:

> "(c) If no timely objection or motion to rescind or modify the ex parte custody, parenting time, or support order is filed, the order is a temporary order. If a hearing date is specified in the ex parte order, the evidentiary hearing will proceed on the specified hearing date."
> — MCR 3.207(B)(5)(c)

In plain terms: **an ex parte order that you do not object to in time converts, by operation of the rule, into a temporary order.** Silence is a decision. If you have been served with an ex parte order, the clock in Module 14 is running *now*.

## 13.2 How long "temporary" really lasts

The rule tells you plainly how long a temporary order stays in force, and the answer is longer than the word suggests.

> "(3) A temporary order may be modified at any time during the pendency of the case, following a hearing and upon a showing of good cause.
> (4) A temporary order must state its effective date and whether its provisions may be modified retroactively by a subsequent order.
> (5) A temporary order remains in effect until modified or until the entry of the final judgment or order.
> (6) A temporary order not yet satisfied is vacated by the entry of the final judgment or order, unless specifically continued or preserved. This does not apply to support arrearages that have been assigned to the state, which are preserved unless specifically waived or reduced by the final judgment or order."
> — MCR 3.207(C)(3)–(6)

Three consequences a reader should sit with:

- **It lasts until the case ends.** Subrule (5): a temporary order "remains in effect until modified or until the entry of the final judgment or order." A contested custody case can run many months. Whatever the temporary order sets up, the child *lives it* for that entire stretch.
- **Changing it is not easy.** Subrule (3): modification requires *a hearing* **and** *a showing of good cause*. You cannot simply undo a temporary order you regret by asking again — the same barrier that would slow the other parent down also slows you down. This cuts both ways, which is the honest point: the machinery is neutral.
- **The order dissolves at the end, but that is not the relief it sounds like.** Subrule (6): the temporary order is "vacated by the entry of the final judgment." The *order* goes away. What does **not** go away is everything the child's life became while the order was in force. That is the subject of 13.3 and 13.6, and it is the reason "temporary" is such a misleading word.

**Effective date and retroactivity (subrule (4)).** A temporary order "must state its effective date and whether its provisions may be modified retroactively." This matters most for support (whether a later change reaches back), but it is a checklist item on *any* temporary order — an order silent on retroactivity leaves a gap. See the clause checklist in Templates and tools.

## 13.3 Where the trap springs: temporary orders and the established custodial environment

Everything in Module 2.1 was written for this moment. Here is the operational version.

> ### ⚠ BOXED WARNING — THIS IS WHERE THE "JUST FOR NOW" ORDER SPRINGS
> **Module 2.1 owns this rule and carries the verbatim standard and the *Hayes* holding. This box is the operational version. If you have not read Module 2.1, read it first, then come back here.**
>
> An **established custodial environment (ECE)** is a question of **fact about how the child actually lives** — not about what an order says, and not about whether the order was labeled "temporary." As Module 2.1 quotes the controlling authority, it makes no difference whether the environment was created by a court order, without one, or against one. (*Hayes v Hayes*, 209 Mich App 385 (1995); the exact holding-quote is confirmation-pending in the verification ledger — confirm it before putting it in a filing. Module 2.1 carries this flag.)
>
> **The operational consequence — the whole reason this module exists.** If you agree to a temporary order that gives the other parent primary day-to-day care *"just for now,"* and the case then runs, say, fourteen months, the child may form an established custodial environment **in that other home** while everyone waits for the final hearing. At that hearing, the burden of proof is fixed by the ECE that exists **then** — and moving the child *out* of an established environment requires **clear and convincing evidence** (Module 2.2), the most demanding standard in civil law. Your own signature can help build the wall you later have to climb.
>
> **The machinery is neutral.** It favors whoever ends up holding the child's day-to-day environment — which may be you, or may be the other parent. This module does not assume you are the "right" parent (see the project's own rule against that assumption). The point is not who deserves to win. The point is that a *"temporary"* arrangement quietly sets the burden for the *permanent* one, and a reader who does not see that coming can lose the case at the temporary stage without ever knowing a decision was made. `⚠ TIME-CRITICAL`
>
> (Cross-referenced from Module 2.1, FM.0, Module 12, Module 14, and Module 17.)

**The status-quo dynamic, stated plainly.** Courts and the Friend of the Court both tend to value *stability* and *continuity* for a child — and Module 2.4's factor (d) rewards "the length of time the child has lived in a stable, satisfactory environment" and "the desirability of maintaining continuity." A temporary order *creates* a status quo. The longer the case runs, the more the temporary arrangement looks like the child's normal life, and the more a court is reluctant to disturb it. This is why a parent focused only on the final hearing can be quietly outmaneuvered at the temporary stage: whoever establishes the day-to-day environment early, and holds it, is often arguing from the stronger position by the time the "real" hearing arrives. None of that requires anyone to act in bad faith. Time does the work.

**Why "it's only temporary" is cold comfort.** Because the ECE turns on facts (Module 2.1), the label on the order does not control. A child who spends fourteen months looking to one parent for "guidance, discipline, the necessities of life, and parental comfort" (the statutory language Module 2.1 quotes) may have an established custodial environment there *regardless of what the order was called.* Michigan judicial-training materials make the same point Module 2.1 records: a temporary order does not *automatically* create an established environment — but it sometimes does, precisely because the child settles in while the case drags.

## 13.4 Your shield: the evidentiary-hearing requirement — and how agreement waives it

There is a real protection against a temporary order that would shift the child's established environment. It is worth understanding exactly, because it is powerful **and** because it has an off-switch that most readers flip without realizing it.

**The shield.** When a custody or parenting-time matter is *contested*, the court cannot change the child's established custodial environment without first holding an evidentiary hearing on clear and convincing evidence:

> "(1) When the custody, parenting time, change of domicile, or another motion regarding a minor is contested, the court may not enter an order resolving the contested matter that changes a child's established custodial environment without first holding an evidentiary hearing to determine whether clear and convincing evidence exists to support the order. When the custody of a minor or a motion that would change a child's established custodial environment is contested, a hearing on the matter must be held within 56 days
> (a) after the court orders, or
> (b) after the filing of notice that a hearing is requested, unless both parties agree to mediation under MCR 3.216 or MCR 3.224(G) and mediation is unsuccessful, in which event the hearing must be held within 56 days after the final mediation session."
> — MCR 3.210(C)(1)

> "(3) The court must enter a decision within 28 days after the hearing."
> — MCR 3.210(C)(3)

So if the other parent asks the court to hand them primary care on a temporary basis and **you contest it**, the court may not simply sign that order over your objection. It has to hold an evidentiary hearing — within **56 days** — and find *clear and convincing evidence* before changing the established environment, with a decision due within **28 days** after that hearing. (The court "may extend for good cause the time within which a hearing must be held and a decision rendered," MCR 3.210(C)(7).) `⚠ TIME-CRITICAL` — these periods, and their triggering events (the court's order, or the filing of notice that a hearing is requested), belong in Appendix A (Master Deadline Table).

There is a matching protection at the ex parte stage. When an order *could* alter the ECE, the court cannot enter it without scheduling a fast evidentiary hearing:

> "(b) The court must not issue an order that could alter a child's established custodial environment without also scheduling an evidentiary hearing under MCL 722.27 to determine whether clear and convincing evidence exists to support the order. The hearing must be held within 21 days after entry of the ex parte order and a notice of the hearing must be included in the ex parte order. An ex parte order entered under this subsection will expire upon issuance of a subsequent order following the evidentiary hearing."
> — MCR 3.207(B)(1)(b)

(That **21-day** ex parte hearing, and the 14-day objection clock that precedes it, are Module 14's territory — noted here only so you can see the shield exists at every stage.)

**A sword for the responding parent.** If the other side moves for a temporary order that would change custody or parenting time, their papers are required to address the ECE head-on:

> "(a) A verified pleading or an affidavit attached to a motion or pleading that requests an ex parte custody or parenting time order or that requests a change of custody or parenting time must include the following information:
> (i) facts establishing whether the child has an established custodial environment with either parent, both parents, neither parent, or a third party; and
> (ii) either facts establishing that entry of the requested order will not change the child's established custodial environment, facts establishing that clear and convincing evidence exists that the change in the child's established custodial environment is in the child's best interest, or if the request is from a third party, facts establishing by clear and convincing evidence that it is not in the child's best interests for the parent(s) to have custody."
> — MCR 3.207(B)(1)(a)

A motion that skips this is missing something the rule *requires*. A reader responding to such a motion can point that out. (The same rule is a *requirement* if **you** are the one moving — Module 12 and Module 14 cover how to plead the ECE facts.)

> ### ⚠ THE OFF-SWITCH: AGREEMENT WAIVES THE SHIELD
> The evidentiary-hearing protection exists for a **contested** matter. Look again at MCR 3.207(C)(2): a temporary order "may not be issued without a hearing, **unless the parties agree otherwise.**"
>
> When you **stipulate** to a temporary order, there is nothing to contest. No contest means no evidentiary hearing, no clear-and-convincing finding, and no judicial gatekeeping of the ECE change at all. The court can enter the order you signed. **The single signature on a stipulated temporary order can hand away the exact protection MCR 3.210(C)(1) was written to give you.**
>
> This is not a reason to refuse every stipulation — agreed orders resolve most cases sensibly and avoid the cost and risk of a hearing. It is a reason to know, before you sign, *whether the order you are agreeing to would change the established custodial environment.* If it would, you are not just agreeing to an arrangement; you may be waiving a hearing and pre-setting your own burden for the final decision. That is what the ECE-impact declaration in Templates and tools is for. `⚠ TIME-CRITICAL`

## 13.5 What to ask for, and what to be careful about agreeing to

Because the ECE is built by facts on the ground, the terms of a temporary order are not housekeeping — they are the frame the rest of the case is argued inside. This section is about *sizing and wording*, not about who should win.

### Terms worth asking for

- **Specific, enforceable parenting-time terms — not "as the parties agree."** A vague temporary order ("reasonable parenting time as the parties agree") leaves you dependent on the other parent's cooperation, and gives you nothing a court can enforce if that cooperation fails. Module 2.5 identifies the statutory hooks for insisting on specificity (the parenting-time factors, including whether a parent can be expected to comply and whether a parent has frequently failed to exercise time), and Module 2.7 explains why the *narrowest relief that solves your actual problem* is usually the disciplined ask. The mechanics of that motion are Module 12.
- **A review or return date.** A temporary order with a built-in date to come back and reassess keeps "temporary" from silently becoming "permanent by inertia." It also gives you a scheduled moment to raise a problem instead of having to clear the "good cause" bar for modification (13.2) from a standing start.
- **A stated effective date and a retroactivity provision.** Subrule (C)(4) requires the order to state its effective date and whether it can be modified retroactively. Do not leave this blank by default.
- **The residence protections a custody order must contain.** A judgment or order awarding custody "must provide" that the child's domicile may not be moved from Michigan without the court's approval, that the custodian must notify the Friend of the Court of an address change, and that neither parent may change the child's legal residence except in compliance with MCL 722.31. (MCR 3.211(C)(1)–(3).) These protect against a quiet relocation; be wary of any temporary term that waives or contradicts them.

### Terms to be careful about agreeing to

- **Any term that shifts the child's primary day-to-day care.** This is the ECE-builder from 13.3. It is not automatically wrong — sometimes it reflects reality, and sometimes it is genuinely best for the child — but it is never neutral, and it should never be signed as if it were routine. If a proposed order would move primary care, run the ECE-impact declaration first.
- **Vague "as the parties agree" language.** The mirror image of the ask above. In a high-conflict case, "as agreed" means "as the other parent permits."
- **Evidentiary stipulations buried in the order.** Watch for a clause agreeing that a report may be admitted, or that objections are waived. In particular, **a stipulation that the Friend of the Court report may be admitted into evidence gives away a protection Module 15 explains in detail** (the report is not evidence unless both parties agree to admit it — so agreeing is exactly what converts a not-evidence document into evidence *for and against you*). Do not trade that away in a temporary order without understanding what Module 15 says.
- **Consent to relocation or a change of the child's legal residence.** A clause that consents to a move, or to enrolling the child in a school near the other parent's new home, can function as a domicile waiver. Change of domicile is its own subject with its own standard (Module 27), and unilateral or contested relocation is a **stop-and-seek-counsel** trigger (see below). Do not resolve it inside a "temporary" order without advice.

### The "without prejudice" recital — useful, but not a cure

Many stipulated temporary orders include a recital along the lines of: *this order is temporary and entered without prejudice to either party's position on custody or parenting time, and shall not be treated as evidence of an established custodial environment.* Asking for language like that is reasonable and generally worth doing.

But understand its limit, which follows directly from Module 2.1: **a recital governs how the *order* is characterized; it cannot change the *facts* of how the child actually lives.** The established custodial environment is decided on the child's real, lived experience — where the child looks for daily care and comfort over an appreciable time — not on a sentence in an order disclaiming that effect. A recital may help frame the order's legal significance; it does not freeze the child's life in place or guarantee the court will disregard fourteen months of one arrangement. Treat it as a partial protection worth asking for, not a shield that undoes 13.3.

> `ℹ PROVISIONAL` **How much weight a Michigan court gives a "not evidence of ECE / without prejudice" recital in a temporary order is not something this guide can state as a settled rule from the primary sources on hand.** The caution above is reasoned from the verified principle that the ECE is a question of fact (Module 2.1), not from a case squarely on the recital's effect. A licensed Michigan attorney should confirm the point before you rely on such a recital as protection.

> ### 🛑 SAFETY OVERRIDE — temporary orders in a domestic-violence context
> If there is a PPO, a no-contact order, or any safety concern, a temporary order's exchange and parenting-time terms **do not** override those protections, and you should not be pressured into a "cooperative" stipulation that puts you or the child in contact you are trying to avoid. A temporary custodial parent's decision to reside with the child in a domestic-violence shelter is, by statute, **not** evidence of an intent to conceal the child (Module 2.5 owns that carve-out). Coordinate any temporary order with the PPO and safety processes — Module 17 and the safety plan in FM.5 — rather than negotiating it in isolation. 🛑

## 13.6 How a temporary order meets the final best-interest hearing

This is the interaction the whole module has been building toward. Recall subrule (C)(6): the temporary order "is vacated by the entry of the final judgment or order, unless specifically continued or preserved." So at the end of the case the *order* dissolves. Two things survive it, and they are what decide the final hearing.

1. **The facts the order created survive.** The established custodial environment that formed while the temporary order was in force is a fact about the child's life (Module 2.1). It does not vanish when the order is vacated. At the final hearing, the court decides the ECE **as it actually exists at that point** — and then applies the burden that follows from it (Module 2.2). If the temporary order gave one parent primary care for a year, the ECE analysis at trial starts from *that* reality, not from where things stood at filing.

2. **The status quo the order created carries weight.** The best-interest factors reward stability and continuity (Module 2.4, factor (d)). A year of one arrangement is a year of "the child's environment" that a court is measuring against any proposed change.

Put those together and the lesson is blunt: **the temporary order is where the burden for the final order is often quietly decided.** By the time you reach the evidentiary hearing that Module 16 covers — for many Genesee pro se parents, the referee hearing is the real trial — the ground you are fighting on was largely graded at the temporary stage. That is not a reason to panic at the start of a case; it is a reason to treat the "temporary" order with the seriousness the final one deserves, and to understand that the two are connected by the facts of the child's life even though the rule vacates the first when it enters the second.

> ### ⚠ The connection in one sentence
> The final judgment **vacates the temporary order** (MCR 3.207(C)(6)) but is **decided against the established custodial environment the temporary order helped create** (Module 2.1–2.2) — which is why "it's only temporary" is the most expensive sentence in a custody case.

## Templates and tools

**1. "Does this temporary order change the ECE?" decision tree.** Walk it before you sign or stipulate to anything:
*Does the proposed order move the child's primary day-to-day care from where it is now? → If no → note it and proceed (still check the other clauses below). → If yes → With whom is the child's established custodial environment now (you / the other parent / both / neither — from Module 2.1)? → Would this order, run over the likely length of the case, shift the child's daily environment to the other home? → If yes → recognize that (a) you may be waiving the MCR 3.210(C)(1) evidentiary hearing by agreeing, and (b) you may be pre-setting a clear-and-convincing burden against yourself at the final hearing. Do not sign on that basis without deciding, deliberately, that you accept it — and consider getting advice first.*

**2. Temporary-order clause checklist (blank aid).** For any temporary order — proposed by you, or handed to you to sign — check that it addresses each item, and note what each says:
- [ ] **Custody / primary care:** who has the child day to day, and does this *change* the current environment?
- [ ] **Parenting time:** are the terms **specific and enforceable**, or vague ("as the parties agree")?
- [ ] **Effective date** stated? (MCR 3.207(C)(4))
- [ ] **Retroactivity** addressed — may provisions be modified retroactively? (MCR 3.207(C)(4))
- [ ] **Review / return date** built in?
- [ ] **Residence / domicile protections** present? (MCR 3.211(C) — no move from Michigan without approval; FOC notice of address change; MCL 722.31 legal-residence restriction)
- [ ] **Evidentiary stipulations** — does the order agree to admit any report or waive any objection? (See Module 15 before agreeing.)
- [ ] **Relocation / school-change consent** — any clause that consents to a move? (Stop — Module 27; stop-and-seek gate below.)
- [ ] **Non-prejudice / "not evidence of ECE" recital** present — and do you understand its limit (13.5)?

**3. ECE-impact declaration (blank aid).** Before signing, write down: *(a) Where does the child primarily live and look for daily care right now?* *(b) What would this order change about that?* *(c) Does any term shift primary care to the other parent?* *(d) If the case runs 12–18 months under this order, whose home becomes the child's established environment?* *(e) What burden would I face at the final hearing as a result — preponderance, or clear and convincing (Module 2.2)?* *Done when every term that moves primary care is named and paired with the burden it would create.*

**4. "Before you sign" review card.** Four questions, in order, for any stipulated or consent temporary order:
1. **ECE:** Does this shift the child's primary environment? (If yes, 13.3–13.4 apply.)
2. **Evidence waiver:** Does this admit a report or waive an objection? (Module 15.)
3. **Relocation:** Does this consent to a move or residence change? (Module 27; stop-and-seek.)
4. **Vagueness:** Is any key term left "as the parties agree" instead of specific and enforceable? (Modules 2.5, 2.7, 12.)

**5. Worked example — a sample stipulated temporary order (teaching artifact, NOT filing-ready).**
*Assumed posture:* a pending divorce with one minor child; before this order the child lived primarily with Parent A and saw Parent B on alternating weekends; the parties are negotiating a temporary order at the start of the case. *Do not use if:* your facts differ (they will); there is any domestic-violence, PPO, or safety issue; a domicile or relocation question is live; or you have not read Modules 2, 12, and 14. This sample exists to be marked up, not filed.

> **STIPULATED TEMPORARY ORDER (SAMPLE — DO NOT FILE)**
> 1. Pending further order, the minor child shall reside primarily with **Parent B**, and Parent A shall have parenting time **as the parties agree**.
> 2. The parties **stipulate that the Friend of the Court report, when completed, may be admitted into evidence** at any hearing in this matter.
> 3. This order is **effective on entry.**

*Completed ECE-impact declaration for the sample (the answer key to the exercise below):*
- **Term 1 shifts primary care from A to B.** This is the ECE-builder. Before the order, the child's established environment was with Parent A. Run over a year, this term builds a new established environment in Parent B's home — after which Parent A must show **clear and convincing evidence** to move it back (Module 2.2). And because it is *stipulated*, Parent A gets no MCR 3.210(C)(1) evidentiary hearing on that change (13.4). One signature, two protections gone.
- **Term 1 also leaves Parent A's time "as the parties agree"** — vague and unenforceable, leaving A dependent on B's cooperation (Modules 2.5, 2.7).
- **Term 2 stipulates the FOC report into evidence** — waiving the protection Module 15 explains (the report is not evidence unless both parties agree; agreeing makes it evidence for and against A).
- **Term 3 is silent on retroactivity and has no review or return date** — gaps under MCR 3.207(C)(4), with no scheduled moment to revisit.

## Stop and seek counsel 🛑

- **A domicile or relocation term.** If a proposed temporary order consents to a move, a change of the child's legal residence, or a school change tied to a relocation, this guide stops here. Change of domicile has its own standard (Module 27), and **threatened unilateral relocation is a hard-stop gate** — get advice before agreeing. 🛑
- **Any ex parte order changing custody or suspending parenting time.** If you have been served with an ex parte order that removes the child from you or suspends your time, the 14-day clock and the response mechanics are in **Module 14**, and the situation warrants counsel — especially with any CPS or criminal overlay. 🛑 `DO NOT USE THIS GUIDE ALONE`
- **Any PPO, no-contact order, or safety issue** interacting with a temporary parenting-time or exchange term. Coordinate with Module 17 and FM.5; a temporary order does not override a safety order. 🛑 SAFETY OVERRIDE
- **A stipulation you do not understand.** If you cannot tell whether a temporary order you are being asked to sign changes the established custodial environment, that uncertainty is itself the signal to get advice **before** you sign — the free resources in Module 4.7 (Genesee Legal Resource Center, Legal Services of Eastern Michigan, limited-scope referral) exist for exactly this moment. 🛑
