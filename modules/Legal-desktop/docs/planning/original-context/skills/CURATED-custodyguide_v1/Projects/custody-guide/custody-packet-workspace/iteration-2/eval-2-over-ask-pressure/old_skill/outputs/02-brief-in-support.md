     BRIEF IN SUPPORT OF [MOVING PARTY]'S MOTION FOR ENTRY OF
     PARENTING TIME IN SPECIFIC TERMS

I.   INTRODUCTION

     The order entered March 15, 2023 states that parenting
time shall be "as the parties agree." It states no date, no
time, and no location. The moving party asks the Court to state
parenting time in specific terms and to set the conditions that
allow those terms to function. The moving party does not ask
the Court to change the child's primary residence or to change
custody.

II.  FACTS

     1. The order entered March 15, 2023 provides, as to
parenting time: "as the parties agree." It provides, as to
communication: "the parties shall communicate regarding
parenting time."

     2. The order states no date, time, or location for
parenting time, and no method or response time for
communication.

     3. The moving party requested parenting time in writing,
through direct written contact consistent with the order's
communication provision, on November 2, 2024, December 14,
2024, and January 20, 2025 (Exhibit A). Each request went
unanswered.

     4. The child support order in this case recites [104]
overnights for the moving party. [Confirm the figure and the
recital against your support order — 2025 MCSF Sec. 3.03(E)
requires the order to state it.]

III. THE COURT SHOULD GRANT PARENTING TIME IN SPECIFIC TERMS
     UNDER MCL 722.27a(8)

     MCL 722.27a(8) provides:

          "Parenting time shall be granted in specific terms
          if requested by either party at any time."

     The moving party is a party. The moving party requests
specific terms. The subsection is mandatory ("shall"), states
no threshold, and applies "at any time."

     The Court of Appeals has applied this provision directly.
In Pickering v Pickering, 268 Mich App 1 (2005), the Court held
that "'[r]easonable and liberal parenting time' is plainly not
a grant of parenting time in 'specific' terms," and that a
request for specific terms "must be considered by the court
regardless of the timing of the request." [Pickering quotes
this provision as subsection (7); the current statute places it
at (8).] An order providing that parenting time occurs only "as
the parties agree" is less definite than the "reasonable and
liberal" provision Pickering found insufficient.

     The Child Custody Act contemplates both forms of order.
MCL 722.27(1)(b) permits a court to provide for parenting time
"by general or specific terms and conditions." MCL 722.27a(8)
is the provision by which either parent obtains the specific
form.

     The moving party does not contend that MCL 722.27a(8)
displaces any threshold the Court finds applicable to
modification of an existing order. Section IV addresses that
question in the alternative.

IV.  IN THE ALTERNATIVE, ANY THRESHOLD IS SATISFIED

     A. The requested order does not change the established
        custodial environment.

     MCL 722.27(1)(c) provides that "[t]he custodial
environment of a child is established if over an appreciable
time the child naturally looks to the custodian in that
environment for guidance, discipline, the necessities of life,
and parental comfort."

     The minor child has an established custodial environment
with [the other parent / both parents — state your true
facts]. The moving party does not ask the Court to disturb it.
The proposed order changes no residence, no school, and no
day-to-day care. It states times, dates, and locations that the
current order leaves to agreement, and it does so at
approximately the number of overnights the support order in
this case already recites. The requested schedule therefore
does not approach the change of custody that a substantial
increase in overnights would represent.

     B. Because the established custodial environment is not
        changed, the parenting-time standard governs and the
        burden is a preponderance.

     In Shade v Wright, 291 Mich App 17 (2010), the Court of
Appeals held:

          "We discern nothing in the Vodvarka opinion that
          requires the standards used to determine the
          existence of proper cause or change of circumstances
          for custody determinations to apply to
          determinations regarding parenting time, absent a
          conclusion that a change in parenting time will
          result in a change in an established custodial
          environment. . . . If a change in parenting time
          results in a change in the established custodial
          environment, then the Vodvarka framework is
          appropriate."

     Shade limited its own holding. In footnote 4 the Court
stated that it did "not seek to precisely define the proper
cause or change of circumstances necessary to change parenting
time," and that its holding was "limited to" the facts before
it. The moving party cites Shade for the ECE-based distinction
it drew, not for a general test.

     In Pierron v Pierron, 486 Mich 81, 92-93 (2010), the
Supreme Court allocated the burden: where the proposed change
alters the established custodial environment, the standard is
clear and convincing evidence; where it does not, the standard
is a preponderance of the evidence, and the burden rests on the
parent proposing the change. The moving party accepts that
burden here.

     C. Proper cause and change of circumstances, if required.

     Vodvarka v Grasmeyer, 259 Mich App 499, 511, 513-514
(2003), defines the showing. "[P]roper cause means one or more
appropriate grounds that have or could have a significant
effect on the child's life to the extent that a reevaluation of
the child's custodial situation should be undertaken." A change
of circumstances requires proof that "since the entry of the
last custody order, the conditions surrounding custody of the
child, which have or could have a significant effect on the
child's well-being, have materially changed," and "something
more than the normal life changes."

     The facts are these, stated without characterization. A
provision that operates only on agreement has produced no
scheduled, enforceable parenting time in the years since the
order entered. Three written requests, made through the ordered
channel, produced no parenting time. The minor child is six
years old, an age at which the regularity of contact bears on
the relationship the statute presumes to be in the child's
interest. Whether those facts clear the threshold is for the
Court; they are stated as facts, with dates, for the Court to
weigh.

V.   THE STATUTORY FRAMING SUPPORTS SPECIFIC TERMS

     MCL 722.27a(1) provides: "It is presumed to be in the best
interests of a child for the child to have a strong
relationship with both of his or her parents," and that
parenting time "shall be granted to a parent in a frequency,
duration, and type reasonably calculated to promote a strong
relationship." A provision that has produced no scheduled
parenting time is not, in operation, reasonably calculated to
promote that relationship.

     MCL 722.27a(3) provides: "A child has a right to parenting
time with a parent unless it is shown on the record by clear
and convincing evidence that it would endanger the child's
physical, mental, or emotional health." No such showing has
been made or attempted.

VI.  THE PARENTING-TIME FACTORS

     MCL 722.27a(7) provides that "[t]he court may consider the
following factors when determining the frequency, duration, and
type of parenting time to be granted." Two are engaged by this
record. [Older opinions cite these factors as MCL 722.27a(6)(a)-
(i); the current statute places them at (7)(a)-(i).]

     Factor (f) asks "[w]hether a parent can reasonably be
expected to exercise parenting time in accordance with the
court order." The moving party has communicated only through the
ordered channel and has made specific written requests
(Exhibit A).

     Factor (g) asks "[w]hether a parent has frequently failed
to exercise reasonable parenting time." Exhibit A shows written
requests and their outcomes.

VII. THE REQUESTED CONDITIONS ARE AUTHORIZED BY STATUTE

     MCL 722.27a(9) provides that a parenting-time order "may
contain any reasonable terms or conditions that facilitate the
orderly and meaningful exercise of parenting time," including
division of transportation responsibility and cost, subsections
(9)(a) and (b); that the child be ready at a specific time,
subsection (9)(d); that each parent arrive and return the child
at specific times, subsection (9)(e); reasonable notice when
parenting time will not occur, subsection (9)(h); and "[a]ny
other reasonable condition determined to be appropriate,"
subsection (9)(i). The proposed order tracks these subsections
paragraph by paragraph.

     Under subsection (9)(i), the proposed order sets a written,
date-stamped method of communication limited to matters
concerning the child, so that the notice required by the order
can be given and proved. The current provision — "the parties
shall communicate regarding parenting time" — specifies no
method and no response time, and a written request under it drew
no response on three occasions. The moving party does not
request direct contact beyond that written method, and does not
ask the Court to relax any protection.

     MCL 722.27a(10) requires that a parenting-time order "shall
contain a prohibition on exercising parenting time in a country
that is not a party to the Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects
of International Child Abduction," absent both parents' written
consent. The proposed order contains that provision.

VIII. AN ENFORCEABLE ORDER IS THE OBJECT OF THIS MOTION

     MCL 552.641(2) permits the office of the friend of the
court to decline to respond to an alleged parenting-time
violation where "[t]he custody or parenting time order does not
include an enforceable provision that is relevant to the . . .
violation alleged." MCL 552.641(2)(c). An order providing that
parenting time shall be "as the parties agree" contains no term
that can be violated. The relief requested would give both
parents terms that can be followed and, if not followed,
enforced.

IX.  RELIEF REQUESTED

     The moving party requests entry of the attached proposed
order.

### Why this brief is shaped the way it is

- **Section III concedes something on purpose.** "The moving party does not contend that
  MCL 722.27a(8) displaces any threshold" removes the easiest way to lose. No case has
  been read for this project on how (8) interacts with the modification threshold, so
  claiming it settles the question would be a claim nobody can back. `UNVERIFIED — case
  law on subsection (8)'s interaction with the MCL 722.27(1)(c) threshold not read. Do
  not upgrade this to settled law.`
- **Section IV.A is the anti-recharacterization paragraph.** It states affirmatively
  that the schedule does not alter the ECE and ties that to the overnight figure the
  support order already recites. This is the sentence that keeps the motion in *Shade*'s
  preponderance lane instead of *Vodvarka*'s clear-and-convincing lane.
- **Section VI walks two factors, not nine.** Walking all nine when two are engaged reads
  as padding.
- **No unpublished opinion is cited, and no case is cited whose holding was not read.**
  MCR 7.215(C)(1) would require explaining why an unpublished opinion is cited where
  published authority exists; the simplest safe course is not to. *Hayes v Hayes* is a
  natural fit for the ECE point and is deliberately absent — its holding text was not
  read for this project.
