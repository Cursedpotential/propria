# Why this module has two doors

Most modules teach one thing. This one teaches two, because an "ex parte order" shows up in a custody case from two opposite directions, and the reader is almost always standing at one door or the other:

- **You are thinking about asking for one** — something has happened that feels like it cannot wait for the ordinary motion timeline, and you want the court to act *now*, before the other parent is even notified. **Go to 14.1.** The short version: the bar is high, it is not a shortcut for an ordinary dispute, and asking for one you cannot support can cost you credibility you will need later.
- **One was served on you** — the other parent went to court without telling you, walked out with an order, and now you are holding it. **Go to 14.2, today.** The short version: a 14-day clock is already running, the order is in force right now whether you agree with it or not, and doing nothing is the one move that makes it stick.

Both doors open onto the same rule — **MCR 3.207(B)** — so this module quotes that rule in full and in its own words, because in an emergency posture the exact text is the thing that protects you. Read the door you are standing at first; read the other one when you have time.

**One idea to carry through both doors:** an ex parte order is the court acting on *one side's sworn word*, with the other side absent. The law lets that happen only in a narrow set of genuine emergencies, and it builds in a fast hearing where the absent side finally gets heard. Understanding *why* the law is stingy with these orders is what lets you either ask for one correctly or take one apart.

## 14.1 Getting one — the high bar, and why it is not a shortcut

### An ex parte order is the exception, not a faster version of the normal motion

The ordinary way to get temporary relief in a custody case is a **noticed motion**: you file, you serve the other parent, both sides show up, the court hears it. That is Module 12 (how a motion works) and Module 13 (what a temporary order does). The rule says so plainly — a temporary order normally **cannot** issue without a hearing:

> "A temporary order may not be issued without a hearing, unless the parties agree otherwise or fail to file a written objection or motion as provided in subrules (B)(5)."
> — MCR 3.207(C)(2)

An **ex parte** order skips all of that — no notice, no hearing, the other parent not in the room. Because it skips the thing our system treats as fundamental (the right to be heard before you lose something), the court may enter one **only** when the request clears a heightened standard. Here is that standard, verbatim:

> "Pending the entry of a temporary order, the court may enter an ex parte order if the court is satisfied by specific facts set forth in an affidavit or verified pleading that irreparable injury, loss, or damage will result from the delay required to effect notice, or that notice itself will precipitate adverse action before an order can be issued."
> — MCR 3.207(B)(1)

Read the operative words slowly, because each one is a filter:

- **"specific facts set forth in an affidavit or verified pleading."** Not a conclusion. Not "she's unstable" or "he's dangerous." *Specific facts*, sworn under oath — what happened, when, who, where. A paragraph of adjectives is not specific facts, and a judge reading an ex parte request has nothing but your paper to go on.
- **"irreparable injury, loss, or damage."** Harm that a later hearing could not undo. The ordinary friction of a custody dispute — a missed exchange, a disagreement about school, a bad week — is not irreparable, because the normal noticed-motion process can still fix it.
- **"will result from the delay required to effect notice."** The harm has to come from *waiting the few days it would take to notify the other parent*. If the situation can safely wait for a noticed hearing, it does not meet this prong.
- **"or that notice itself will precipitate adverse action."** The separate second path: telling the other parent first would itself trigger the harm — for example, a credible, fact-supported risk that being served would prompt them to flee with the child or destroy something. This is a real category, and it is also the one most often *claimed* without facts to back it.

**Neither prong is met by "this is really important to me" or "I don't trust them."** They are met by dated, specific, sworn facts about immediate, unfixable harm. That is the whole difference between an emergency and an ordinary dispute you feel strongly about.

### The extra pleading requirement when custody or parenting time is involved

If the ex parte order you want touches custody or parenting time — or asks to *change* custody or parenting time — the rule adds a second, mandatory layer. Your affidavit or verified pleading **must** address the established custodial environment (the "ECE" — Module 2 owns that concept). Verbatim:

> "A verified pleading or an affidavit attached to a motion or pleading that requests an ex parte custody or parenting time order or that requests a change of custody or parenting time must include the following information:
> (i) facts establishing whether the child has an established custodial environment with either parent, both parents, neither parent, or a third party; and
> (ii) either facts establishing that entry of the requested order will not change the child's established custodial environment, facts establishing that clear and convincing evidence exists that the change in the child's established custodial environment is in the child's best interest, or if the request is from a third party, facts establishing by clear and convincing evidence that it is not in the child's best interests for the parent(s) to have custody."
> — MCR 3.207(B)(1)(a)

Plain-language translation of what a pleading has to do here: **(1)** say who the child actually looks to for day-to-day care right now (that is what ECE means — see Module 2.1), and **(2)** either show your request would *not* disturb that, **or** show — by the highest civil standard, **clear and convincing evidence** — that disturbing it is in the child's best interest. A pro se motion that leaves this out is **facially deficient**: it can be denied on its face for missing a required part, before anyone weighs the merits.

*(A note the project is tracking honestly: this content requirement sits under the heading "Ex Parte Orders," yet its own text also reaches any pleading "that requests a change of custody or parenting time." Whether it governs only ex parte requests or every change request is not something this book resolves — but it does not change the safe course, which is to address ECE in the pleading either way. Cross-ref the same flag in the P1 motion-packet material.)* `⚠ VERIFY — scope of MCR 3.207(B)(1)(a)`

### If the order could alter the ECE, a fast evidentiary hearing is built in

The rule will not let an ex parte order quietly lock in a custody change. If the order *could* alter the established custodial environment, the court must set an evidentiary hearing — automatically, within a fixed window — and say so in the order:

> "The court must not issue an order that could alter a child's established custodial environment without also scheduling an evidentiary hearing under MCL 722.27 to determine whether clear and convincing evidence exists to support the order. The hearing must be held within 21 days after entry of the ex parte order and a notice of the hearing must be included in the ex parte order. An ex parte order entered under this subsection will expire upon issuance of a subsequent order following the evidentiary hearing."
> — MCR 3.207(B)(1)(b)

This is important in **both** directions. For the person *asking*: an ex parte order that shifts the child's environment is not a way to win custody without a fight — it buys you at most 21 days before you have to prove your case with real evidence, at the clear-and-convincing standard, with the other side present. For the person *served*: if your order changes where the child effectively lives, there should already be a hearing date on the face of it, within 21 days of entry (14.2 covers what to do with that date).

### After you file: service, effect, and the FOC copy

If a court does enter an ex parte order, the person who obtained it carries duties immediately:

> "The moving party must arrange for the service of a true copy of the ex parte order on the friend of the court and the other party within 3 days of the order being issued."
> — MCR 3.207(B)(2)

> "An ex parte order is effective upon entry and enforceable upon service."
> — MCR 3.207(B)(3)

So the order binds from the moment the judge signs it, and becomes enforceable once the other parent is served — which must happen within **3 days**.

### Why most ex parte requests are denied — and the credibility cost of a weak one

Judges deny ex parte custody and parenting-time requests routinely, and the reasons trace straight back to the rule:

- **The facts are conclusions, not specifics.** "Specific facts set forth in an affidavit" is a real requirement, and most denied requests fail it.
- **The harm is not irreparable.** The ordinary noticed-motion process can address most disputes, which by definition means the harm is not the kind that cannot wait.
- **The ECE part is missing or unsupported.** No facts on the custodial environment, or no clear-and-convincing showing where the order would change it.

There is a further cost that does not show up in the rule but shows up in the case. A judge who reads an ex parte request that is really an ordinary grievance dressed as an emergency remembers it. It can quietly damage how the court weighs your credibility on **best-interest factor (g)** (mental and physical health, judgment) and **factor (j)** (whether you support the child's relationship with the other parent — see Module 2.4). Manufacturing an emergency is exactly the behavior factor (j) is written to notice. **An ex parte motion is a scarce, high-trust tool. Spending it on a non-emergency can cost you more than it buys.**

## 14.2 Fighting one — the 14-day clock 🔴 ACT TODAY

You were served with an ex parte order. Three facts are true at once, and they pull in different directions, so hold all three:

1. **The order is in force right now.** It binds you today, before any judge has heard your side.
2. **You have 14 days from service to challenge it** — and if you let those days pass, the order stops being "temporary until a hearing" and becomes a temporary order by default.
3. **Challenging it does not pause it.** There is no automatic "hold" while you object. The only thing that changes the order is a *later* order from the court.

Here is the notice that the rule requires to be printed on the order itself, quoted verbatim — find it on your own document and read it against this:

> "Notice:
> 1. You may file a written objection to this order or a motion to modify or rescind this order. You must file the written objection or motion with the clerk of the court within 14 days after you were served with this order. You must serve a true copy of the objection or motion on the friend of the court and the party who obtained the order.
> 2. If you file a written objection, the friend of the court must notify the court to cancel any evidentiary hearing date specified in the ex parte order and try to resolve the dispute. If the friend of the court cannot resolve the dispute, the friend of the court must provide you with form pleadings and written instructions if you wish to bring the matter before the court without the assistance of counsel, and must reschedule the cancelled evidentiary hearing or schedule a hearing with the court.
> 3. The ex parte order will automatically become a temporary order if you do not file a written objection or motion to modify or rescind the ex parte order. If an evidentiary hearing date is specified in the ex parte order and you do not file a written objection or motion, a hearing will take place on the specified date. If an evidentiary hearing date is specified in the ex parte order and you file a motion, a hearing will take place on the specified date. Even if an objection or motion is filed, the ex parte order will remain in effect and must be obeyed unless changed by a later court order."
> — MCR 3.207(B)(6)

Notice the last sentence of item 3. The book is not adding a caution the rule does not contain; **the rule itself tells you the order stays in effect and must be obeyed even after you challenge it.** That is why "I filed an objection" is not a defense to violating the order in the meantime.

### The two ways to respond — and their different clocks

The rule gives a served parent **two response paths**, and they run on different tracks. A parent who wants to challenge the order files one or the other (a common SCAO form combines them into a single "objection **and** motion to rescind or modify," which is why they are often filed together):

**Path A — a written objection.** This routes the dispute through the Friend of the Court first:

> "If a party files a timely objection, the friend of the court must notify the court to cancel any hearing date specified in the ex parte order and attempt to resolve the dispute within 14 days of the objection being filed. If the friend of the court cannot resolve the dispute, the friend of the court must:
> (i) provide form pleadings and written instructions to the objecting party if the party wishes to proceed without the assistance of counsel, and
> (ii) reschedule a cancelled evidentiary hearing with the court, or if no evidentiary hearing has previously been scheduled, schedule a hearing to be held within 21 days of the motion being filed."
> — MCR 3.207(B)(5)(b)

For a self-represented parent, subpart (i) is worth underlining: if you object and the FOC cannot resolve the dispute, the FOC is **required** to hand you the form pleadings and written instructions to proceed **without a lawyer**, and to get you a hearing. The rule builds a pro se assist into the process.

**Path B — a motion to rescind or modify, without a separate objection.** This goes straight to a hearing before the judge:

> "If a party files a motion to rescind or modify the ex parte order without filing an objection, the court must hold an evidentiary hearing and resolve the dispute within 21 days of the motion to rescind or modify being filed or, if a hearing date is specified in the ex parte order, on the hearing date specified in the ex parte order."
> — MCR 3.207(B)(5)(a)

Either way, the outer timeline is short: an objection triggers a **14-day** FOC resolution attempt, and a hearing is set within **21 days** of the motion. The one move that forfeits all of it is the third possibility —

**Path C — do nothing.** The rule spells out what silence buys:

> "If no timely objection or motion to rescind or modify the ex parte custody, parenting time, or support order is filed, the order is a temporary order. If a hearing date is specified in the ex parte order, the evidentiary hearing will proceed on the specified hearing date."
> — MCR 3.207(B)(5)(c)

An order you never challenged becomes a temporary order that governs your case until a final judgment — and by then, undoing it is a different and harder fight (see the ECE trap below).

### How the burden works after entry — the part people get backwards

Here is the point that changes how a served parent should think about the hearing. **An ex parte order was granted on one side's sworn word, with you absent. It did not permanently shift any burden onto you to prove the order "wrong."** At the evidentiary hearing, the question the court answers is whether the *evidence* supports the order — and where the order would change the child's established custodial environment, the standard is **clear and convincing evidence**, which the rule ties directly to MCL 722.27:

> "…an evidentiary hearing under MCL 722.27 to determine whether **clear and convincing evidence** exists to support the order."
> — MCR 3.207(B)(1)(b) (emphasis added)

So if the order moved the child's day-to-day environment, the hearing is where the parent who *obtained* the order has to justify it with competent evidence, at that high standard, with you finally in the room. The evidentiary hearing is, in a real sense, **your first day** — the first time both sides are heard. Preparing for it is the referee/evidentiary-hearing work in Module 16 and the hearing-prep material; the rules of evidence apply there, and a favorable-sounding order entered without you does not carry anyone's burden at it.

> ### ⚠ THE ECE TRAP, IN THE EX PARTE VERSION — why silence is the real danger
> This is the hinge between this module and Module 2, and it is why the 14-day clock matters beyond the paperwork.
>
> Module 2.1 sets out the rule (from *Hayes v Hayes*) that an established custodial environment is a **question of fact about how the child actually lives** — and that "it makes no difference whether that environment was created by a court order, without a court order, in violation of a court order, or by a court order that was later reversed." An **ex parte "temporary" order is still a court order**, and a child living under it forms real, daily attachments in real time.
>
> Put that together with Path C above. If you are served with an ex parte order that gives the other parent primary care and you **do nothing for 14 days**, the order becomes a temporary order — and the longer the child then lives under it, the more an established custodial environment can take root **in the other parent's home**. Down the road, changing it back may require **you** to prove your case by **clear and convincing evidence** — the very burden the other side would have had to carry at a prompt hearing. **Inaction can flip the burden onto you, through nothing but the passage of time.** That is the trap. The 14-day objection is how you keep the burden where the ex parte posture put it: on the party who got the order. `⚠ TIME-CRITICAL` (cross-ref Module 2.1, Module 13.)
>
> One partial guardrail, stated honestly so you do not over-rely on it: if the order *could alter* the ECE, the court was required to set a hearing within 21 days of entry with the date on the order (14.1, MCR 3.207(B)(1)(b)). But for an ex parte order that does **not** obviously alter the ECE — or if the required hearing date is missing — the protection that actually keeps your burden low is **your timely objection**, not a hearing you are counting on the court to have scheduled.

## 14.3 Living under an ex parte order 🛑 SAFETY OVERRIDE

Between the day you are served and the day a court changes the order, you are living under it. A few things are firm:

- **The order is effective and enforceable, and stays that way until a later order.** Two subrules say it directly:
  > "An ex parte order is effective upon entry and enforceable upon service."
  > — MCR 3.207(B)(3)
  > "An ex parte order remains in effect until modified or superseded by a temporary or final order."
  > — MCR 3.207(B)(4)
- **Objecting does not suspend it.** The mandated notice (14.2, item 3) says the order "will remain in effect and must be obeyed unless changed by a later court order." There is no self-help pause button; violating the order while your challenge is pending is its own problem, separate from whether the order was fair.
- **A later change can reach back.** After the hearing, the court's new order can be made retroactive to the day the ex parte order was entered:
  > "A change that occurs after the hearing may be made retroactive to the date the ex parte order was entered."
  > — MCR 3.207(B)(5)(d)

### The conflicting-orders danger

Emergencies rarely come one at a time. It is common for an ex parte custody or parenting-time order to be moving at the same time as a **PPO**, a **CPS** matter, or a **criminal** no-contact condition — and these come from **different tracks that do not automatically know about each other**. You can end up holding two orders that point in opposite directions: one that grants parenting time, another that forbids contact. Trying to comply with both by guessing is how people violate one while obeying the other.

This is a **stop point**, not a puzzle to solve alone. A no-contact or safety order generally takes precedence over a custody or parenting-time order until the conflict is sorted out by the court — and sorting it out is work for a lawyer and, where DV is present, a domestic-violence advocate. Module 17 owns the safety and criminal firewalls; this module hands you off to it the moment a second, safety-based order is in the picture. `SAFETY OVERRIDE` (cross-ref Module 17, FM.2.)

## Traps

Three specific ways this posture hurts people, pulled together:

1. **The harmful emergency motion.** Using an ex parte request as a pressure move in an ordinary dispute. It usually fails the "specific facts / irreparable injury" test (14.1), and it can cost you credibility on factors (g) and (j) that you will need for the rest of the case. The tool is scarce for a reason.
2. **Assuming the objection stays the order.** It does not. The rule says the order "must be obeyed unless changed by a later court order" (MCR 3.207(B)(6)). Filing your challenge and then acting as if the order is suspended is a distinct mistake with its own consequences.
3. **Conflicting orders.** Treating an ex parte custody order and a PPO or no-contact order as if they can simply be stacked. They can contradict, a safety order generally wins, and the reconciliation is not a do-it-yourself step (14.3, Module 17).

A fourth, quieter than the others but the most common loss: **letting the 14 days pass.** It is not dramatic and it does not feel like a decision, but it is the one the court will honor (14.2, Path C).

- **The emergency involves domestic violence, a PPO, CPS, or the police.** Safety and criminal firewalls come first — **Module 17**, then counsel. An ex parte custody motion is not the front-line tool here. 🛑 `SAFETY OVERRIDE`
- **The request — yours or the one served on you — asks to remove a child** from a parent's care, or from the state. 🛑 `DO NOT USE THIS BOOK ALONE`
- **The request asks to suspend parenting time** entirely. The stakes and the standards here are heavy enough that going it alone is a mistake this book will not pretend is safe.
- **There is a criminal or CPS matter running alongside** the custody question — anything you file or swear to can land in that other case (Module 17). 🛑 `DO NOT USE THIS BOOK ALONE`
- **Two orders conflict** — an ex parte order and a PPO or no-contact order that cannot both be obeyed (14.3).

And the standing one: if you are **afraid**, that is not a motion-timing question. **911** for danger now; the DV hotline (**1-800-799-7233**) otherwise. Safety outranks every deadline on this page.
