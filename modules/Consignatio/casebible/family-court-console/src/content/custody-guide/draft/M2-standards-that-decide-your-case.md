# Why this module comes first

Most people walk into family court thinking the question is *"who is the better parent?"* It is not — at least not first. Before a judge ever weighs who should have the child, the judge has to answer two narrower questions that decide **how hard your case is**:

1. **Does the child already have an "established custodial environment," and with whom?** That answer sets your **burden of proof** — the amount of evidence you need.
2. **If there is already an order, have you cleared the "gate"?** A judge will not even re-open a final custody order until you show *proper cause or a change of circumstances*.

Get these two wrong and it will not matter how good your evidence is — you will be held to a burden you did not know you had, or turned away at the gate before anyone hears you. This module is the framework. Everything else in the book is built on it.

## 2.1 The established custodial environment (ECE)

The single most important sentence in Michigan custody law is the one that defines the **established custodial environment**. Here it is, quoted from the statute as the Court of Appeals states it:

> "The court shall not modify or amend its previous judgments or orders or issue a new order so as to change the established custodial environment of a child unless there is presented clear and convincing evidence that it is in the best interest of the child. The custodial environment of a child is established if over an appreciable time the child naturally looks to the custodian in that environment for guidance, discipline, the necessities of life, and parental comfort. The age of the child, the physical environment, and the inclination of the custodian and the child as to permanency of the relationship shall also be considered."
> — MCL 722.27(1)(c), as quoted in *Ludema v Ludema* (Mich Ct App 2003)

Read it twice. Three things follow from it, and they surprise almost everyone:

- **An ECE can exist in one home, in both homes, or in neither.** It is not a title. It is a description of where the child actually looks for comfort and daily care.
- **It is a question of *fact about how the child lives*, not about what an order says.** An order can say one thing while the child's real life says another. The facts win.
- **If a change you ask for would move the child *out of* an established environment, you must prove your case by clear and convincing evidence** — the highest burden in civil law.

> ### ⚠ BOXED WARNING — THE "JUST FOR NOW" ORDER TRAP
> **This is the highest-value warning in the entire book. Read it before you agree to any temporary order.**
>
> An established custodial environment is a question of fact, and — in the words courts use — *it makes no difference whether that environment was created by a court order, without a court order, in violation of a court order, or by a court order that was later reversed.* (*Hayes v Hayes*, 209 Mich App 385 (1995). ⚠ *citation verified; the exact holding language is confirmation-pending in our ledger — confirm the quote before you put it in a filing.*)
>
> **What this means for you:** if you agree to a temporary order that gives the other parent primary care *"just for now,"* and the case then takes fourteen months to resolve, the child may have formed an established custodial environment **in that other home** — and now *you* have to overcome it **by clear and convincing evidence**. Your own signature helped build the wall you now have to climb.
>
> Michigan judicial-training materials say the same thing: a temporary order does **not automatically** create an established environment — but sometimes it does, precisely because the child settles in while the case drags. **Never sign a "just for now" order without understanding this section.** `⚠ TIME-CRITICAL` (cross-referenced from FM.0, Module 12, Module 13, Module 14, Module 17.)

## 2.2 The two burdens, and why the order matters

There are two burdens of proof in a custody case, and **which one applies to you is decided by the ECE question above** — *before* the judge reaches the best-interest factors.

| If… | Your burden is… | In plain terms |
|---|---|---|
| There is **no** established custodial environment, **or** the ECE is with **you** (the parent asking for the change) | **Preponderance of the evidence** | "More likely than not" — just over 50%. |
| The ECE is with the **other** parent (or with both), and your request would **change** it | **Clear and convincing evidence** | A demanding standard — well above "more likely than not," below "beyond a reasonable doubt." |

Two consequences:

- **The court must decide the ECE question first.** A judge who jumps to "best interests" without first deciding the custodial environment has skipped a required step — and applied the wrong burden. (This is why the ECE analysis, not the factors, is the true starting point.)
- **The burden allocation is a rule, not a mood.** Where a proposed change would alter the established environment, the change requires **clear and convincing evidence**; where it would not, the lesser **preponderance** standard governs, and the burden sits on the parent proposing the change. (*Pierron v Pierron*, 486 Mich 81 (2010) — Michigan Supreme Court, binding.)

**One myth worth killing now:** working outside the home does not, by itself, cost you the custodial environment. A parent who works to support the family should **not** have that held against them in the ECE analysis. (*Bofysil v Bofysil*, 332 Mich App 232 (2020).) If the other side runs an "absentee working parent" narrative, this is your answer.

## 2.3 The gatekeeper: proper cause or change of circumstances

If there is **already a final custody order** and you want it changed, there is a gate you must clear before the court will look at the merits at all:

> The court may modify a custody order only for **proper cause shown or because of a change of circumstances.**
> — MCL 722.27(1)(c)

This is where **most pro se modification motions die** — not on the facts, but at the threshold, because the moving parent never showed a qualifying change.

- **For a change of *custody***, the leading case is *Vodvarka v Grasmeyer*, 259 Mich App 499 (2003). *Vodvarka* limits what counts: in deciding whether there is a **change of circumstances**, the court looks only at events that have happened **since the last custody order was entered** — and "normal life changes" (a new job, a child getting older, ordinary ups and downs) generally are **not** enough. "Proper cause" means a fact of the kind that has, or could have, a significant effect on the child's well-being.
- **For a *parenting-time-only* change** that would **not** alter the established custodial environment, the threshold is **lower**. *Shade v Wright*, 291 Mich App 17 (2010), holds that the demanding *Vodvarka* standard does not apply to an ordinary parenting-time adjustment — the very "normal life changes" that *Vodvarka* says are not enough to change custody **can** be enough to modify parenting time.

> ### ⚠ The line that decides which test you get
> *Shade* draws the line at the **established custodial environment.** A parenting-time request that would **change** the ECE is treated as a custody matter and gets the full *Vodvarka* threshold and the clear-and-convincing burden. A parenting-time request that would **not** change the ECE gets the lower *Shade* threshold. **You cannot lower your burden just by calling a custody change a "parenting-time" change** — the court looks at what the relief would actually do to the child's environment, not at the label on your motion. (This is exactly why *how much* you ask for matters — see 2.7.)

## 2.4 The twelve best-interest factors

Once the ECE and threshold questions are settled, the merits are decided by the **twelve best-interest factors**. The judge must **consider and make findings on every one** — but need **not** give them equal weight (*Fletcher v Fletcher*, 447 Mich 871 (1994)), and custody is decided on the **sum total** of the factors, not by counting which side "won" more of them (*Baker v Baker*, 411 Mich 567 (1981); custody should not be changed "except in the most compelling cases").

Here is the statute, verbatim, with what each factor means, what tends to prove it, and where a high-conflict opponent will attack:

> As used in this act, "best interests of the child" means the sum total of the following factors to be considered, evaluated, and determined by the court:

**(a) The love, affection, and other emotional ties existing between the parties involved and the child.**
*Proven by:* consistent daily involvement, routines, the child's comfort with you. *Attacked by:* claims of coldness or absence — answered with documented time and involvement, not adjectives.

**(b) The capacity and disposition … to give the child love, affection, and guidance and to continue the education and raising of the child in his or her religion or creed, if any.**
*Proven by:* homework, school involvement, religious or moral upbringing you actually provide. *Attacked by:* "uninvolved parent" narratives — answered with records (school contact logs, attendance at events).

**(c) The capacity and disposition … to provide the child with food, clothing, medical care … and other material needs.**
*Proven by:* you meeting daily needs; medical appointments kept. *Note:* this is about *capacity and disposition*, not who earns more — do not let it become a wealth contest.

**(d) The length of time the child has lived in a stable, satisfactory environment, and the desirability of maintaining continuity.**
*This factor overlaps heavily with the ECE analysis (2.1).* Stability and continuity cut toward whoever has provided the child's steady daily life.

**(e) The permanence, as a family unit, of the existing or proposed custodial home or homes.**
*About the stability of the home as a unit* — not its size or income. Frequent moves and unstable household composition cut against a parent here.

**(f) The moral fitness of the parties involved.**
*Narrower than it sounds:* Michigan courts limit this to conduct that affects a parent's **functioning as a parent**, not general moral judgments. Keep it tied to parenting impact.

**(g) The mental and physical health of the parties involved.**
> ⚠ **This factor is your own exposure.** It cuts both ways. Your medical or mental-health history can be raised here. Reasonable treatment is not a disqualifier, but this is a place to be prepared, not surprised. (Cross-ref Module 11 — your own conduct audit.)

**(h) The home, school, and community record of the child.**
*Proven by:* report cards, attendance, activities, community ties — the child's actual record, documented.

**(i) The reasonable preference of the child, if the court considers the child to be of sufficient age to express preference.**
*The judge interviews the child privately;* you do **not** coach, question, or relay the child's views. **The child is never your witness, courier, or evidence-gatherer** — see the safety rule in Module 8 and the guardrails on the child.

**(j) The willingness and ability of each of the parties to facilitate and encourage a close and continuing parent-child relationship between the child and the other parent … A court may not consider negatively … any reasonable action taken by a parent to protect a child or that parent from sexual assault or domestic violence by the child's other parent.**
> This is the **facilitation** factor — the statutory home for what people call "alienation." You prove a problem here with **dated, documented denials and interference**, mapped to this factor — not with the word "alienation," which Michigan does not recognize as a freestanding claim. **The second sentence is a shield:** reasonable protective action against DV or sexual assault **cannot** be counted against you here. (Cross-ref Module 18.)

**(k) Domestic violence, regardless of whether the violence was directed against or witnessed by the child.**
*Mandatory* — the court must weigh domestic violence whether or not the child saw it. (Cross-ref Module 17, and the safety override FM.2.)

**(l) Any other factor considered by the court to be relevant to a particular child custody dispute.**
*The catch-all* — where anything genuinely relevant that the other factors miss can come in.

> **The strategic point for a high-conflict case:** you do not win these factors with labels ("narcissist," "abuser," "alienator"). You win them by mapping **dated conduct** to the specific factor it proves, with evidence a court can actually admit. That mapping is what the **Best-Interest Evidence Matrix** (below) is for.

## 2.5 The parenting-time factors — a *separate* list most people miss

Custody is not the only thing with a factor list. **Parenting time has its own set of factors**, at MCL 722.27a(7)(a)–(i), governing the *frequency, duration, and type* of parenting time. They are easy to miss because they sit in a different subsection — and two of them are the **statutory hooks for a parent who is being denied time**.

The starting point is the presumption:

> A child has a right to parenting time with a parent unless it is shown on the record by **clear and convincing evidence** that it would endanger the child's physical, mental, or emotional health. — MCL 722.27a(3)

The parenting-time factors then include *(substance confirmed against the outline and the SCAO Establishing Parenting Time checklist; ⚠ confirm the exact verbatim wording and lettering of the current subsection (7) before quoting in a filing — this list was renumbered from a former subsection (6))*:

- special needs of the child;
- whether the child is a **nursing infant**;
- the likelihood of **abuse or neglect of the child** during parenting time;
- the likelihood of **abuse of a parent** resulting from the exercise of parenting time;
- the **travel time and burden** for the child;
- **(f) whether a parent can reasonably be expected to comply** with the parenting-time terms;
- **(g) whether a parent has frequently failed to exercise** reasonable parenting time;
- the threatened or actual **detention or concealment** of the child — with the express carve-out that a custodial parent's temporary residence with the child **in a domestic-violence shelter is not evidence of an intent to retain or conceal**;
- any other relevant factor.

> **Factors (f) and (g) are the denied parent's hooks.** If the other parent has *frequently failed to make your time happen*, or *cannot reasonably be expected to comply* with a vague "as agreed" arrangement, those are not just grievances — they are **statutory parenting-time factors** that support asking the court for **specific, enforceable terms** instead of "as the parties agree." (This is the legal spine of the specific-parenting-time motion — cross-ref Module 12 and the P1 packet material.)

## 2.6 Joint vs. sole; legal vs. physical

Two different axes, often confused:

- **Legal custody** = decision-making authority (school, medical, religion). **Physical custody** = where the child lives day to day. You can have joint legal + primary physical, or any other combination.
- **Joint custody** must be **considered by the court in every case on a parent's request**, and the court must **state its reasons** for granting or denying it. (MCL 722.26a. ⚠ *confirm the exact current subsection text before quoting.*)

> **The cooperation catch — and it can cut against you.** Joint *legal* custody depends on the parents' ability to **cooperate and generally agree** on important decisions for the child. Where they cannot, a court may decline joint legal custody. (*Foskett v Foskett*, 247 Mich App 1 (2001).) In a high-conflict case this is double-edged: the same "they can't cooperate" argument you might make against the other parent can be turned around on **you**. Asking for sole custody *merely because there is conflict* can look like you are the source of it. (See 2.7.)

## 2.7 Framing the relief precisely — the mistake that raises your own burden

Custody and parenting time are **different requests with different standards**. The most common self-inflicted wound in a pro se motion is asking for too much, too broadly, and thereby:

- **turning a winnable parenting-time motion into a custody fight** — because the relief you asked for would alter the ECE, which (per *Shade*, 2.3) snaps the *Vodvarka* threshold and the clear-and-convincing burden back into place; and
- **exposing yourself under factor (j)** — because demanding sole custody *because there is conflict* can read as manufacturing conflict rather than facilitating the child's relationship with the other parent.

> **The disciplined ask:** request the **narrowest relief that solves your actual problem**, in terms a court can place on a calendar and enforce. If your problem is a vague "as the parties agree" order, the precise ask is **specific parenting-time terms** — not "give me custody." Grow the arrangement later, from a clean record, rather than over-reaching now and inheriting the higher burden. (The mechanics of that motion are Module 12; the sizing strategy is in the P1 packet material.)

## Templates and tools

**1. ECE / burden decision tree.** Walk it before every custody or parenting-time motion:
*Is there an established custodial environment? → With whom (you / the other parent / both / neither)? → Would my requested relief change it? → If yes → clear and convincing; if no → preponderance.* Write the answer down before you draft.

**2. The Best-Interest Evidence Matrix (the master template — used throughout the book).**

| Factor (a–l) | My evidence (dated facts) | Their likely evidence | My witness | My exhibit | **Admissibility route** (how it gets in — Modules 5–7) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| (a) emotional ties | | | | | |
| … | | | | | |
| (l) other | | | | | |

*You are done with this matrix when every factor has at least one dated fact in column 1 with an admissibility route in the last column — a fact you cannot get admitted is not yet evidence.* Build it once here; you will reuse it in Modules 8, 16, 21, and 22.

**3. Custody-vs.-parenting-time relief worksheet.** State your problem in one sentence, then the narrowest relief that fixes it, then check: *would this relief change the ECE?* If yes, you are in custody territory and the higher burden applies.

**4. Proper-cause / change-of-circumstances worksheet.** List every fact that has occurred **since the last order**. Cross out ordinary "normal life changes." What remains is your threshold showing — if nothing remains, you are not past the gate yet.

**5. Verbatim-quote cards.** The ECE clause (2.1), the twelve factors (2.4), and the parenting-time presumption (2.5), printed for quoting exactly in filings — never paraphrase an operative standard.
