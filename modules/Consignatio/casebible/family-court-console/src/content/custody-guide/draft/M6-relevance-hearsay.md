# Why this module exists

Two ideas keep more evidence out of a custody hearing than any other, and a self-represented parent who understands only these two is already ahead of most:

- **Relevance.** A judge only receives evidence that helps decide a fact that actually matters in *this* case. "Everything bad she ever did" is not a category the rules recognize.
- **Hearsay.** A judge is wary of secondhand statements — what someone said *out of court*, offered to prove it was true. Most of what a stressed parent wants to hand up ("her sister told me," "the babysitter said," "my daughter told me Mom said…") is hearsay, and hearsay is presumed **out** unless a specific door opens.

The rest of this module is the small set of doors, and one rule that is worth more to a parent than all of them combined: **anything the other parent said, wrote, or posted can usually come in against them.**

A note on scope. Michigan restyled its Rules of Evidence effective **January 1, 2024**, so older articles, forms, and web pages may quote superseded numbering. The text quoted below is from the current rules. This module does not cover all of Article VIII — only the handful of rules a custody hearing actually turns on. Two neighboring modules carry the pieces this one leaves out: **Module 5** decides *where* these rules even apply (they apply at the referee hearing and at trial; they do **not** apply to the judge's private interview of the child or to the Friend of the Court report), and **Module 7** covers **authentication** — proving a text, screenshot, or record is what you say it is. Authentication and hearsay are **different questions**, and clearing one does nothing for the other.

## 6.1 Relevance, and the judge's patience (MRE 401, 402, 403)

**Relevance** is the first filter. The test is broad but not empty:

> Evidence is relevant if:
> (a) it has any tendency to make a fact more or less probable than it would be without the evidence; and
> (b) the fact is of consequence in determining the action.
> — MRE 401

Relevant evidence usually comes in; irrelevant evidence never does:

> Relevant evidence is admissible unless any of the following provides otherwise: the United States Constitution; the Michigan Constitution; these rules; or other rules prescribed by the Supreme Court. Irrelevant evidence is not admissible.
> — MRE 402

The "fact of consequence" in a custody case is fixed by **Module 2** — the established custodial environment, the threshold (proper cause or change of circumstances), the twelve best-interest factors, and the parenting-time factors. Evidence earns its place by moving one of those facts. A ten-year-old grievance that maps to no current factor is not "bad evidence"; it is *irrelevant*, and a judge is entitled to ignore it.

Even relevant evidence can be excluded when its cost outweighs its worth:

> The court may exclude relevant evidence if its probative value is substantially outweighed by a danger of one or more of the following: unfair prejudice, confusing the issues, misleading the jury, undue delay, wasting time, or needlessly presenting cumulative evidence.
> — MRE 403

For a pro se parent the operative words are **undue delay, wasting time,** and **needlessly presenting cumulative evidence**. A binder of two hundred screenshots proving the same point twenty times does not make the point twenty times stronger — it invites a 403 objection, and it makes the person holding the binder look consumed rather than credible. The disciplined version is a short stack of dated exhibits, each tied to a specific factor.

> ### ⚠ This filter points at you too
> Relevance and 403 are neutral tools. The same rules that keep the other parent's noise out will keep *your* noise out — and your own conduct is relevant when it bears on a factor. The lesson is not "hide things"; it is "**offer less, and offer it tied to a factor.**" Over-documenting every slight is not thoroughness. It is a 403 problem and, in a high-conflict case, it can read as the very fixation the other side wants the court to see.

## 6.2 You can only speak to what you actually know (MRE 602)

A witness — including you — testifies from **personal knowledge**, not from what they assume, heard, or concluded:

> A witness may testify to a matter only if evidence is introduced sufficient to support a finding that the witness has personal knowledge of the matter. Evidence to prove personal knowledge may consist of the witness's own testimony. This rule does not apply to a witness's expert testimony under Rule 703.
> — MRE 602

This is where the difference between a **fact** and an **inference** matters. "She was drunk at the exchange" is a conclusion. "She slurred her words, her breath smelled of alcohol, and she stepped off the curb sideways" is what you *perceived* — and it is far harder to keep out. A lay witness may offer an opinion, but only a narrow kind:

> If a witness is not testifying as an expert, testimony in the form of an opinion is limited to one that is: (a) rationally based on the witness's perception; and (b) helpful to clearly understanding the witness's testimony or to determining a fact in issue.
> — MRE 701

Diagnoses ("she's a narcissist"), and anything that depends on training you do not have, are not lay opinion. The banned-vocabulary rule in this book and the symmetry rule both point the same way: describe the **conduct you saw**, and let the court name the pattern.

## 6.3 Hearsay in ninety seconds (MRE 801–802)

Three short definitions do all the work:

> (a) Statement. "Statement" means a person's oral assertion, written assertion, or nonverbal conduct if the person intended it as an assertion.
> (b) Declarant. "Declarant" means the person who made the statement.
> (c) Hearsay. "Hearsay" means a statement that: (1) the declarant does not make while testifying at the current trial or hearing; and (2) a party offers in evidence to prove the truth of the matter asserted in the statement.
> — MRE 801(a)–(c)

> Hearsay is not admissible unless these rules provide otherwise.
> — MRE 802

So **hearsay** is an *out-of-court* statement offered *for its truth*. Both halves have to be true for something to be hearsay. The Evidence Benchbook frames the analysis as four questions, and they are worth memorizing as a screen (MJI *Evidence Benchbook* §5.1):

**The four-question hearsay screen**
1. **Is it a "statement"** — a person's assertion, in words or in conduct meant as an assertion? (MRE 801(a))
2. **Was it made out of court** — by someone *other than* the witness testifying right now? (MRE 801(c)(1))
3. **Is it offered to prove the truth of what it asserts?** (MRE 801(c)(2))
4. **If yes to all three,** does it still come in because it is *defined as not hearsay* (MRE 801(d)) or fits an *exception* (MRE 803, 803A, or 804)?

If the answer to question 3 is **no**, the statement is **not hearsay at all**, and you never reach the exceptions. That is the most under-used escape hatch a parent has: a statement offered **not for its truth** but for its **effect on the listener** or simply that it *was said*. "Mom told him he doesn't have to listen to me" offered to prove the child *heard* it and became defiant — not to prove the statement was accurate — is not hearsay. (The line is narrow, and a judge may still limit how the statement is used; see the child-hearsay caution in 6.6.)

Everything else — where the statement *is* offered for its truth — has to find a door in the next three sections.

## 6.4 The rule that wins cases: an opposing party's statement (MRE 801(d)(2))

This is the single most useful rule for a parent. A statement by the **other party**, offered **against** them, is defined as **not hearsay**:

> (2) An Opposing Party's Statement. The statement is offered against an opposing party and:
> (A) was made by the party in an individual or representative capacity, except a statement made in connection with: (i) a guilty plea to a misdemeanor motor-vehicle violation; or (ii) an admission of responsibility for a civil infraction under a motor-vehicle law;
> (B) is one the party manifested that it adopted or believed to be true;
> (C) was made by a person whom the party authorized to make a statement on the subject;
> (D) was made by the party's agent or employee on a matter within the scope of that relationship and while it existed; or
> (E) was made by the party's coconspirator during and in furtherance of the conspiracy, if there is independent proof of the conspiracy.
> — MRE 801(d)(2)

In plain terms, under subrule (A): **anything the other parent themselves said, texted, emailed, or posted comes in against them without a hearsay problem.** The Michigan Supreme Court put the reason memorably:

> "[T]he admissibility of [an opposing party's] statement springs from a sense of fundamental fairness captured in the phrase, 'You said it; you're stuck with it.'"
> — *Shields v Reddo*, 432 Mich 761, 775 (1989), as quoted in MJI *Evidence Benchbook* §5.2

**Worked examples (all clear the hearsay bar under 801(d)(2)(A)):**
- A text from the other parent: *"I'm keeping him this weekend, court order or not."* Offered by you, against them — not hearsay.
- An email: *"I told the school not to call you."* — not hearsay against them.
- A public Facebook post admitting a missed exchange or an insult about you — not hearsay against them.
- A voicemail in the other parent's voice canceling your time — not hearsay against them (you still have to **authenticate** the voice and the recording under Module 7, and you must **never** create a recording that raises a consent problem — recording questions are a hard stop, see 6.8).

> ### ⚠ The same door swings both ways — this is the most important warning in the module
> MRE 801(d)(2) has **no** requirement that the speaker be dishonest, only that they be a *party*. That means **your own** texts, emails, and posts are equally not-hearsay when the **other side** offers them **against you**. Every angry message you have ever sent is a potential exhibit in their hands. Before you rely on this rule, assume the other parent is holding the mirror image of your evidence, and write — today and going forward — as if a judge will read it. (Communication discipline and preservation are Module 8.)

Two smaller points. Subrules (B)–(E) reach statements the party *adopted* or that an authorized agent made — narrower, and worth counsel if you are leaning on them. And a party's own statement is still subject to **relevance** (6.1): "you said it" only helps if what they said moves a factor.

## 6.5 The exceptions you will actually use (MRE 803)

When a statement *is* offered for its truth and the speaker is **not** the opposing party, you need an **exception**. MRE 803 collects the exceptions that apply **whether or not the person who spoke is available to testify**:

> The following are not excluded by the rule against hearsay, regardless of whether the declarant is available as a witness:
> — MRE 803 (opening clause)

Below are the ones that actually recur in family cases. Each is quoted, then translated.

### 803(1) — Present sense impression
> (1) Present Sense Impression. A statement describing or explaining an event or condition made while or immediately after the declarant perceived it.
> — MRE 803(1)

A statement made *as it happens or right after* — a text sent from the exchange saying *"he didn't show, it's 6:10"* — carries built-in reliability because there was no time to invent it. The benchbook notes Michigan courts require the statement to describe the event, come from someone who personally perceived it, and be "substantially contemporaneous," with independent corroboration of the event (MJI *Evidence Benchbook* §5.3, citing *People v Hendrickson*, 459 Mich 229 (1998) — a 911 call made while the assailant was still leaving the house).

### 803(2) — Excited utterance
> (2) Excited Utterance. A statement relating to a startling event or condition, made while the declarant was under the stress of excitement that it caused.
> — MRE 803(2)

A statement blurted out under the stress of a startling event — often a 911 call or a statement to a first responder in a domestic-violence incident. The focus is "the lack of capacity to fabricate, not the lack of time" (MJI *Evidence Benchbook* §5.3, citing *People v Smith*, 456 Mich 543 (1998)). 🛑 If the startling event is violence or an abuse disclosure, this is safety-and-counsel territory, not a documentation project — see 6.8.

### 803(3) — Then-existing state of mind
> (3) Then-Existing Mental, Emotional, or Physical Condition. A statement of the declarant's then-existing state of mind or emotional, sensory, or physical condition (such as intent, plan, motive, design, mental feeling, pain, or bodily health), but not including a statement of memory or belief to prove the fact remembered or believed unless it relates to the validity or terms of declarant's will.
> — MRE 803(3)

This exception covers what a person *feels or intends in the moment* — "I'm scared," "I'm not bringing her back" — but **not** their memory of a past event offered to prove that event happened. The distinction is exact and easy to trip on: *"I'm afraid of him"* (a present feeling) can come in; *"he hit me last week"* (a memory offered to prove the hitting) cannot come in under 803(3).

In a family-court illustration, the Court of Appeals allowed a child's statements to a therapist and a guardianship investigator about the **fear** the child felt toward a parent, as evidence of the child's then-existing emotional condition (MJI *Evidence Benchbook* §5.3, citing *In re Utrera*, 281 Mich App 1 (2008)). Note *how* those statements reached the court: **through the therapist and the investigator**, professionals who heard them in their own work — not through a parent quoting the child. That routing is not optional (see 6.6).

### 803(4) — Statements for medical treatment or diagnosis
> (4) Statement Made for Purposes of Medical Treatment or Diagnosis in Connection with Treatment. A statement that: (A) is made for—and is reasonably necessary to—medical treatment or diagnosis in connection with treatment; and (B) describes medical history, past or present symptoms or sensations, their inception, or their general cause.
> — MRE 803(4)

What a patient tells a doctor or nurse to get treated is trusted because people have reason to be truthful with their own clinicians. This is how an injury, a symptom, or its general cause can enter through a medical record or a treating provider. When the patient is a **child**, Michigan courts scrutinize trustworthiness closely — the benchbook lists factors a court weighs, including the child's age, whether the questions were leading, who initiated the exam, and the child's motive (MJI *Evidence Benchbook* §5.3, citing *People v Meeboer (After Remand)*, 439 Mich 310 (1992)). Statements *identifying* an assailant are generally **not** covered, because identity is not the "general cause" of an injury — with a limited exception courts have recognized for a child's abuser where identity bears on treatment and safety (MJI *Evidence Benchbook* §5.3). The practical point: this door opens **through the medical provider and the record**, not through you relaying what the child told you.

### 803(5) — Recorded recollection
> (5) Recorded Recollection. A record that: (A) is on a matter the witness once knew about but now cannot recall well enough to testify fully and accurately; (B) was made or adopted by the witness when the matter was fresh in the witness's memory; and (C) accurately reflects the witness's knowledge. If admitted, the record may be read into evidence but may be received as an exhibit only if offered by an adverse party.
> — MRE 803(5)

This is the door for a note written *close in time* by a witness who, on the stand, no longer remembers the details. Note the unusual limit built into the rule: the proponent may **read it aloud** but cannot hand the document in as an exhibit — only the *opposing* party can do that.

### 803(6) — Records of a regularly conducted activity ("business records")
> (6) Record of a Regularly Conducted Activity. A record of an act, transaction, occurrence, event, condition, opinion, or diagnosis if: (A) the record was made at or near the time by—or from information transmitted by—someone with knowledge; (B) the record was kept in the course of a regularly conducted activity of a business, organization, occupation, or calling, whether or not for profit; (C) making the record was a regular practice of that activity; (D) all these conditions are shown by the testimony of the custodian or another qualified witness, or by a certification that complies with a rule prescribed by the Supreme Court or with a statute permitting certification; and (E) the opponent does not show that the source of information or the method or circumstances of preparation indicate a lack of trustworthiness.
> — MRE 803(6)

This is the **business record** exception, and it is the workhorse for school records, medical records, and therapy records. Three things a parent should hold onto:
- **It needs a sponsor.** The record comes in through the **custodian or another qualified witness**, or a qualifying **certification** — not by you carrying a printout to the podium. Lining up that custodian or certification is preparation that happens *weeks* ahead (Module 7 covers the certification route; the hearing-prep module covers subpoenaing a records custodian).
- **Trustworthiness is an express condition (subrule (E)).** A record prepared *in anticipation of litigation*, or by someone with a motive to shade it, can be refused even when the paperwork looks perfect (MJI *Evidence Benchbook* §5.3, citing *Solomon v Shuell*, 435 Mich 104 (1990), and *People v McDaniel*, 469 Mich 409 (2003)).
- **Being a record does not admit everything inside it** — see hearsay within hearsay, 6.7.

### 803(8) — Public records (police reports, agency records) — with a caveat
> (8) Public Record. A record or statement of a public office if it sets out: (A) the office's activities; or (B) a matter observed while under a legal duty to report, but not including: (i) in a criminal case, a matter observed by law-enforcement personnel; and (ii) information to which the limitations in MCL 257.624 apply.
> — MRE 803(8)

Public records — including some police and agency records — have their own door. Two caveats a parent will hit:
- The **law-enforcement caveat** in subrule (B)(i) is written for **criminal cases**. A custody case is civil, so (B)(i) is not itself the bar; the real obstacles in a civil hearing are the **trustworthiness** limit (a report generated in an adversarial setting may be excluded — *Solomon*, *McDaniel*, above) and **hearsay within hearsay**.
- A police report is full of *other people's statements*. The report may clear 803(8), but each quoted statement inside it needs **its own** door (6.7). CPS and evaluator records raise the same layered problem — and, separately, may be **confidential**; how they are obtained and whether filing them publicly is wise is a privilege-and-safety question, not a hearsay question (6.8, and Module 17).

### MRE 612 — refreshing recollection (a technique, not an exception)
When a witness's memory has simply gone blank, a document can be shown to jog it. Under MRE 612 the adverse party then has the right to see that document, inspect it, and cross-examine on it. The benchbook describes the foundation as showing that the witness's present memory is inadequate, that the writing could refresh it, and that it *actually does* (MJI *Evidence Benchbook* §3.11). The key difference from 803(5): if the memory **comes back**, the witness testifies from memory and the paper is just a prompt; if it **does not**, you are back to the recorded-recollection door above. Refreshing recollection is **not** a way to read a document into evidence around the hearsay rule.

### The narrower doors, in one breath
- **MRE 804** collects exceptions that apply **only when the declarant is *unavailable*** — for example, a declarant who has died, is privileged, refuses despite a court order, or cannot be located after reasonable effort. Its list (former testimony, statement against interest, and others) is narrower and less common in custody work; most family hearsay runs through 801(d)(2) or 803. Unavailability is defined by rule:
  > (a) Criteria for Being Unavailable. A declarant is considered to be unavailable as a witness if the declarant: (1) is exempted from testifying about the subject matter of the declarant's statement because the court rules that a privilege applies; (2) refuses to testify about the subject matter despite a court order to do so; (3) testifies to not remembering the subject matter; (4) cannot be present or testify at the trial or hearing because of death or a then-existing infirmity, physical illness, or mental illness; or (5) is absent from the trial or hearing [and the proponent cannot procure attendance or testimony by reasonable means].
  > — MRE 804(a)
- **MRE 807** is a narrow **residual** backstop for a trustworthy statement that fits no other exception; it requires advance **notice** to the other side and is rarely a plan you build on:
  > (a) In General. Under the following conditions, a hearsay statement is not excluded by the rule against hearsay even if the statement is not admissible under a hearsay exception in Rule 803 or 804: (1) the statement has equivalent circumstantial guarantees of trustworthiness; (2) it is offered as evidence of a material fact; (3) it is more probative on the point for which it is offered than any other evidence that the proponent can obtain through reasonable efforts; and (4) admitting it will serve the purposes of these rules and the interests of justice.
  > — MRE 807(a)

## 6.6 Hearsay within hearsay — the records trap (MRE 805)

The most common mistake with records is assuming that admitting the *record* admits every *statement inside* it. It does not:

> Hearsay within hearsay is not excluded by the rule against hearsay if each part of the combined statements conforms with an exception to the rule.
> — MRE 805

Read that as: **each layer needs its own door.** A therapy note (outer layer: business record, 803(6)) may quote the other parent (inner layer: opposing-party statement, 801(d)(2) — a door) and also quote the child (inner layer: usually **no** door in a custody case — see 6.7). The note can be admitted while the *child's* quoted words inside it are still excluded. The same is true of school records, medical records, police reports, CPS files, GAL and evaluator reports: the container clearing a door tells you nothing about the statements packed inside.

> ### ⚠ This is where over-broad records hurt you
> When you offer a thick record because one line in it helps, you also hand over every harmful line the other side can now read back — including *your own* statements captured in it, which are not hearsay against you (6.4). Offer the **narrowest** record that carries the fact you need, and be ready to name the door for each layer you rely on.

## 6.7 The child-hearsay problem — the exception you will look up and it will not save you

> ### 🔴 MRE 803A DOES NOT APPLY IN CUSTODY CASES
> The "tender-years" exception, MRE 803A, is the rule a parent finds online and assumes will let the court hear *"my child told me Mom said…"* It will not. The rule says so in its first line:
>
> > (a) Scope. This rule applies in criminal and delinquency proceedings only.
> > — MRE 803A(a)
>
> A custody or parenting-time hearing is **neither** a criminal nor a delinquency proceeding. MRE 803A is unavailable here. A theory of proof that depends on the reader repeating the child's out-of-court statements is a theory built on a door that is closed.

**The guardrail that sits on top of this.** Beyond the hearsay problem, this book has an absolute rule: **the child is never your source.** No exercise, script, or strategy in this guide asks you to interview, debrief, coach, task, record, or emotionally lean on your child, and none asks you to relay the child's statements as your evidence. That rule is not a tactic; it protects the child and it protects your credibility. 🛑 **A child's disclosure of abuse is a hard stop** — it is a `DO NOT USE THIS GUIDE ALONE` situation that goes to counsel, and to the professionals and authorities equipped for it, not into an exhibit binder (Module 17).

**The legitimate routes a child's experience *can* reach the court**, none of which run through you quoting the child:
1. **Not for its truth.** A statement offered only for its **effect on the listener** or that it *was said* is not hearsay at all (6.3) — a limited, carefully-bounded use.
2. **Then-existing state of mind (803(3)).** The child's *present* feeling — fear, reluctance — offered through the professional who heard it, as in *In re Utrera* (6.5). Not the child's memory of past events.
3. **The court's private (in camera) interview** of the child, limited to the child's **reasonable preference** (best-interest factor (i), Module 2). The rules of evidence do **not** apply to that interview:
   > (6) In Camera Custody Hearings. In camera proceedings in child-custody matters to determine a child's custodial preference.
   > — MRE 1101(b)(6)
   Both halves matter: the interview **exists**, and it is **narrow** — it is the judge asking the child about preference, not a channel for the child to narrate the other parent's misconduct. The interview is conducted under **MCR 3.210(C)(5)**; the mechanics live in Module 5. *(⚠ VERIFY — the exact wording and subsection of MCR 3.210(C)(5) are taken from the outline, not from a primary source loaded for this draft; confirm against the court rule before relying on it.)*
4. **A court-appointed evaluator or lawyer-guardian ad litem (LGAL)**, who can gather and present the child's information within their role.
5. **A treating therapist or physician**, through 803(3)/803(4) and the records rules — the professional is the witness.
6. **The other parent's own words (801(d)(2)),** where the alienating or damaging statement was made *by the other parent directly*. This is the strongest and safest route, and it does not involve the child at all.

## 6.8 Completeness, originals, duplicates, and privilege

**Completeness (MRE 106).** If the other side reads a slice of a text thread or a letter, you can require the rest that fairness demands be heard at the same time:

> If a party introduces all or part of a writing or recorded statement, an adverse party may require the introduction, at that time, of any other part—or any other writing or recorded statement—that in fairness ought to be considered at the same time.
> — MRE 106

This cuts against cherry-picking — in both directions. A screenshot cropped to one message invites the other side to demand the messages around it.

**Originals and duplicates (MRE 1002–1003).** The general rule asks for the original to prove a document's content, but a **duplicate** is usually fine:

> An original writing, recording, or photograph is required in order to prove its content unless these rules or a statute provides otherwise.
> — MRE 1002

> A duplicate is admissible to the same extent as the original unless a genuine question is raised about the original's authenticity or the circumstances make it unfair to admit the duplicate.
> — MRE 1003

For electronic evidence, this is why keeping the **native file** matters: a printout is a duplicate, and if the other side raises a genuine authenticity dispute, the ability to produce the original export is what answers it. That is an **authentication** problem, and it belongs to Module 7 — remember that admitting a duplicate under MRE 1003 still does nothing about hearsay.

**Privilege (MRE 501).** Some communications are protected regardless of relevance:

> The common law governs a claim of privilege, unless a statute or court rule provides otherwise.
> — MRE 501

Specific privileges a family case runs into — physician–patient, psychologist/therapist–patient, and spousal — are set by **statute**, and their scope, exceptions, and how they can be **waived** are exactly the kind of thing to confirm from the statute and, where a waiver is in play, with counsel. *(⚠ VERIFY — the specific privilege statutes are not quoted from a primary source in this draft; confirm the operative statute and its exceptions before relying on a privilege claim.)* Two practical warnings:
- 🛑 **Any question of privilege waiver is a stop-and-seek-counsel gate.** Putting your **own** mental health at issue, or signing broad releases, can waive protection and open your records to the other side — the symmetry rule again.
- **Filing sensitive material publicly has a cost.** A child's therapy records or medical records placed in an open court file can harm the child and are rarely worth what one helpful line buys. Whether such material should be filed at all, sealed, or handled another way is a counsel question, not a self-help one.

## 6.9 Preserving the issue — objection → grounds → ruling → offer of proof (MRE 103)

Whether the rules helped you or hurt you, the ruling only counts later if it was made **on the record** the right way:

> (a) Preserving a Claim of Error. A party may claim error in a ruling to admit or exclude evidence only if the error affects a substantial right of the party and: (1) if the ruling admits evidence, a party, on the record: (A) timely objects or moves to strike; and (B) states the specific ground, unless it was apparent from the context; or (2) if the ruling excludes evidence, a party informs the court of its substance by an offer of proof, unless the substance was apparent from the context.
> — MRE 103(a)

Two moves follow from this rule:
- **When something objectionable comes in:** a *timely* objection stating the *specific ground* ("objection, hearsay"; "objection, relevance"; "objection, no foundation"). A general "I object" without a ground preserves little.
- **When the court keeps *your* evidence out:** an **offer of proof** — a short, on-the-record statement of what the excluded evidence *would have shown* — so a reviewing court can see what was lost. Without it, an exclusion is very hard to challenge later.

An **offer of proof** is simply telling the judge, for the record, the substance of the evidence the ruling kept out. It is the difference between a wrong ruling you can complain about and a wrong ruling that vanishes without a trace.

## Templates and tools

*These are analytical and practice aids, not filings and not scripts calibrated to any particular judge. Each assumes the Michigan Rules of Evidence apply to your proceeding — confirm that in Module 5. None is legal advice; do not use any of them as a substitute for confirming the current rule text and, where flagged, counsel.*

**1. The four-question hearsay screen (card).** For any statement you want to offer:
> 1. Is it a statement (someone's assertion)? 2. Made out of court? 3. Offered for its truth? → If **no** to #3, it is **not hearsay** (name the non-truth purpose). If **yes** to all three → 4. Is the speaker the **other party** (801(d)(2))? Does an **803 exception** fit? For a **record**, does each layer inside have its own door (805)? Is it a **child's** statement (803A does **not** apply — see 6.7)?

**2. "Is it in or out?" decision path (hearsay decision tree).** Walk it per exhibit:
> **a.** What **fact of consequence** (Module 2) does this move? None → **out** (MRE 402).
> **b.** Is it needlessly cumulative or more prejudicial than probative? → **403 risk**, trim it.
> **c.** Do you have **personal knowledge** (602), or a **witness/record** that does?
> **d.** Is it a text/photo/record/screenshot? → **authenticate it (Module 7).** (Separate question from hearsay.)
> **e.** Does it contain a **statement offered for its truth**? No → hearsay is not your problem. Yes → run the four-question screen.
> **f.** Statement by the **other party**? → **in** as non-hearsay (801(d)(2)) — and check whether **your** words are exposed the same way.
> **g.** Offered **not for truth**? → **in** for that limited purpose.
> **h.** Fits an **803 exception** (present sense / excited utterance / state of mind / medical / recorded recollection / business record / public record)? → **in**, with the foundation that exception needs.
> **i.** A **record**? → check **805** — each internal statement needs its own door.
> **j.** A **child's** statement? → **803A does not apply**; use the 6.7 routes; abuse disclosure → **stop, counsel.**
> **k.** **Privilege** (501)? Could it be excluded — or does offering it **waive** your own protection or expose the child?
> **l.** If **in**: lay the foundation and preserve it (103). If **out**: make an **offer of proof** (103).

**3. Party-opponent worked-example set.** Collect the other parent's own statements (texts, emails, posts, voicemails, messaging-app exports) and, for each, write: the date, the exact words, the **factor it moves** (Module 2), the **authentication route** (Module 7), and a one-line note that it is offered under **801(d)(2)(A)**. *Done when each item names a factor and an authentication route — a statement that moves no factor is not evidence, it is venting.*

**4. Objection and offer-of-proof cards (practice aids, oral use).**
> *Objection:* "Objection — [hearsay / relevance / no foundation / MRE 403]." If pressed: one sentence of ground.
> *Offer of proof (when your evidence is excluded):* "For the record, if permitted, [witness/exhibit] would show [substance], which is relevant to factor [ ]."
> *Do not use if* the rules of evidence do not apply to your proceeding (Module 5), or where a judge's own practice governs how objections are raised — observe first.

**5. Privilege screen.** Before offering or filing anything sensitive, ask: Is this a protected communication (therapy, medical, spousal)? Am I about to **waive** my own privilege by putting my condition at issue? Does filing this **publicly** expose the child or me? If any answer is yes or unclear → **hold and route to counsel** (6.8).

## Exercises

1. **Analyze five proposed exhibits, end to end *(evidence-log exercise).*** Pick five things you intend to offer. For each, complete every column: **(1) authentication route** (how it is shown to be genuine — Module 7); **(2) hearsay answer** (not hearsay / which exception / out); **(3) privilege answer** (protected? waiver risk?); **(4) disclosure answer** (has it been, or will it be, exchanged/listed by your hearing's exhibit deadline — hearing-prep module); **(5) sponsoring witness** (who puts it in — you, a custodian, a provider). *Done when each of the five exhibits has all five answers filled in, and any exhibit missing an answer is set aside rather than carried to the hearing.*
2. **Convert a journal entry into admissible-source leads *(scenario exercise).*** Take one entry from your documentation (Module 8). Rewrite what you *personally observed* as fact rather than conclusion (6.2), then list the **independent, adult sources** that could prove each fact a court could actually admit — a record, a custodian, a professional who was already involved, the **other parent's own message**. *Done when every lead is a document, an adult witness, your own firsthand knowledge, or the other party's own statement — and **no** lead asks anything of, or about-through, the child.* If the only route to a fact runs through the child, mark it "not my evidence to gather" and stop.

## Stop and seek counsel 🛑

- **A child's disclosure of abuse.** Do not build it into an exhibit or try to route it through a hearsay exception. `DO NOT USE THIS GUIDE ALONE` — counsel, and the professionals and authorities equipped for it (Module 17).
- **Any recording question at all.** Whether a recording is lawful is unsettled and carries criminal exposure; this book does not answer it. Any recording — of the other parent, of a call, and never of the child — is a stop. `DO NOT USE THIS GUIDE ALONE`.
- **Any question of privilege waiver** — your own records, the child's records, or broad releases (6.8).
- **CPS or police involvement, or a PPO/no-contact order** interacting with your evidence or exchanges. 🛑 SAFETY OVERRIDE; see Module 17.
