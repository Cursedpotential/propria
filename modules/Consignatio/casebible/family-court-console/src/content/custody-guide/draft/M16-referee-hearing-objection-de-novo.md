# Why this module matters

Most self-help material treats the referee hearing as a warm-up — an informal first pass before the "real" hearing in front of the judge. For a self-represented parent in Genesee County, **that is backwards, and it is one of the most expensive misunderstandings in the whole process.**

Two facts, taken together, are the reason:

1. **The rules of evidence apply at the referee hearing.** It is a real evidentiary proceeding — often your first and only one — and what gets proven there becomes the record everything after it is built on. (This is the point Module 5.2 makes; this module carries it into practice.)
2. **What happens next — the objection, and the "de novo" hearing — is far narrower than the phrase "de novo" sounds.** The judge who later reviews a referee's recommendation must let you present live evidence, but may bar evidence you *could* have presented to the referee and did not, and may decide the whole matter on the referee's record.

Put those together and the conclusion is uncomfortable but important: **for most Genesee pro se parents, the referee hearing is the trial.** Not the rehearsal for it. The trial itself.

This module explains what a referee hearing is, how the record you build there governs everything after, the two clocks that start when a recommendation arrives, exactly what a proper objection requires, and what "de novo" actually means in Michigan. It rests on two verified sources: **MCR 3.215** (the court rule) and **MCL 552.507** (the statute).

> **📄 The worked instance.** This module is the doctrine. For a step-by-step worked version in a real posture — the proof table, the tabbed exhibit sets, the two subpoena tracks, the courtroom running order, and a completed objection with a notice of hearing — see `draft/P1-hearing-prep-referee.md` and the completed objection at `draft/P1-worked-examples.md` **Part Two**. This module tells you *why*; the P1 packet shows you *how*, once.

## 16.1 What a referee hearing actually is

A domestic-relations **referee** is a lawyer the court assigns to hear certain motions. The referee does not sign your order. The referee holds a hearing, makes findings, and issues a **recommendation for an order** that a judge later approves or that you may object to. Whether your motion goes to a referee at all depends on local assignment practice — Module 4 explains the two-track system, and confirming your own track is one phone call.

> **MCR 3.215(A):** "A referee appointed pursuant to MCL 552.507(1) must be a member in good standing of the State Bar of Michigan."

*(VERIFIED verbatim — `sources/primary/michigan-court-rules_2026-07-31.md`.)*

The single most important line in this module is the one that tells you the hearing is a real evidentiary proceeding:

> **MCR 3.215(D)(1):** "The Michigan Rules of Evidence apply to referee hearings."

*(VERIFIED verbatim.)*

That one sentence is why everything in the proof half of this book — relevance and hearsay (Module 6), authentication (Module 7), the Best-Interest Evidence Matrix (Module 2) — comes to bear at the referee hearing, not later. A screenshot still needs a sponsoring witness. Hearsay is still hearsay. The other parent's own texts are still admissible against them as an opposing party's statement. The referee is applying the same rules a judge would.

A few structural facts follow from the rule:

- **The hearing is recorded.** MCR 3.215(D)(4): "An electronic or stenographic record must be kept of all hearings." The record is what an objection later attaches to; without it there is nothing to review.
- **Testimony is live.** MCR 3.215(D)(3): "Testimony must be taken in person, except that a referee may allow testimony to be taken by telephone for good cause, or under MCR 2.407."
- **The referee has to tell you about your right to a judicial hearing — twice.** MCR 3.215(D)(2): "A referee must provide the parties with notice of the right to request a judicial hearing by giving (a) oral notice during the hearing, and (b) written notice in the recommendation for an order."
- **Your notice must say it is a referee.** MCR 3.215(C)(1) requires that the notice scheduling the hearing "must clearly state that the matter will be heard by a referee." If the notice you received does not say that, the clerk can tell you who is hearing it.

*(All VERIFIED verbatim.)*

> ### The referee recommendation is **not** the FOC report — a distinction that costs people
>
> Two different documents, written by two different people, follow two different objection tracks and two different clocks. Readers confuse them constantly.
>
> | | **FOC report / recommendation (Module 15)** | **Referee recommendation for an order (this module)** |
> |---|---|---|
> | Written by | A Friend of the Court **investigator** | A **referee** (a lawyer) |
> | After | An **investigation** — interviews, records, home contacts | A **contested evidentiary hearing** |
> | Do the rules of evidence apply to the court's use of it? | **No** — MRE 1101(b)(9) exempts the court's *consideration* of it | **Yes** — the MRE applied at the hearing (MCR 3.215(D)(1)) |
> | Is it evidence? | **Not unless both parties agree** to admit it (see below) | The recommendation is not "evidence"; it rests on the hearing record |
> | How you respond | Review and object **before the judge decides** (MCR 3.210(C)(6)) — Module 15 | Object within **21 days**, triggering a judicial hearing (16.3–16.5) |
>
> **On the FOC report, one instruction carries across both modules:** a Friend of the Court report "is not admissible as evidence unless both parties agree to admit it in evidence" — *Duperon v Duperon*, 175 Mich App 77 (1989). **Agreeing to admit it converts a not-evidence document into evidence, for you and against you.** The full treatment — *Duperon*, *Marshall v Beal*, and the affirmative objection when a recommendation's findings track a report the hearing record does not independently support — is in **Module 15**, with the worked version at `draft/P1-worked-examples.md` Part Two, Ground 5. This module does not repeat it; it only flags the line where the two tracks meet: **a referee's recommendation that leans on an unstipulated FOC report is a place to look closely, not a place to stipulate.**

## 16.2 Building the record — because there may not be a second chance

If the referee hearing is the trial, then the discipline is simple to state and hard to do: **everything you would ever want a decision-maker to see goes in at the referee hearing.** Every exhibit, every witness, every date. The reasons are in 16.5, but the practical instruction belongs here, because it changes what you do *before* the hearing, not after.

The hands-on preparation — a proof table that maps each thing the court must find to the exhibit and the witness that establish it, three identical tabbed exhibit sets, the two different subpoena tracks for getting a witness or a document there, and what to say and not say — is worked in full at `draft/P1-hearing-prep-referee.md` Part A. Rather than repeat it, this module adds the three record-preservation points that make an objection possible later:

**Ask the referee to make findings.** An objection has to attach to a specific finding; if the referee never states one, there is nothing to object to.

> **MCR 3.215(E)(1)(a):** "The referee must find facts specially and state separately the law the referee applied. Brief, definite, and pertinent findings and conclusions on the contested matters are sufficient, without overelaboration of detail or particularization of facts."

*(VERIFIED verbatim.)* At the close of the hearing, it is fair to ask the referee to state findings on the record on the contested points.

**Get a ruling, and preserve what is kept out.** When evidence is excluded, the way to preserve it for review is the sequence taught in Module 6: state the objection or the offer, get the referee's ruling, and make an **offer of proof** — a brief statement, on the record, of what the excluded evidence would have shown. Evidence that was offered and refused, with an offer of proof, is preserved in a way that evidence never offered is not. (The mechanics are in Module 6; the spoken version is at `draft/P1-worked-examples.md` Part Four.)

**Know what the record costs.** The court's record is made on the court's equipment. A *transcript* of it is a separate thing you may have to order and pay for.

> **MCR 3.215(D)(4)(b):** "If ordered by the court, or if stipulated by the parties, the referee must provide a transcript, verified by oath, of each hearing held. The cost of preparing a transcript must be apportioned equally between the parties, unless otherwise ordered by the court."
>
> **MCR 3.215(D)(4)(c):** "At least 7 days before the judicial hearing, a party who intends to offer evidence from the record of the referee hearing must provide notice to the court and each other party. If a stenographic transcript is necessary, except as provided in subrule (4)(b), the party offering the evidence must pay for the transcript."

*(VERIFIED verbatim.)* Transcripts take time and money. A reader who decides to object learns the cost and lead time early, not the week of the judicial hearing. `ℹ PROVISIONAL — transcript cost and turnaround are local; ask the referee's office.`

> ### ⚠ This is the court's record — not permission to record anything
> MCR 3.215(D)(4)(a) lets the parties make a **contemporaneous copy of the court's own record**, on the court's equipment, "solely to assist the parties during the proceeding recorded," and it "may not be used publicly." That is the only recording this module discusses. **Nothing here tells you that any recording *you* make is lawful.** Do not record a conversation you are not part of, do not record the child, and do not place a device anywhere. Michigan law here is unsettled and the exposure is criminal. Any recording that already exists is a question for a lawyer before it is a question for a hearing. 🛑 `DO NOT USE THIS GUIDE ALONE`

## 16.3 The recommendation, and the two clocks it starts

After the hearing, the referee issues findings and a recommendation on a fixed timeline:

> **MCR 3.215(E)(1):** "Within 21 days after a hearing, the referee must either make a statement of findings on the record or submit a written, signed report containing a summary of testimony and a statement of findings. In either event, the referee must make a recommendation for an order and arrange for it to be submitted to the court and the attorneys for the parties, or the parties if they are not represented by counsel. A proof of service must be filed with the court."

*(VERIFIED verbatim.)*

The recommendation is not automatically the end. But **doing nothing makes it the end** — and that is the fact this module exists to make unmissable.

> **MCR 3.215(E)(1)(b)(ii):** the referee's recommended order must include "notice that if the recommended order is approved by the court and no written objection is filed with the court clerk within 21 days after the recommended order is served, the recommended order will become the final order."
>
> **MCR 3.215(E)(1)(c):** "If the recommendation is approved by the court and no written objection is filed with the court clerk within 21 days after service, the recommended order will become a final order."

*(VERIFIED verbatim.)*

> ### 🔴 THE 21-DAY OBJECTION CLOCK — the whole issue can be lost by silence
> **This is the deadline the router (FM.0) sends parents here for. Treat it as one of the hardest deadlines in the book.**
>
> If a referee's recommendation goes against you and you file nothing within 21 days, **the recommendation becomes the final order.** Not a draft. Not a proposal. The order that decides the issue — custody, parenting time, or support — without a judge ever independently weighing your evidence. There is no hearing you forgot to attend and no ruling you can appeal from; the loss happens by operation of the rule, quietly, because the clock ran.
>
> That is why this deadline is treated throughout this book as **forfeiture-grade**: missing it does not weaken your case, it ends the issue. Doing nothing is a decision, and the court will honor it.
>
> **The clock runs from when the recommendation is *served* (MCR 3.215(E)(4)).** Enter the date on a calendar the day anything arrives, and — for the reasons in the trigger note just below — treat your working deadline as **a week earlier** than the raw 21st day.
> ⚠ **TIME-CRITICAL** · appears in Appendix A · 🔴 **ACT TODAY**

### The other clock: 7 days to object to a *proposed recommended order*

There is a second, shorter clock that catches people who are watching only for the 21-day one. A party — often the other side — may draft the *proposed recommended order* itself and start a 7-day objection window running against you:

> **MCR 3.215(E)(3)(d):** "within 7 days after the conclusion of the referee hearing, a party may serve a copy of a proposed recommended order on all other parties with a notice to them that it will be submitted to the referee for approval if no written objections to its accuracy or completeness are filed with the court clerk within 7 days after service of the notice."
>
> **MCR 3.215(E)(3)(d)(ii):** "To object to the accuracy or completeness of a proposed recommended order, the party must within 7 days after service of the proposed order, file written objections with the court clerk that state with specificity the inaccuracy or omission in the proposed recommended order, and serve the objections on all parties as required by MCR 2.107, together with a notice of hearing and an alternative proposed recommended order."

*(VERIFIED verbatim.)*

That is a **different objection**, on a **different clock**, aimed at a **different problem** — whether the written order *accurately captures what the referee decided*, not whether the referee decided rightly. It requires three things at once: written objections filed with the clerk stating specifically the inaccuracy or omission; service on all parties per MCR 2.107; and a **notice of hearing plus an alternative proposed order you have drafted.** Seven days. **If a proposed recommended order arrives in the mail, that is the day the week starts.** 🔴 **ACT TODAY**

> ### ⊗ CONFLICTED — the 21-day trigger is described three different ways
> The court rule counts 21 days from when the recommendation is **served** (MCR 3.215(E)(4)). The statute counts 21 days from when it is "**made available to that party**" (MCL 552.507(4), quoted in 16.5). Genesee's own custody-motion packet has been reported to say the recommendation becomes an order unless an objection is filed within 21 days after it was "**mailed**." Those three triggers can fall on different dates — a recommendation handed to you at the hearing is arguably "made available" that day, while service by mail lands later.
>
> **This project does not resolve which controls; both rule and statute are verified verbatim, and no case reconciling them has been read.** The instruction all three readings converge on: **count 21 days from the *earliest* date you could have had the document, write that date down the day it arrives, and act well before it.** `ℹ PROVISIONAL — the Genesee packet's "mailed" language is a local fact; confirm the current packet with the clerk.`

### The consent trap: signing gives up the hearing

There is also a way to lose the objection right on purpose without meaning to:

> **MCR 3.215(E)(8):** "The parties may waive their right to object to the referee's recommendation for an order by consenting in writing to the immediate entry of the recommended order."

*(VERIFIED verbatim.)* **Signing the recommended order gives up the judicial hearing.** If a recommendation is handed over in the hallway with a request to sign so it can be entered today, that signature is the waiver in (E)(8). Where the recommendation grants what was asked for, in specific enforceable terms, signing may be sensible. Where it does not, the 21 days exist precisely so the document can be read at home, line by line, against what was requested. You have the 21 days whether or not you use them.

**The official objection form** is **FOC 68** (objection to referee's recommended order); the notice language appears on **FOC 60**. Link and use the current SCAO/Genesee forms — this book does not reproduce them. `ℹ PROVISIONAL — confirm the current FOC 68 / FOC 60 revision on the footer before filing; the audited copy's revision was not verified.`

## 16.4 Writing an objection that works

The 21-day objection is not a letter of disagreement. The rule sets out what it must contain, and a generic objection can quietly forfeit most of your case.

> **MCR 3.215(E)(4):** "A party may obtain a judicial hearing on any matter that has been the subject of a referee hearing and that resulted in a statement of findings and a recommended order by filing a written objection and notice of hearing within 21 days after the referee's recommendation for an order is served on the attorneys for the parties, or the parties if they are not represented by counsel. The objection must include a clear and concise statement of the specific findings or application of law to which an objection is made. Objections regarding the accuracy or completeness of the recommendation must state with specificity the inaccuracy or omission."
>
> **MCR 3.215(E)(5):** "The party who requests a judicial hearing must serve the objection and notice of hearing on the opposing party or counsel in the manner provided in MCR 2.119(C)."

*(VERIFIED verbatim.)*

Read closely, (E)(4) and (E)(5) require **four** things, not one:

1. **A written objection** that names the **specific** findings or applications of law objected to — not "I object to the recommendation," but *this finding, for this reason.*
2. **A notice of hearing** — filed *with* the objection, not instead of it. An objection without a notice of hearing is incomplete.
3. **Filed with the court clerk** within 21 days.
4. **Served** on the opposing party or counsel in the manner of MCR 2.119(C) — the same 9-days-by-mail / 7-days-by-delivery service math as a motion (Module 12), with a proof of service.

**Why specificity is not a formality.** Under the de novo rule in the next section, a court "may … prohibit a party from presenting evidence on findings of fact to which no objection was filed" and "determine that the referee's finding was conclusive as to a fact to which no objection was filed" (MCR 3.215(F)(2)(a)–(b)). A vague, whole-recommendation objection can therefore leave every specific finding standing as conclusive. **The objection is where you decide which findings are still in play.** Naming them is how they stay contested.

The disciplined objection maps, finding by finding: *identify the finding → state why it is wrong, as fact or as law → cite where the record shows it.* The blank worksheet is in the tools below; a completed fictional objection — with six grounds, a notice of hearing, the MCR 3.215(D)(4)(c) notice, and a proof of service — is worked at `draft/P1-worked-examples.md` **Part Two**. This module does not reproduce it.

> **A same-day option to know about.** MCR 3.215(E)(7) lets a court "hear a party's objection to the referee's recommendation for an order on the same day as the referee hearing," but only if the scheduling notice said a same-day judicial hearing would be available *and* the parties may refuse it if they have not yet decided whether to object. *(VERIFIED verbatim.)* A reader who has not decided may decline the same-day hearing and keep the full 21 days.

`ℹ PROVISIONAL — whether a Genesee referee objection must also carry an LCR 2.119 concurrence certificate and a proposed order, as a motion would, is a local question; ask the clerk. (Genesee LCR 2.119 imposes both on motions; the P1 packet lists them as an open call-list item.)`

## 16.5 What "de novo" really means — and what it does not

Here is the misconception this module exists to kill.

**The myth:** "de novo" means the judge starts over from scratch — a clean do-over, the referee's work set aside, a fresh trial where you can finally put on the case you were saving. Version 1 of this material told readers the judge "does not defer to the referee's findings but rehears the matter fresh." **That is materially misleading, and it is a trap.**

Start with what the statute actually says a de novo hearing *includes*:

> **MCL 552.507(6):** "Subject to subsection (5), de novo hearings include, but are not limited to, the following: **(a) A new decision based entirely on the record of a previous hearing**, including any memoranda, recommendations, or proposed orders by the referee. (b) A new decision based only on evidence presented at the time of the de novo hearing. (c) A new decision based in part on the record of a referee hearing supplemented by evidence that was not introduced at a previous hearing."

*(VERIFIED verbatim — legislature.mi.gov full-section fetch, Aug 9, 2026; recorded in `verification_ledger.md` and `draft/P1-hearing-prep-referee.md`. ⚠ not yet re-anchored to an archived primary — see drafting notes.)*

Read (6)(a) twice. By statute, a de novo hearing **can be "a new decision based entirely on the record of a previous hearing."** The judge is allowed to decide the whole thing on what happened in front of the referee. That is the opposite of a fresh trial — and it is why "save your best evidence for the judge" is not a strategy but a way to lose.

The statute makes the reason explicit. A hearing stays "de novo" even with restrictions **only because you had your chance at the referee hearing**:

> **MCL 552.507(5):** "A hearing is de novo despite the court's imposition of reasonable restrictions and conditions to conserve the resources of the parties and the court if the following conditions are met: (a) The parties have been given **a full opportunity to present and preserve important evidence at the referee hearing.** (b) For findings of fact to which the parties have objected, the parties are afforded a new opportunity to offer the same evidence to the court as was presented to the referee and to supplement that evidence with evidence that could not have been presented to the referee."

*(VERIFIED verbatim — same source.)*

Now the court rule, which sets both the **floor** and the **ceiling** of the judicial hearing:

> **MCR 3.215(F)(2):** "To the extent allowed by law, the court may conduct the judicial hearing by review of the record of the referee hearing, but **the court must allow the parties to present live evidence at the judicial hearing.** The court may, in its discretion:
> (a) prohibit a party from presenting evidence on findings of fact to which no objection was filed;
> (b) determine that the referee's finding was conclusive as to a fact to which no objection was filed;
> **(c) prohibit a party from introducing new evidence or calling new witnesses unless there is an adequate showing that the evidence was not available at the referee hearing;**
> (d) impose any other reasonable restrictions and conditions to conserve the resources of the parties and the court."

*(VERIFIED verbatim.)*

Both halves are true, and most self-help material teaches only one:

- **The floor (what protects you):** the judge **must** allow live evidence. A court that decides your objection on the paper record alone, refusing to let you put on any live testimony, has gone too far. "De novo" is therefore **more than an appeal** — it is not pure record review.
- **The ceiling (what traps you):** the judge **may** shut out *new* evidence you could have presented to the referee and did not (F)(2)(c), **may** treat unobjected findings as conclusive (F)(2)(a)–(b), and — under MCL 552.507(6)(a) — **may** decide entirely on the referee's record. "De novo" is therefore **less than a fresh trial.**

**So what does "de novo" mean in Michigan?** It means the judge reaches an **independent decision** — the judge is not a rubber stamp for the referee — but reaches it on a record that is largely **fixed at the referee hearing**. You have a right to present live evidence at the judicial hearing; you do **not** have an automatic right to present *new* evidence, and you may face a recommendation whose unobjected findings are already conclusive. It sits between an appeal and a new trial, and closer to the referee's record than the word "de novo" suggests.

> ### 🔴 THE FATAL PRO SE STRATEGY: "save your best evidence for the judge"
> This is the single most damaging plan a self-represented parent can carry into a referee hearing, and it follows directly from the myth above.
>
> Because MCR 3.215(F)(2)(c) lets the judge **bar new evidence** unless you make "an adequate showing that the evidence was not available at the referee hearing," the parent who held their strongest exhibit or witness back for the judge may **never be allowed to present it.** And because MCL 552.507(6)(a) lets the judge decide **entirely on the referee's record**, there may be no fresh hearing to save anything for.
>
> **The referee hearing is the record. Failing to build it there is often unrecoverable.** Everything goes in at the referee hearing — every exhibit, every witness, every date. If a document or witness genuinely could not be obtained in time, that unavailability is worth documenting *when it happens*, because (F)(2)(c) turns on exactly that question. The "everything in now" completeness checklist in the tools below exists for this one purpose.

Two more mechanical points close the loop:

- **The judicial hearing is itself on a clock.** MCR 3.215(F)(1): "The judicial hearing must be held within 21 days after the written objection is filed, unless the time is extended by the court for good cause." *(VERIFIED verbatim.)*
- **If you rely on the referee record, give notice and expect to pay.** Offering evidence from the referee's record requires the 7-day notice under MCR 3.215(D)(4)(c) (16.2), and the party offering it generally pays for the transcript.

**And it cuts both ways** (`GUARDRAILS.md` §7). The same rules that can bar *your* late evidence bar the other parent's; the same conclusive-findings rule that can freeze a bad finding against you can freeze a good one in your favor. The record you build — or fail to build — governs both sides.

## 16.6 The cost of a bad objection

An objection is a right, not a reflex. The rule attaches a price to objecting for the wrong reasons:

> **MCR 3.215(F)(3):** "If the court determines that an objection is frivolous or has been interposed for the purpose of delay, the court may assess reasonable costs and attorney fees."

*(VERIFIED verbatim.)*

An objection that names specific findings and explains why each is wrong on the record is doing what the rule asks. An objection filed because a parent is unhappy, or to slow the case down, is exposed under (F)(3) — and under the wider sanctions that run at every filer: MCR 1.109(E) (your signature certifies the document is well grounded and not filed to harass, with a sanction for violation) and MCL 600.2591 (costs and fees for a frivolous claim or defense). **These run at the reader as readily as at the other parent** (`GUARDRAILS.md` §7). The protection against them is not caution about objecting — it is specificity about *what* you object to.

## 16.7 Interim effect: the recommendation may already be operating

Objecting does not automatically freeze the recommendation. A court may give a recommendation **interim effect** while the judicial hearing is pending — but the rule fences off the most serious orders.

> **MCR 3.215(G)(1):** "the court may, by an administrative order or by an order in the case, provide that the referee's recommended order will take effect on an interim basis pending a judicial hearing. The court must provide notice that the referee's recommended order will be an interim order by including that notice under a separate heading in the referee's recommended order, or by an order adopting the referee's recommended order as an interim order."
>
> **MCR 3.215(G)(3):** "The court may not, by administrative order, give interim effect to a referee's recommendation for the following types of orders: (a) An order under subrule (G)(2); (b) An order that changes a child's custody; (c) An order that changes a child's domicile; (d) An order that would render subsequent judicial consideration of the matter moot."

*(VERIFIED verbatim.)* Subrule (G)(2), referenced in (G)(3)(a), separately bars interim effect for orders of incarceration, forfeiture of property, or costs/fines/sanctions.

Two things to notice:

- **Custody and domicile changes cannot be given interim effect by administrative order** — the categories where the harm of running an order before it is reviewed would be hardest to undo.
- **Parenting time is absent from the (G)(3) list.** A parenting-time recommendation is not among the categories excluded from interim effect by administrative order. That cuts both ways: a favorable parenting-time recommendation may begin operating before the judicial hearing, and an unfavorable one may too. The recommended order should carry the separate-heading interim-effect notice the rule requires; reading for it, and calendaring accordingly, is how you learn which. `ℹ PROVISIONAL — whether Genesee has an administrative order giving referee recommendations interim effect is a local fact; ask the clerk.`

The statute adds a service deadline for an interim order: **MCL 552.507(7):** "Pending a de novo hearing, the referee's recommended order may be presented to the court for entry as an interim order as provided by the Michigan court rules. The interim order shall be served on the parties within 3 days and shall be subject to review as provided under this subsection." *(VERIFIED verbatim — same source and caveat as MCL 552.507(4)–(6).)*

## Templates and tools

**1. Referee-hearing proof plan (the master aid — worked in P1).** For each thing the court must find, one row: *what must be established → the exhibit → who sponsors it → how it gets in (authentication route, hearsay answer).* This is the Best-Interest Evidence Matrix from Module 2, pointed at the hearing. The completed version for a real posture is `draft/P1-hearing-prep-referee.md` A.5.

**2. The "everything in now" completeness checklist.** Run it before the referee hearing, because of MCR 3.215(F)(2)(c):

- [ ] Every exhibit that supports any element of my motion is in the tabbed sets and will be offered **at the referee hearing.**
- [ ] Every witness I would ever want a decision-maker to hear is subpoenaed and called **at the referee hearing** (or the reason one is not is written down).
- [ ] Every date I rely on is in my own testimony, from personal knowledge.
- [ ] Nothing on my proof plan is marked "save for the judge."
- [ ] For anything I could not obtain in time, the **unavailability is documented and dated** — because (F)(2)(c) turns on whether the evidence "was not available at the referee hearing."

## Stop and seek counsel 🛑

- You receive a recommendation you want to object to but **cannot state, in one sentence, which specific finding is wrong.** A vague objection risks MCR 3.215(F)(3) costs; advice before filing is worth it.
- The other side seeks **costs or fees** against you.
- The recommendation **changes custody or domicile** rather than parenting time. Different burden (Module 2), different interim-effect rules (16.7), and a different appeal landscape — an order granting or denying a change of legal custody, physical custody, or domicile is appealable **of right on a 21-day jurisdictional clock** (Module 28). 🔴 **ACT TODAY**
- Anyone suggests the **child** should speak to anyone about the case. 🛑 `DO NOT USE THIS GUIDE ALONE`
- A **CPS, police, or criminal** matter surfaces at or after the hearing (Module 17). 🛑 `DO NOT USE THIS GUIDE ALONE`
- Any **recording** comes up, in any form (Module 9). 🛑 `DO NOT USE THIS GUIDE ALONE`
- **DV, a PPO, or a no-contact order** touches how you can safely attend or respond. 🛑 SAFETY OVERRIDE (FM.2, Module 17).

**Free and low-cost help in Genesee County** `ℹ PROVISIONAL — confirm before relying`: Genesee County Legal Resource Center (courthouse Room 203); Legal Services of Eastern Michigan (Flint); YWCA Greater Flint; Michigan Legal Help (michiganlegalhelp.org); State Bar of Michigan lawyer referral. A single referee hearing, or a single objection, is a strong candidate for **limited-scope representation** — a lawyer for one hearing (Module 4.7).
