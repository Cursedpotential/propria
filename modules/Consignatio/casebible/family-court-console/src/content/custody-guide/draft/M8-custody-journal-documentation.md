# Why this module exists — and the habit that starts today

Almost everything a custody case runs on is ordinary documentation: what happened on a given day, who was there, what was said, and the texts, emails, photos, and records that go with it. This module is about capturing that raw material so it is still usable months later, in a room where the rules of evidence apply.

It is built around one uncomfortable correction, and the sooner you absorb it the better every other habit in this module becomes:

**Your journal is scaffolding. It is not the evidence.** The evidence is your live testimony, the other parent's own words, and the records a business or office keeps. The journal's job is to make your testimony accurate and to tell you which admissible sources to go pull. A parent who treats the notebook itself as the proof builds a case on the one thing in the room the judge is least likely to be allowed to read.

Everything below follows from that. And it does not require you to wait for anything or spend anything.

> ### The three moves you can make today
> None of these is a filing. None of them starts a clock. Together they are the day-one habit this module teaches.
>
> 1. **Open one folder** — paper or digital — named for the case, with room for entries, sent and received communications, and preserved files.
> 2. **Start a contemporaneous log** — today's date, and from here forward, one dated entry per event, written the same day.
> 3. **Freeze your own data** — stop editing, deleting, or "cleaning up" anything on your own devices and accounts (Section 8.5). The cheapest evidence mistake to avoid is the one where you erase your own proof.

## 8.0 The rule that governs this entire module — the child is never your source

Before any template, one rule sits above all of them, and it does not bend.

**You document your own observations and the other adult's conduct. You never gather documentation through your child.**

That means, in this module and everywhere in this guide:

- You do **not** interview, question, debrief, or "check in with" the child about the other parent's household, and you do not write down what the child reported about the other home.
- You do **not** coach the child on what to say, ask the child to remember things for you, or task the child with watching or reporting.
- You do **not** record the child, and you do **not** send the child anywhere carrying a recording device. (Recording is a hard stop for a separate reason too — Section 8.5 and Module 9.)
- You do **not** rely on the child emotionally as a witness, a courier, or a confidant about the case.

This is not a balancing test, and it is not softened by a good reason (`GUARDRAILS.md` §8; acceptance test 8). It also happens to be sound strategy: a parent who uses a child as an intelligence source is the parent the court starts watching, and factors (j) and (g) of MCL 722.23 (Module 2) turn against them. But the rule holds whether or not it helps you.

**What you *may* write down:** what **you** personally saw or heard, and what **another adult** said **to you.** Nothing in your journal should begin *"she told me that Mom said…"* If a fact only reached you through the child, it is not a journal entry — and if what the child said is a disclosure of abuse, that is a stop-and-route-out event, not a documentation task (see the stop gates at the end of this module, and Module 6's child-hearsay warning).

## 8.1 What the journal is for — and the trap in using it

The journal has four honest jobs. None of them is "be the exhibit."

1. **It refreshes your memory so that *you* are an accurate witness.** Your live testimony is admissible; your recollection of a date six months out is not reliable without help. The note is the help.
2. **It routes you to admissible sources.** An entry that reads "3/6, exchange missed, texted him at 4:40" tells you to go preserve the *text* — which, being the other parent's own words, is not hearsay against him (MRE 801(d)(2); Module 6). The journal is a map to the real evidence.
3. **It can impeach** — if the other parent testifies to something your contemporaneous note contradicts.
4. **It can, narrowly, be read into the record as recorded recollection** — but only in a specific situation, and even then it does not become your exhibit.

On that last point, here is the operative language, quoted from the rule Module 6 owns in full:

> If admitted, the record may be read into evidence but **may be received as an exhibit only if offered by an adverse party.**
> — MRE 803(5) (recorded recollection; full three-part test and verbatim rule in Module 6)

Read that clause twice. Even when a note qualifies as recorded recollection, **you** do not get to hand the journal to the court as your exhibit — only the *other side* can move it in as an exhibit. This is the rule telling you, in its own words, that the journal is scaffolding.

> ### ⚠ The refreshing-memory trap — the moment your journal becomes the other side's to read
> The journal's main use is to refresh your memory. But the instant you use a writing to refresh your memory as a witness, the other side gets rights to it:
>
> > An adverse party is entitled to have the writing or object produced, to inspect it, to cross-examine the witness about it, and to introduce in evidence—for its bearing on credibility only unless otherwise admissible under these rules—any portion that relates to the witness's testimony.
> > — MRE 612(b)
>
> **The practical consequence:** if you glance at your journal on the stand to remember a date, opposing counsel may be entitled to see the page — and to cross-examine you on everything in it that touches your testimony. That is the structural reason every entry has to be written as though the other side will read it out loud. A journal full of adjectives, theories, and venting is a cross-examination waiting to happen. A journal of dated, dry facts is not.
>
> It is also the reason Sol's warning matters so much: **the journal is never filed or handed over as a whole** by you (Section 8.2). The parts of it that become useful become useful through the *sources* they point to, and through your testimony — not by the notebook changing hands.

**None of this is a reason to stop journaling.** It is the reason to journal in a form that survives being read by someone who is not on your side. The rest of this module is that form.

## 8.2 The custody log — keeping one that survives cross-examination

A log entry that survives has a fixed, boring shape. The fields are the same every time, so that the writing stays factual and so that each entry already knows which part of your case it belongs to.

**Each entry records, in this order:**

| Field | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| **Date and time** | The actual date and clock time. Written the same day. |
| **Place** | Where it happened, specifically enough that a stranger could find it. |
| **Who was present** | The adults present. (Not the child as a *source* — the child's mere presence is a fact; what the child *said* about the other home is not an entry — Section 8.0.) |
| **What happened** | Facts only. What you saw, did, and heard, in plain past-tense sentences. No conclusions, no motives, no adjectives about character. |
| **Related communications** | The text, email, app message, or letter tied to this event — and *where the actual item is preserved* (Section 8.5), not a retyped version of it. |
| **The order term or factor it maps to** | The specific provision of your order this touches, or the best-interest factor (MCL 722.23) or parenting-time factor (MCL 722.27a(7)) it is evidence of. This is the column that connects the log to your case (Section 8.3 and below). |
| **Follow-up / corroboration** | Who else saw it, what record would confirm it, what you did next. |

**The discipline that keeps entries admissible-adjacent and cross-examination-proof:**

- **Contemporaneous.** Written the same day, while it is fresh. A log reconstructed weeks later is worth far less and, worse, *looks* reconstructed. Which is the next trap.
- **Factual, not editorial.** "Exchange set for 6:00; he arrived 6:41; I waited in the lot; child produced 6:44." Not "he was late again, like always, to jerk me around." The first sentence is evidence; the second is an argument you have handed the other side.
- **No retroactive additions.** You do not go back and add to, sharpen, or "improve" an old entry. If you learn something later, it is a **new entry with today's date** that references the earlier one. A journal with entries edited after the fact is a journal the other side can attack as a whole.
- **One line, one fact.** Assume every sentence will be read aloud by someone who is not on your side (this is the same read-aloud test as the communication rule in Section 8.4).
- **Concise and corroborated beats exhaustive.** The goal is a short record of things that matter, each pointing to something that can confirm it — not a grievance log of every slight. Over-documenting every minor annoyance buries the three entries that decide the case and makes the writer look obsessive (a relevance problem the court will notice — MRE 401/403, Module 6).

> ### How the log connects to the rest of your case — the Best-Interest Evidence Matrix (Module 2)
> The **factor-tag column** above is not decoration. It is the pipe between your daily log and the master template in Module 2 — the **Best-Interest Evidence Matrix** (12 factors × my evidence / their likely evidence / witness / exhibit / admissibility route).
>
> Each time you tag a log entry with a factor, that dated fact has a home: it becomes a line in the Matrix's "my evidence (dated facts)" column for that factor. Then the Matrix does the thing the journal cannot — it forces the next question: **how does this fact get admitted?** Which witness, which exhibit, which authentication route (Modules 5–7). A fact you log but cannot route into the Matrix's admissibility column is not yet evidence; it is a lead. The log gathers leads all day; the Matrix turns the good ones into a plan.
>
> So the workflow is: **log the fact → tag it to a factor or order term → carry it to that factor's row in the Matrix → answer the admissibility column there.** The journal feeds the Matrix; the Matrix feeds the hearing.

> ### ⚠ Do not file the log, and do not hand it over whole
> This warning travels with the template itself, every time it is used. Your log is **not** filed with any motion, and it is **not** handed to a Friend of the Court investigator, a custody evaluator, or a Lawyer-Guardian ad Litem as a complete document. Handing over the whole thing can disclose privileged material, your strategy, hearsay you cannot use, and any intemperate line you ever wrote — all at once, to be read by someone forming an opinion about you. When a specific entry matters, it matters through the **admissible source it points to** and through **your testimony** — not by the notebook changing hands. If an evaluator or the court orders production of records, that is a moment to get advice (stop gates, below), not to volunteer the binder.

## 8.3 Facts, inference, emotion, requested action — kept in four separate columns

The single most useful editing habit is to separate four things that a stressed writer blends together. Blended, they poison the whole entry; separated, the facts stay clean and the rest stays private.

- **Fact** — what you observed. Datable, place-able, and something another person could have seen or heard. *"He arrived at 6:41."*
- **Inference** — a conclusion you are drawing from the fact. It is not itself a fact, and it is exactly what the court decides, not what you assert. *"He seems to run late whenever I have plans."*
- **Emotion** — how you felt. Real, and legitimately yours — and not evidence of anything about the other parent. *"I felt humiliated waiting in the lot."*
- **Requested action** — what you might do or ask for. *"Ask for a fixed exchange time and a grace-period term."*

Only the **fact** column feeds your log entry and the Matrix. The **inference** and **emotion** columns are for you — they help you think, and they belong nowhere near a filing or an evaluator. The **requested action** column is where a pattern of clean facts eventually turns into precise relief (Module 12), phrased in order language rather than grievance (Module 2, §2.7).

Keeping these apart is also what makes the read-aloud test survivable. The lines that get people in trouble on cross-examination are almost always inference and emotion written *as if* they were fact. Column them off, and the fact that remains is short, dull, and durable.

## 8.4 Communication hygiene — one written channel, and the read-aloud test

How you communicate with the other parent is itself documentation, and it is documentation that can be aimed at **you**. Two ideas govern it.

**First: one written channel.** The cleanest record comes from consolidating your co-parenting communication into a single **written** channel and keeping it there. Writing gives you a dated, quotable record; a phone call gives you a memory dispute. If your case allows direct contact, that channel is usually a co-parenting app or a single email thread. If it does not —

> ### 🛑 SAFETY OVERRIDE / DV interstitial — the order decides your channel, not this module
> If a **PPO, a no-contact order, a bond condition, or your custody order** routes or restricts your communication, **that restriction controls over every word in this section.** Do not open a channel, send a message, or create contact of any kind that an order forbids — and never create contact in order to generate documentation. If you are unsure what your order permits, that uncertainty is itself a reason to stop and ask someone who can read it (FM.2; Module 17; and the pre-filing safety planning in FM.5). The mechanics of writing *permitted* parenting-time requests through a restricted channel are handled in the interim documentation protocol (Packet 1) and are not repeated here.

**Co-parenting apps** (OurFamilyWizard, TalkingParents, AppClose and the like) earn their place for a specific evidentiary reason: they timestamp messages, they are hard to alter after the fact, and — the payoff — a **certified export the app produces as the record-keeper** can be authenticated the easy way, as a business record, without you having to prove each message by live testimony. **How** that certificate works, and the sharp line between a *certified export* and a *screenshot you took of the app*, is Module 7's territory (§7.3, §7.5) — the short version is that they are **not** interchangeable: the certified export can ride the custodian-certificate route, and your screenshot cannot. Preserve both (Section 8.5), and read Module 7 before you rely on either.

**Second: the read-aloud test.** Write every message as though it will be **read aloud in court, in full, by someone who is not on your side** — because it can be. Here the symmetry rule bites (`GUARDRAILS.md` §7): the other parent's messages are useful to *you* precisely because they are that parent's own words (MRE 801(d)(2), Module 6) — and **your** messages are useful to *them* for the identical reason. A message you would not want read back to you is a gift to the other side, not documentation.

A message passes the read-aloud test when it is short, specific, about the child's logistics, and free of any word describing the other parent's character, mind, or motives. The banned vocabulary is absolute and applies to your messages as much as your filings: **narcissist, alienator, abuser, gaslighting, parental alienation, PAS, personality disorder** — and anything like them (`GUARDRAILS.md` §2; outline §0.5). You plead and you write **dated conduct**, and you let the court name any pattern.

> ### ⚠ Trap — documenting by provocation
> A record built by **provoking** an incident is worse than no record. Sending messages designed to bait a reaction, showing up to manufacture a confrontation, or needling the other parent so they respond badly does not build evidence — it builds a **course of conduct** that can be pointed at you. Michigan's harassment and stalking law reaches "a pattern of conduct composed of a series of 2 or more separate noncontinuous acts evidencing a continuity of purpose," and while it excludes "conduct that serves a **legitimate purpose**," you do not want to be the person arguing about where that line falls (MCL 750.411h; see the interference material behind Module 12). Ordinary, child-focused co-parenting communication serves a legitimate purpose. A campaign engineered to produce screenshots does not. Document what happens; do not arrange for things to happen so you can document them.

## 8.5 Digital preservation — keeping it intact so it can be authenticated later

This section is about **holding on to** the digital material a case runs on — texts, emails, app messages, photos, voicemails, call logs, and records — in a form that Module 7 can later get admitted. It is not about *how* authentication works (that is Module 7, and it is not restated here). It is about the preservation moves that make Module 7's foundations possible in the first place. Do these wrong and the best foundation script in the world has nothing to stand on.

**The litigation hold on your own data — the most important preservation rule.** From the day a dispute is live, treat your own devices and accounts as frozen: **you do not edit, selectively delete, crop-and-replace, or "clean up" anything**, including messages you regret sending. There are two reasons, and both come from rules Module 7 quotes in full:

- A **duplicate** (a screenshot, a printout) is admissible to the same extent as the original **until authenticity is genuinely disputed** — and when it is disputed, the thing that answers the dispute is the **native file**, the message as it lives in the app or on the device, with its metadata (MRE 1003; Module 7 §7.4). If you have deleted the native file and kept only a cropped screenshot, you have nothing to answer a forgery claim with.
- The limited excuse for a missing original does **not** cover a loss you caused: it forgives destruction only where the proponent was **not** "acting in bad faith" (MRE 1004(a); Module 7 §7.4). Selectively deleting your own messages is how you turn your own evidence into a credibility problem.

A gap in a record reads as exactly what it is. And the messages usually exist on the other side's device and the carrier's or platform's servers regardless — so deletion does not erase them, it only erases *your* copy and *your* credibility.

**Keep the native file, not only the screenshot.** For each item, preserve the source where it actually lives — the message in the app, the photo in the camera roll with its embedded date/location data (EXIF), the email in the account with its full header — in addition to any printout. The screenshot is for reading; the native file is for proving (Module 7 §7.4).

**Capture the complete context.** Preserve the **whole** thread, the **full** email header and chain, the conversation **continuous** with no gap where messages were removed. A fragment invites the other side to demand the rest at the worst possible moment (the completeness rule is MRE 106, Module 6; the foundation consequences are Module 7 §7.5–7.7).

**Document the collection method.** Note, for each item, **when and how** you captured or exported it and **from what device or account**, and that you still hold the original. That note is not busywork — it is precisely step five of the foundation Module 7 teaches (§7.5), written down while you remember it instead of reconstructed on the stand.

**A chain-of-custody log** (template below) ties all of this together: one row per item, recording what it is, where the native file is, when and how you captured it, and where the copies live. It is your own working record of preservation; like the journal, it is scaffolding, not an exhibit.

> ### 🔴 DO NOT USE THIS GUIDE ALONE — recordings are a hard stop, routed to Module 9 and counsel
> **This section preserves material you *already lawfully have*. It does not tell you to create a recording, and it takes no position on whether any recording is lawful.** Whether you may lawfully make, keep, or use an audio or video recording is a hard stop in Michigan — the law carries criminal exposure and unsettled questions — and it belongs entirely to **Module 9** and a licensed Michigan attorney (`GUARDRAILS.md` §9; outline §0.4).
>
> So: preserving **a voicemail the other parent left on your phone**, or **a video the other parent posted publicly**, is ordinary preservation. Creating a recording is not — and three things are never in doubt no matter what Module 9 concludes: **do not record a conversation you are not part of; do not send a child anywhere with a recording device; do not record the child** (`GUARDRAILS.md` §8). Likewise, **do not** try to preserve evidence by getting into the other parent's phone, email, or accounts, or by placing a tracker — account access and trackers route to Module 9 and counsel, and material gathered that way can be a crime and can poison your case. If preserving something would require you to record someone, access an account that is not yours, or plant a device, **stop and go to Module 9.**

## 8.6 Privacy, redaction, and your address

Preserving everything privately is the opposite of exposing everything publicly. Two rules keep the second from happening.

**Protected personal identifying information stays out of public filings.** Michigan protects a specific list of identifiers, verbatim:

> The following personal identifying information is protected and shall not be included in any public document or attachment filed with the court on or after April 1, 2022, except as provided by these rules:
> (i) date of birth,
> (ii) social security number or national identification number,
> (iii) driver's license number or state-issued personal identification card number,
> (iv) passport number, and
> (v) financial account numbers.
> — MCR 1.109(D)(9)(a)

When a protected identifier genuinely has to reach the court, the rule's own mechanism is to file the public document with the identifier **redacted** and to supply the full information separately on the SCAO personal-identifying-information form (MCR 1.109(D)(9)(b)). The preservation point for this module: keep your **unredacted native** copies privately, in your own folder — and let the **public filing** be the redacted one.

**Do not over-share the child or your private life into the public file.** Beyond the listed identifiers, a public filing is a bad place to volunteer the child's school, a home address, a medical detail, or an account number that the motion does not actually need. The discipline is to include only what the relief requires and to keep the rest in your private folder.

**Your address, if disclosing it is unsafe.** A court may, for cause, make identifying information confidential:

> Upon a finding of just cause, on the court's own motion or on motion of the party, the court may order any personal identifying information be made confidential. … If a party's home address or telephone number is made confidential, the order shall designate an alternative address for serving documents on that party …
> — MCR 1.109(D)(9)(b)(vii)

For readers with a safety concern, the address question is handled up front in the pre-filing safety planning (FM.5) and belongs with the safety resources there — including asking the Genesee County Legal Resource Center about Michigan's **Address Confidentiality Program** and about keeping your address out of filings. `ℹ PROVISIONAL — the Address Confidentiality Program's current intake, and the local office details, are confirm-before-relying items; route to FM.5 rather than acting on this paragraph alone.` `🛑 SAFETY OVERRIDE` — if your address is a safety matter, that concern outranks any documentation goal in this module.

- ☐ I have **stopped deleting** messages, emails, photos, and app content on my own devices and accounts — including ones I regret.

## Exercises

1. **One week of entries — first fictional, then your own** *(evidence-log exercise).* Using the template, complete a full week of log entries for the fictional scenario above, then a week of entries from your **own** recent events. *Done when every entry is a single dated event written in facts only, each entry names the order term or factor it maps to, and no entry contains a conclusion, a character adjective, or anything that reached you only through the child.*
2. **Audit ten entries** *(self-assessment).* Take ten entries — your own or the sample — and score each on four axes: **relevance** (does it map to a factor or order term, or is it noise?), **tone** (facts, or editorial?), **corroboration** (what would confirm it?), and **privacy** (does it contain a protected identifier, the child as a source, or something you would not want read aloud?). *Done when each of the ten has a yes/no on all four axes and a one-line fix for every "no."*
3. **Sort the pile — useful / needs corroboration / leave out** *(red-flag spotting).* Given a set of sample entries, sort each into **useful evidence** (dated fact, maps to a factor, points to an admissible source), **needs corroboration** (real but currently only your word — identify the witness or record that would confirm it), or **leave out** (noise, editorializing, catharsis, privacy problem, or anything sourced through the child). *Done when every entry is in exactly one pile and each "needs corroboration" entry names the specific source that would move it to the first pile.*

## Common traps, named

- **Journaling as catharsis.** The notebook is not a diary and not a place to vent. Emotion is real and belongs in the private "emotion" column (Section 8.3), never in the log — because the log can be read by the other side (the MRE 612 trap, Section 8.1), and venting on the page is a cross-examination handed over free.
- **Retroactive reconstruction.** A log built weeks later, or an old entry edited to "improve" it, is worth little and looks worse. Contemporaneous or a dated new entry — never a quiet rewrite (Section 8.2).
- **Privacy violations.** Protected identifiers, the child's identifying details, and unnecessary medical or account information do not belong in public filings (Section 8.6, MCR 1.109(D)(9)) — and never in anything posted or shared online.
- **Documenting by provocation.** Manufacturing incidents to generate screenshots builds a *course of conduct* against you, not evidence (Section 8.4; MCL 750.411h; Module 12). Document what happens; do not arrange for it to happen.

## Stop and seek counsel 🛑

- **Any recording — audio or video — you are thinking of making, keeping, or using.** 🔴 Recording legality is a hard stop routed to **Module 9** and a licensed Michigan attorney (`GUARDRAILS.md` §9). This module preserves only recordings you **already lawfully have**, and it never records, photographs, or sends a device with the child (§8; Section 8.0).
- **Any preservation that would require accessing the other party's phone, email, or accounts, or placing a tracker.** Whether that material was lawfully obtained is outside this guide — account access and trackers route to **Module 9** and counsel (outline §0.4). Do not gather what you cannot lawfully hold.
- **Anything that would use the child as a source, courier, or subject of a recording or a staged photograph.** Not a balancing test — it does not happen (`GUARDRAILS.md` §8; Section 8.0). 🛑
- **A child's disclosure of abuse, or any CPS or police contact surfacing in your records.** Stop the documentation workflow; this routes out (outline §0.4; Module 6's child-hearsay warning). 🛑
- **If documenting, communicating, or preserving intersects with domestic violence or a PPO** — for example, a channel restriction, or evidence that would require contact or account access — safety comes first (**FM.2**, **FM.5**, **Module 17**); do not let a documentation task override a safety plan. 🛑 SAFETY OVERRIDE
