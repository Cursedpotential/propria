## Comprehensive Analysis

The three models converged on a verdict that is unusually unanimous for a council review: **the outline should not go to a drafting model in its present form.** That convergence matters more than any single critique, because the three reached it from different directions. Gemini 3.1 Pro approached it as a topical-completeness audit and found six or seven missing subjects. GPT-5.6 Sol approached it as a systems problem and concluded the outline lacks a procedural control layer entirely. Claude Opus 5 approached it through the evidentiary and procedural-mechanics layer and concluded that roughly 35–40% of the necessary substance is absent — and that the absent portion is disproportionately the part that is irreversible when gotten wrong. Three independent methods producing the same verdict is strong signal.

The high-confidence findings cluster tightly. Every model flagged evidence and admissibility as the largest single gap, and every model located the same root cause: the outline teaches strategy before it teaches proof. A reader can understand perfectly why a fact matters under MCL 722.23(j) and still lose because the text messages proving it never got admitted. All three flagged the subpoena problem, and all three identified the same specific pro se trap — that under MCR 2.305(A)(1) an unrepresented party must move the court for non-party discovery subpoenas rather than issuing them the way an attorney would. All three found the de novo module materially misstated: the outline tells the reader the judge "rehears the matter fresh," but MCR 3.215(F)(2)(c) lets the court bar evidence that was available at the referee hearing, which converts the widespread pro se instinct to "save the good stuff for the judge" into a case-ending error. All three demanded that appeals come back into scope — not to teach appellate briefing, but because a 21-day jurisdictional clock that the guide never mentions is a right lost silently. And all three insisted that clinical vocabulary — "narcissist," "alienator," "PAS" — be banned from every drafted template, because a pro se litigant who opens by diagnosing the other parent is heard as the high-conflict party.

The disagreements are more interesting than the agreements, and two of them are decisions you actually have to make. The first is structural: should the guide keep ICLE's lesson numbering? Gemini says yes — it maps cleanly and preserves provenance. Opus calls it "the outline's central design error," and Sol says the numbering is "editorial provenance value with low user value." The Opus and Sol position is better reasoned for this reader. The ICLE course is organized around what an attorney studies across a practice area; a self-represented parent in crisis navigates by "what happened to me today and what is my deadline." Concretely, the current outline places Foundational Legal Standards at Lesson 7 — after the reader has supposedly drafted motions, mediated, tried the case, and entered judgment — and places procedural survival (how to file, what a praecipe is) at Lesson 6, after five lessons the reader cannot execute without it. That ordering is an artifact of mirroring, and it is backwards. The fix both models propose is the same: reader-facing stages, with the ICLE mapping preserved as a crosswalk appendix so nothing is lost.

The second live disagreement is legal, and it should not be resolved by a model. On recording, Opus grounds the danger in the participant/non-participant line — a participant may lawfully record under *Sullivan v Gray*, and the felony arises when a non-participant records, which is exactly what sending a child into the other parent's home with a recorder is. Gemini asserts a distinct doctrine: an affirmative vicarious-consent prohibition barring a parent from consenting on a minor's behalf, citing *Fisher v Perron*. Sol refuses to state any rule and instead installs a hard stop directing the reader to specific counsel. The three paths converge on the same practical instruction — do not record the child, do not plant devices — but they rest on different legal theories, and the guide will be read by people whose liberty depends on getting this right. Adopt Sol's hard-stop architecture as the default, use Opus's participant/non-participant framing for the explanation, and get the vicarious-consent question confirmed by a Michigan attorney before a single sentence is drafted.

Opus's unique findings carry the most drafting-relevant weight, largely because it went deepest on the layer that actually decides these cases. Three deserve special attention. The Michigan Rules of Evidence apply at referee hearings under MCR 3.215(D)(1) — meaning the proceeding the outline treats as informal is a formal evidentiary hearing and, for most Genesee parents, the real trial. Michigan has no analogue to FRE 902(13)/(14), so there is no self-authentication certificate for digital records and every screenshot requires live testimony under MRE 901; this means the outline's entire texts/OFW/journal strategy currently has no route into evidence. And MRE 803A, the tender-years hearsay exception the reader will inevitably find online, reaches only criminal and juvenile delinquency proceedings — which quietly removes the evidentiary foundation the alienation lesson is implicitly built on. Opus also surfaced the finding I would put on page one: under *Hayes v Hayes*, an established custodial environment is a question of how the child actually lives, regardless of whether it arose from a court order, no order, or a violated order. A parent who signs a "just for now" temporary order giving the other parent primary care may, fourteen months later, have to overcome by clear and convincing evidence an ECE their own signature helped create. Sol's most valuable contribution is different in kind: the 12-point drafting contract and 12 acceptance tests are the most directly reusable artifact any of the three produced, because they convert an outline into a specification a drafting model can be held to. Gemini's contribution is narrower but concrete — the 2025 MCSF manual's reduction from 20 to 18 deviation factors is exactly the kind of currency error that would otherwise propagate from older secondary sources.

**Recommendation.** Rebuild the outline rather than patch it, using Sol's five-part pathway architecture as the spine, Opus's 18-module content map for substance, and Sol's drafting contract and acceptance tests as the gate every module must pass before drafting begins. Sequence the rebuild in this order: pathway triage first, then legal standards, then evidence, then everything else — because evidence determines what the reader must preserve and obtain months before any hearing. Add the six subjects all three models found missing as full modules, not insertions: evidence and admissibility, subpoenas and non-party records, parenting-time enforcement and contempt, emergency ex parte practice (both requesting and responding), change of domicile, and appellate triage with stays. Write the glossary first and use it verbatim throughout — Opus is right that deferring definitions across seven lessons guarantees "de novo," "ECE," and "proper cause" get defined inconsistently. Replace the single VERIFY BEFORE RELYING flag with a tiered system that distinguishes a wrong mediation fact (costs an afternoon) from a wrong objection deadline (costs the case), and apply the top tier to every deadline in the book plus a master deadline table with the consequence of missing each one. Finally, three things should not be settled by any model and need a Michigan family-law attorney before publication: the vicarious-consent recording question, every Genesee County local fact (fees, judicial and referee assignments, FOC procedure, MiFILE status, administrative orders), and any template that a reader might mistake for filing-ready. Sol's rule is the right one — no template gets labeled "filing-ready," and every sample states the procedural posture and facts it assumes.
