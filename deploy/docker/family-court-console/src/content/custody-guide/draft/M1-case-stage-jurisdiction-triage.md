# You cannot pick a strategy until you know which road you are on

Family court is not one process. It is at least **ten different roads**, and each one has its own standard to meet, its own form, its own way of serving the other side, and its own clock. The single most common pro se mistake is not losing an argument — it is **filing the wrong kind of request**, on the wrong road, and having it dismissed or ignored before anyone reaches the merits.

This module does one thing: it tells you which road you are on. Do this before you draft anything.

A word of encouragement, because the list below is long: you are only on **one or two** of these roads at a time. You do not need the other eight. Find yours and move on to the module it points to.

---

## The ten roads

Read down the middle column until you find your situation. Then note the **standard** you will have to meet and the **module** that teaches it.

| Road | You are here if… | The standard that governs it | Go to |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. No case yet** | There is no court case and no order about your child at all | You are asking a court to decide custody/parenting time for the first time — **best interests**, after the court decides the custodial environment | M2, M4 |
| **2. Pending original action** | A case is open (often inside a divorce or paternity case) but no final judgment yet | Temporary relief now; best interests at judgment | M2, M12, M13 |
| **3. Temporary-order stage** | The court is about to enter, or just entered, a "for now" order | ⚠ **Read Module 2 first** — a temporary order can create a custodial environment you later have to overcome | M2, M13 |
| **4. Postjudgment modification** | There is already a final custody order and you want it changed | **Gate first:** proper cause or change of circumstances (*Vodvarka*), *before* the court will even look at best interests | M2, M27 |
| **5. Enforcement** | There is an order and the other parent is not following it | Not a modification — enforcement. Different track, different forms (FOC, contempt, make-up time) | M26 |
| **6. Objection to an ex parte order** | An order was entered without a hearing and served on you | **14 days from service** to object or move to rescind/modify | M14 · 🔴 ACT TODAY |
| **7. Objection to a referee recommendation** | A referee heard your matter and issued a recommendation | **21 days from service** to file a written objection *and* a notice of hearing | M16 · 🔴 ACT TODAY |
| **8. Emergency / PPO / CPS / criminal overlap** | Immediate danger, a PPO, CPS, or police are involved | A different standard entirely, and one this book cannot carry alone | M14, M17 · 🛑 |
| **9. Domicile / interstate** | A parent wants to move, or another state or country is involved | **Jurisdiction** decides everything else — and it may not be Michigan's | M3, M27 · 🛑 on interstate |
| **10. Appeal or stay** | A final order entered and you believe it was legal error | **21 days from entry** to claim an appeal — jurisdictional, no extensions | M28 · 🔴 ACT TODAY |

If two roads seem to fit, you are probably dealing with both at once (for example, an **enforcement** problem that you also want to fix by **modifying** the order). Handle the one with the running clock first.

## Three requests people confuse — and the courts do not

Roads 4, 5, and 8 look similar from the outside ("I want the court to fix my parenting time"), but they are three different legal asks with three different bars. Getting this wrong is the difference between a motion the court must consider and one it can reject on sight. The verbatim standards live in **Module 2**; here is the map:

- **A postjudgment custody motion** must first clear a **gate** — proper cause or a change of circumstances (*Vodvarka v Grasmeyer*) — before a judge will re-open best interests at all. Most pro se modification motions die at this gate. (Module 2.3.)
- **A parenting-time-only request** that does **not** change the established custodial environment is measured by a **lower threshold** (*Shade v Wright*) — normal changes in a child's life can be enough. But if the parenting-time change *would* alter the custodial environment, the higher custody gate snaps back into place. You cannot lower your burden just by calling a custody change a "parenting-time" change. (Module 2.3.)
- **An emergency (ex parte) request** is a different animal again — it asks the court to act *without notice* to the other side, and it must show the kind of immediate, irreparable harm that justifies skipping notice. It is not a shortcut for an ordinary dispute. (Module 14.)

> ⚠ **The trap:** filing the wrong vehicle. A parent who files a "motion to change custody" when what they needed was a parenting-time motion may be held to the *Vodvarka* gate they could have avoided. A parent who files an "emergency" motion over a non-emergency can lose credibility with the judge they will see again. Match the request to the road.

## Before any of this: do you even have standing?

A court can only decide custody for someone whose legal relationship to the child is already established. You do not need to become an expert in this — you need to confirm one thing before you file:

- **Married parents / divorce:** custody is decided inside the divorce or post-divorce case.
- **Unmarried parents:** there must be an **acknowledgment of parentage** or a court **paternity** determination first. Without it, a father may have no standing to ask for custody yet, and establishing parentage is its own step. *(This book does not teach paternity or divorce procedure in full — it flags the prerequisite so you do not file a custody motion the court cannot hear. Module 4 and Appendix K point you onward.)*
- **Non-parents (grandparents, relatives):** custody and "grandparenting time" are narrow, separate doctrines with their own high bars — outside this book's scope. 🛑

If you are unsure whether your parentage is legally established, resolve that question **before** you spend a filing fee on a custody motion.

## The jurisdiction screen — the question that comes before every other question

A Michigan court can only decide your custody case if it has **jurisdiction** — the legal power to hear it. For anything touching another state or country, jurisdiction is governed by a law called the **UCCJEA** (Uniform Child-Custody Jurisdiction and Enforcement Act), and getting it wrong can undo everything you build on top of it.

Screen yourself against these triggers **now**. If any is true, jurisdiction is a live question and you must read **Module 3** before filing — and for most of them, get a lawyer:

- The child has **not lived in Michigan for the last 6 months** (Michigan may not be the "home state"). 🛑
- There is an **existing custody order from another state**. 🛑
- There is a **custody case already open in another state**. 🛑
- You are thinking about **leaving Michigan with the child**, or the other parent has. 🛑
- There is any **Native American / tribal heritage** in the family — a separate law (ICWA / MIFPA) applies. 🛑
- There is an **international** element — a parent, a passport, or a country of origin abroad. 🛑

> **Never solve a jurisdiction problem by moving first.** Taking the child out of state to create a "home state" somewhere else can be treated as exactly the kind of conduct courts punish, and it can hand the other parent an emergency order. If you are considering a move, that is Module 27 and a lawyer, not a suitcase.

**MC 416 is not paperwork.** The court's UCCJEA affidavit — form **MC 416** — is where you swear, under oath, everywhere the child has lived for five years. It is how the court decides whether it even has power to act. Treat it as jurisdictional testimony, not a routine attachment ([MC 416](https://www.courts.michigan.gov/siteassets/forms/scao-approved/mc416.pdf)). You will complete it in Module 3 from the residence chronology you build below.

## Exercise 1 — Classify your road *(self-assessment)*

On one page, answer:

1. **Is there already a final custody order about this child?** Yes → you are on Road 4, 5, 6, 7, or 10 (something already happened — find the clock). No → Road 1, 2, or 3.
2. **Did something get served on you, or did an order/recommendation just come out?** If yes, write down **what** and the **date you were served** — then go straight to FM.0 and the module for that clock. A deadline outranks everything else in this module.
3. **Does any jurisdiction trigger above apply?** If yes, Module 3 first.
4. **Is your parentage legally established?** If unsure, resolve that before filing.

**You are done when** you can write one sentence: *"I am on Road ___; the standard I must meet is ___ (Module 2); the module that teaches my next step is ___; my nearest deadline is ___."*

**You are done when** every period of the last five years is accounted for **with no gaps**, every address and school is dated, and every prior case or order is listed with its court and number. Gaps are what opposing counsel and FOC investigators probe first — close them now, on your own terms.

## Existing-orders inventory *(fill-in tool)*

List every order that currently touches this child — custody, parenting time, support, PPO, or an order from another case. For each: the **court**, the **case number**, the **date entered**, and **what it says in one line**. You cannot ask a court to change or enforce an order you have not put in front of it, and you cannot spot a conflict between two orders you have not laid side by side.

## Stop and seek counsel 🛑 DO NOT USE THIS BOOK ALONE

Get a lawyer — starting with the free and limited-scope options in **FM.4** and **Appendix K** — before you file if any of these is true:

- Any **custody order or open case in another state**.
- Any **tribal / ICWA** indicator.
- Any **international** element.
- Any reason to fear the other parent may **take the child and disappear** (abduction risk).

These are not places to be brave with a book. They are places where a single wrong first step is very hard to undo.
