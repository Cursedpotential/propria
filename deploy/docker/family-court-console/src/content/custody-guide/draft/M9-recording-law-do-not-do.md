# Why this module is different from every other one

Every other module in this book teaches you how to do something. This one is mostly a list of things **not** to do, and one instruction repeated until it sticks:

**The question you actually want answered — *"is my recording legal, and can I use it in my case?"* — is not answered in this book. It goes to a licensed Michigan attorney. Every time. No exception.**

That is not caution for its own sake. Michigan's eavesdropping statute (the MCL 750.539 series) has generated serious, still-unresolved litigation, and getting it wrong is not a civil misstep you can fix later — it can be a **crime**, and the criminal exposure runs toward **the person who made the recording**. That person would be you. A recording made the wrong way does not just get thrown out; it can hand the other side a police report with your name on it, and it can wreck your credibility on **best-interest factor (g)** (your own conduct and judgment) and **factor (j)** (whether you support the child's relationship with the other parent) at the same time.

So this module gives you four things, and withholds one:

- **It gives you** a list of recording ideas that are crimes — so you can recognize and *not do* them.
- **It gives you** a simple way to sort your situation into *stop* or *ask*.
- **It gives you** the questions to take to a lawyer.
- **It gives you** free and low-cost places to find that lawyer (cross-referenced to FM.4 and Module 4.7).
- **It withholds** a "yes." This book will never tell you that a specific recording is lawful. Only a Michigan attorney can do that, on your specific facts.

> ### 🛑 SAFETY OVERRIDE — read before anything else in this module
> If you are recording, tracking, or gathering "proof" because you are **afraid**, safety comes first and it is not a recording problem. Immediate danger → **911**. Michigan's 24-hour DV help and the National Domestic Violence Hotline (**1-800-799-7233**) take priority over every step in this book (**FM.2**, **Module 17**).
>
> **This runs both ways, and that is the point.** The same statutes that make it a crime for you to hide a recorder or plant a tracker are the statutes that protect a victim from being surveilled by an abuser. If **you** are being recorded, followed by a tracking device, or monitored through your phone or accounts, that is a **safety and possibly a PPO matter** — take it to a DV advocate and to law enforcement, not into a self-help evidence project (**FM.2**, **Module 17**). And if an evidence impulse is pulling against your own safety plan, the safety plan wins. `SAFETY OVERRIDE`

## 9.0 The one rule this whole module is built on

You may have read online that **Michigan is a "one-party consent" state**, and concluded that means you can record. **Delete that sentence from your plan.** It is the single most dangerous thing a self-represented parent repeats, for four reasons:

1. Even the most recording-friendly reading of Michigan law only *arguably* protects a recording where you are a **clear adult participant** in the conversation. It says nothing good about hidden devices, other people's homes and cars, or recordings you are not part of.
2. Whether that participant reading is even correct is **genuinely unsettled** — courts and lawyers are still fighting about it (see the three theories below).
3. "Not a crime" is **not** the same as "admissible," "wise," or "helpful." A recording can be lawful and still be inadmissible, privacy-invading, and damaging to how the court sees you.
4. The reading collapses entirely the moment a **child** is involved — and in custody cases, a child is almost always what the parent is tempted to record. That temptation is where this goes from a bad idea to a felony idea.

So the rule is: **this book does not classify any recording as lawful. It sorts recordings into "crime — do not do it" and "stop and ask a lawyer." There is no third bin here.**

> ### ⊗ COUNCIL CONFLICT — THREE LEGAL THEORIES, PRESENTED SIDE BY SIDE
> **The three legal reviewers of this project reached the recording question from three different theories. All three appear here, in order. This book does not choose among them — it operates on the most protective one (Theory 3).** Nothing in this box is a green light.
>
> **Theory 1 — the participant / non-participant line.**
> MCL 750.539c is written in **all-party-consent** terms, but one line of Michigan authority — *Sullivan v Gray*, 117 Mich App 476; 324 NW2d 58 (1982) — reads the word "eavesdrop" to **exclude a participant** to the conversation, which is where the "one-party consent" shorthand comes from. The State Bar's ethics opinion **RI-309** proceeds on the same understanding; the Michigan Supreme Court **declined** to answer a certified question on it in 2021 (MSC docket 162121); and on **March 30, 2026** a federal court (E.D. Mich.) granted summary judgment in *AFT Michigan v Project Veritas*, described in the press as confirming Michigan is one-party-consent **for a participant**, and that a person whose presence is apparent can count as a party whether or not they speak. **⚠ VERIFY — none of these was obtained as archived primary text; the federal opinion was not obtained as an official slip opinion.** **The consequence that is not in doubt under this theory:** *Sullivan* is explicit that a participant's consent does **not** authorize a **third party** to record. So the most common recording idea an alienated parent has — **sending the child in with a phone or a hidden recorder, or planting a device in the other parent's car or home — is non-participant recording, and is felony conduct, not a clever strategy.**
>
> **Theory 2 — an affirmative vicarious-consent prohibition.**
> On this view Michigan is one-party-consent **only for participants** (per *Sullivan* and, federally, *Fisher v Perron*, 30 F.4th 289 (6th Cir. 2022)), **and** there is a separate, affirmative prohibition on **vicarious consent**: **a parent cannot consent on the child's behalf to record the child's conversations with the other parent.** Under this theory, telling a pro se parent to "just record" without this caveat can walk them straight into a felony eavesdropping charge. **⚠ VERIFY — this is the exact point reserved to a licensed Michigan attorney; see the 🔒 block below.**
>
> **Theory 3 — the unsettled-law hard stop (this book's default).**
> This theory declines to state a rule at all. The statute speaks of the permission of **all** persons; the question has drawn serious litigation; **MCL 750.539d separately restricts devices used to observe or record in a private place**; and a call that **crosses state lines** can trigger another state's law, some of which require **everyone's** consent. The move here is not to summarize the rule but to impose a **stop-and-get-specific-advice gate** for: hidden devices; recordings in another person's home or car; calls crossing state lines; **recording a child**; and any recording that touches a PPO, a criminal allegation, CPS, or a therapy, school, or medical setting. **⚠ VERIFY — MCL 750.539a/539c/539d and *Lewis v LeGrow* were not obtained as archived primary text.**
>
> **Where all three converge — the instruction that is NOT in doubt:** do **not** record the child; do **not** send a child in wearing or carrying a recorder; do **not** plant devices; do **not** record conversations you are not part of.

> ### ⚠ OPEN — added 2026-09-07 (council review finding): the child's OWN device, incidentally
> **This is a distinct scenario from the absolute child rule above, and it is genuinely open, not resolved by that rule.** The absolute rule in §9.2/§9.2's `🛑 THE CHILD IS NEVER A RECORDING TARGET` box bars the **reader** from using, directing, or arranging for the child to record — that instruction has no exceptions and is unaffected by anything in this box.
>
> What that rule does **not** answer: **what happens when the child's own device — a phone, tablet, smart speaker, or laptop the child owns and operates on their own initiative — incidentally records a parent, with no direction, request, or arrangement by the reader?** Example: the child's phone is recording a video for a friend, or has an always-listening assistant active, and a parent's conversation is picked up in the background; the reader did not cause it, does not know it happened until later, and then has to decide what to do with it.
>
> This raises **Michigan one-party-consent doctrine under MCL 750.539c/539d** on facts this module has not analyzed: **whose consent counts when the recording device is the child's, operated by the child, and the child is arguably a "participant" in whatever the device captured?** The **vicarious-consent doctrine is unsettled in Michigan** even for the ordinary parent-directs-recording pattern (Theory 2 above); it is **more unsettled still**, and not analyzed anywhere in this project's sources, for a child's own device acting on the child's own initiative. `⚠ VERIFY — no toolkit source addresses this fact pattern; it is not merely UNVERIFIED, it is UNANALYZED.`
>
> **This is an attorney question, never a self-serve one.** Do not reason by analogy from the parent-directed rule above to conclude either that incidental capture is automatically fine (the "the child did it, not me" theory) or automatically criminal (the "child recordings are always barred" theory) — this module does not know, and neither theory is supported by anything in the archived sources. If this fact pattern arises, preserve the material exactly as found (§9.3's "if a recording already exists" rules apply), do not review, copy, or use it, and take **this specific fact pattern** — a child's own device, the child's own initiative, no parental direction — to a licensed Michigan attorney as its own question, distinct from "can I record" and distinct from "can I have my child record."

## 9.1 The Recording-Law Stoplight

Use this to sort a recording *idea* before you act on it. Notice what is missing.

- **🟢 GREEN — "this is fine."** **This book cannot hand you a green light, and it will not pretend to.** A green light depends on verified facts about who was present, where it happened, and whose law applies — and, per the 🔒 block above, on an attorney's sign-off that does not yet exist. Until a licensed Michigan attorney gives you a green light on *your* facts, **treat green as unavailable.**
- **🟡 YELLOW — "stop and ask."** This is the **default** for anything not already RED — including a call you are personally on. Yellow does not mean "probably okay." It means **do not act until a Michigan attorney tells you where this actually falls.** Most real situations live here.
- **🔴 RED — "do not do this."** A recording lands in RED — a likely **crime**, off the table entirely — whenever it involves any of:
  - a **hidden device** (recorder, camera, "nanny cam" aimed outward, phone left running);
  - **another person's home or vehicle**;
  - **recording a child**, or **sending a child** anywhere with a recording device;
  - a conversation you are **not a participant in**;
  - a **tracking device** on a vehicle;
  - a call that **crosses state lines**;
  - any overlap with a **PPO, a criminal allegation, CPS, or a therapy, school, or medical** setting.

If your idea touches a single RED bullet, you already have your answer: **no.** If it touches none of them, it is still **YELLOW**, not green — you stop and ask.

## 9.2 The Do-Not-Do List

> ### ⚠ THE DO-NOT-DO LIST — these are crimes, not evidence strategies
> Each row is a foreseeable idea that a parent under stress has, and a reason it is off the table. **Statutory details marked `⚠ VERIFY` are stated in substance from secondary/mirror sources and must be confirmed against the primary statute before anyone relies on them — but the instruction in each row does not change: do not do it.**
>
> | The idea | Why it is off the table |
> |---|---|
> | Recording a conversation you are **not part of** — **including sending your child in with a recorder, or a hidden recorder in a backpack, coat, or toy** | Non-participant recording. Charged as felony eavesdropping under **MCL 750.539c** on the *Sullivan* participant reading; a participant's consent does **not** cover a third party. `⚠ VERIFY` the penalty (recorded elsewhere as up to **2 years and/or $2,000**) against MCL 750.539c. Also violates the child rule below, flat. |
> | Installing or placing a **recording or observation device in a private place** — the other parent's home or vehicle | Prohibited-place device use under **MCL 750.539d**. `⚠ VERIFY` the text and the definition of "private place" (MCL 750.539a) against the primary statute. |
> | Putting a **GPS unit or AirTag-style tracker** on the other parent's car | The MJI Domestic Violence Benchbook records this as a **misdemeanor crime** under **MCL 750.539l(1)(a)–(b)** — tracking, installing, or placing a tracking device on a vehicle "without the owner's or lessee's knowledge and consent" — and adds civil liability: see the verbatim block below. `⚠ VERIFY` the exact wording, exceptions (MCL 750.539l(2)), and penalty against legislature.mi.gov. |
> | Logging into the other parent's **email, phone, cloud, or social-media account** with a remembered, shared, or guessed password | Unlawful access. It is the **most common** pro se evidence-gathering method and one of the most destructive — it can be a separate crime, it taints the evidence, and it hands the other side a credibility weapon. |
> | Reading the other parent's **mail** | Separate federal and state exposure. |
> | Using the **child** as an intelligence asset — interrogating, tasking, wiring, or debriefing | Damages **factor (j)**, harms the child, and violates this project's absolute child rule (below). Not a balancing test. |
>
> **What every one of these costs, stated plainly:** a possible **criminal charge**; the evidence **excluded** anyway; your **credibility destroyed**; **factor (g)** and **factor (j)** damage; and, where a PPO or DV context exists, **PPO exposure of your own.**

The one place this module can quote the statute **verbatim** from an archived primary source is the tracking-device prohibition and its civil-liability tail — because a parent tempted to "just track the car to prove she's lying about where the kids are" needs to see it in the statute's own words:

> "A person who illegally installs or uses a tracking device . . . is liable for all damages incurred by the owner or lessee of the motor vehicle caused by the installation or use of the tracking device."
> — MCL 750.539l(4), as quoted verbatim in the MJI Domestic Violence Benchbook (4th ed.). The Benchbook further records that MCL 750.539l "makes it a misdemeanor crime for a person to track, install, or place (or cause the installation or placement) of a tracking device on a vehicle without the owner's or lessee's knowledge and consent" (MCL 750.539l(1)(a)–(b)), subject to exceptions in MCL 750.539l(2). `⚠ VERIFY the current statutory text and penalty against legislature.mi.gov before relying on it in a filing.`

> ### 🛑 THE CHILD IS NEVER A RECORDING TARGET — this one has no exceptions
> **You do not record the child. You do not record the child's calls or video chats with the other parent. You do not send the child anywhere carrying or wearing a device, and you do not ask, hint, or arrange for the child to capture anything.** This is not weighed against how badly you need proof; there is no fact that turns it green (GUARDRAILS §8; and the vicarious-consent problem in Theory 2). It harms the child, it is likely a felony, and it is exactly the conduct **factor (j)** is written to punish. The child is not your witness, your courier, or your recorder. If someone is telling you to do this, that is a **stop-and-get-counsel** moment, not a plan (**Module 8**, **Module 18**). `DO NOT USE THIS BOOK ALONE`

## 9.3 If a recording already exists

Maybe you are not planning anything — you **already** have something. A voicemail the other parent left. A video they posted publicly. A recording you made a year ago before you read this. The rules change from "do not" to "**do not touch it, and do not assume it helps** — take it to a lawyer."

- **Do not delete it.** If a recording might be evidence in a proceeding, destroying it can be its own problem. Preserve it exactly as it is and ask counsel. Do not edit, trim, or "clean it up."
- **"I made it" does not mean "I can use it."** Whether it was **lawfully obtained** is precisely the unsettled, criminal-exposure question this whole module refuses to answer. That goes to the attorney *before* it goes anywhere near a filing or a hearing.
- **Even a lawful recording still has to get *in*.** A recording is not admissible just because it is real. It must be **authenticated** under **MRE 901** — Module 7 owns that, and there is no certificate shortcut in Michigan; you would authenticate it by identifying the voice from live testimony. And its content runs through the hearsay analysis in **Module 6**: a recording **of the other parent** is a party-opponent statement (**MRE 801(d)(2)**) and is *not hearsay* when offered against them — but that rule tells you the statement can come in, **not** that the recording was lawfully made. Two different questions; do not let one answer the other.
- **Some judges react badly to recordings of children even when they are lawful.** A recording that gets admitted can still make *you* look like the problem. That is a strategic reason, on top of every legal one, to route this through counsel rather than spring it.
- **The hand-me-down phone / synced account trap.** If you are using an old device the other parent once used, or a shared or family account, and **their** content starts appearing — messages, photos, backups syncing in — **do not open, search, export, or screenshot it.** Preserve the state of the device and ask counsel how to handle it. If a **child** volunteered something on a device, do not coach any further collection; ask counsel about lawful preservation.

The safe path for anything that already exists is the decision tree in the tools below — which ends, every time, at a lawyer.
