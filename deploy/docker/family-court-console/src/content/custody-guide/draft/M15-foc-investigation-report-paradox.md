# Why this module matters

At some point in a contested Genesee County custody or parenting-time case, a stranger with a caseload will interview you, interview the other parent, gather records, and write a document that recommends what the judge should do with your child. That document is the **Friend of the Court (FOC) report and recommendation.** It will feel like the most important thing in your case.

It is important. But it is important in a way almost nobody understands until it is too late, and the misunderstanding runs in **two opposite directions**:

- A parent who gets a **favorable** report relaxes, stops gathering proof, and shows up to the hearing expecting the recommendation to carry them. It cannot. The report is **not evidence**, and it does not satisfy the burden you still have to meet.
- A parent who gets an **unfavorable** report panics, treats it as a verdict, and either gives up or agrees — often without realizing it — to let the report *become* evidence against them.

Both mistakes come from the same missing idea. This module supplies it: **the FOC report is considered by the court but is not evidence unless both parties agree to make it evidence.** That single rule is the paradox in the title, and it drives everything from how you sit through the interview to whether you ever say the words "we'll stipulate to the report."

This module is the setup's payoff. Module 5 §5.4 placed the FOC report on the evidence map — outside the rules of evidence for the court's *consideration*, but not evidence for the court's *findings*. Here we take that apart completely: what the investigation is, how it gathers information without the rules of evidence, what the report can and cannot do, and the right you have to **review it and object before the judge decides.**

## 15.1 What the Friend of the Court is — and what it is not

The Friend of the Court is an office of the circuit court that assists the court in domestic-relations matters. Three things about it are worth fixing in your mind before anything else:

- **It is not your lawyer.** It does not represent you, the other parent, or the child. Educational material on the FOC Act states the point plainly: the FOC "assists the circuit court in covered domestic matters; it is not either party's lawyer." (Friend of the Court Act, MCL 552.501 *et seq.* `ℹ PROVISIONAL` — the FOC Act's operative pin-level text was not independently verified from primary this pass; this general-role statement is drawn from the project's FOC-Act reference note. Confirm before quoting a specific subsection.)
- **It is not the judge.** The FOC investigates and *recommends*. Only a judge (or, at a referee hearing, a referee subject to review) decides. A recommendation is an opinion the court is free to adopt, modify, or reject.
- **It is not neutral toward you personally, and it is not your enemy.** It is neutral toward the *case*. Treat it as an institution whose written product will be read by the judge, and whose author you cannot cross-examine unless you take specific steps.

**What the FOC does.** In a typical Genesee case the office may: **investigate** custody and parenting-time disputes and write a report with a recommendation; **enforce** existing orders (parenting-time and support complaints); and **administer support** — collecting, disbursing, and accounting. Some functions it performs in-house; others it refers out. Which staff member does what, and how you reach them, is local practice.
`ℹ LOCAL PRACTICE — may have changed` (Genesee FOC office location, phone, hours, staffing, and which functions are handled in-house versus referred out — confirm with the FOC and the clerk; see FM.4. Genesee-specific procedure in this module is `ℹ PROVISIONAL`.)

**Where to point which question.** Questions about your *account* — support balances, payment history, enforcement status — go to the FOC's support/enforcement side. Questions about a *contested custody or parenting-time issue* are case-specific and are decided by the court, not answered by FOC staff as advice. FOC staff, like clerks, give **legal information, not legal advice**: they cannot tell you what to do (cross-ref Module 4.7).

**The case questionnaire.** The FOC typically opens its work by collecting basic information on a case questionnaire — the SCAO form is **FOC 39** (Friend of the Court Case Questionnaire). Complete the official form; do not recreate it, and do not treat the questionnaire as your chance to argue the case. It is intake, not advocacy.
`ℹ LOCAL PRACTICE` — confirm the current FOC 39 revision and whether Genesee uses a local intake form; link the official SCAO form rather than any copy.

> ### ℹ FOC records are confidential — and cannot be reached by an ordinary subpoena
> This cuts two ways, so learn it now. Friend of the Court records are protected:
>
> > "Friend of the court records are not subject to a subpoena issued under these Michigan Court Rules."
> > — MCR 3.218(A)
>
> That means (a) you generally **cannot** subpoena the FOC's internal file to build your case, and (b) sensitive things you tell the FOC live in a file with defined access rules rather than in the open court record. Access to FOC records is governed by MCR 3.218, and specific categories (staff notes, information from a protective-services referral, mediation records, communications from a minor) are treated as confidential. If you need something the FOC holds, the route is MCR 3.218's access provisions, not a subpoena. (Getting *other* records — school, medical, the other parent's messages — into evidence is Modules 6, 7, and 10; the FOC file is a special case.)

## 15.2 Opting in or out of FOC services

**The default is that a Friend of the Court case opens.** For most parents, an FOC case comes with the territory of a custody, parenting-time, or support matter. Michigan law does provide a narrow path to keep an FOC case from opening, or to close one — but "opt out" is a loose label for something more conditional than it sounds.

The governing statute is **MCL 552.505a**, and its actual title is "**Open friend of the court case; closure**" — not "opt-out." Its mechanism, as verified for this project:

- With the **initial pleadings**, the parties may move for an order that **no FOC case be opened**.
- Parties to an **existing** case may move to **close** it.
- Either motion is **defeated** by an enumerated list of conditions — including that a party or child receives (or has applied for) public assistance or Title IV-D services; that **either party objects**; that there is **evidence of domestic violence or of an uneven bargaining position between the parties**; or that a party has not filed the required signed acknowledgment that they understand the services being given up.

(MCL 552.505a. **Disposition: substance ✅ VERIFIED** from the ledger's read of `legislature.mi.gov` [ledger §1]; **exact subsection wording to confirm** before it is quoted in a filing — the section was not archived as primary text this pass.)

**What opting out actually costs.** Closing or forgoing an FOC case means giving up a **neutral investigator** and a **built-in enforcer**. In a low-conflict, cooperative case, that can be a feature — less institutional involvement, more privacy, fewer fees. In a **high-conflict** case it is usually a serious trade:

- You lose the FOC's parenting-time and support **enforcement** machinery, which means enforcement falls to you, by motion, every time.
- You lose the **neutral investigation** — which, paradoxically, can hurt the parent who expected the investigation to validate them *and* the parent who feared it.
- Note the **symmetry** built into the statute: the domestic-violence and uneven-bargaining conditions exist so that a party cannot be pressured into waiving FOC services, and **either party's objection** defeats the motion. You cannot force the other side out, and they cannot force you out.

Because this is a real decision with consequences in both directions, treat it as a decision, not a default — the opt-in-versus-opt-out worksheet below is built for exactly that.

## 15.3 The custody / parenting-time investigation — how it gathers information *outside* the rules of evidence

Here is the mechanism that makes the whole paradox possible. **The FOC investigation is not a hearing.** No one is sworn in the way a witness is at trial. The investigator is **not bound by the Michigan Rules of Evidence** while gathering information. That is precisely why Module 5 mapped the FOC report to the "rules step aside" column — and precisely why a favorable report is not proof.

**What the investigator does.** The investigator gathers information from many sources and weighs them without an evidentiary filter: interviews with each parent; sometimes an interview with or observation of the child; statements from third parties (teachers, relatives, providers); and records (school, medical, police, protective-services referrals). Much of this would be **hearsay** — inadmissible at a hearing without a foundation (Module 6) — yet the investigator may consider it, because the investigation is not the hearing. The report is the investigator's synthesis of all of it, ending in a recommendation.

**Whether the FOC must address the established custodial environment.** A common assumption is that the FOC report will decide, or at least analyze, the ECE (Module 2). It is **not statutorily required to do so**, though a judge may request it. Do not build your strategy on the expectation that the report will resolve the ECE question for you; that question is the court's, decided on hearing evidence.
`ℹ PROVISIONAL` — the "not required, but a judge may request it" point is drawn from Michigan Judicial Institute investigation material (secondary); it was not verified against a controlling statute or rule this pass. The SCAO Custody and Parenting Time Investigation Manual describes the investigation process and is the official practice reference.

**Preparing for your interview.** The interview is where a self-represented parent most often helps or hurts themselves. Some durable principles:

- **Be specific, dated, and neutral.** Describe conduct and its effect on the child, tied to the same statutory factors the court uses (Module 2). "On three listed dates the exchange did not happen and the child missed the activity" beats "she's an alienator." Plead conduct; do not diagnose.
- **Do not use the banned labels.** "Narcissist," "abuser," "alienator," "gaslighting," "parental alienation" — these read as instability and advocacy, not information, and Michigan has no "parental alienation" cause of action anyway (Modules 2 and 18). The investigator is assessing *you* too.
- **Bring organized, sourced documentation — but do not hand over your journal wholesale.** A curated packet that maps facts to factors helps; dumping a raw grievance journal invites the investigator to read *you* as the problem and can expose material you would not choose to put in front of the court (Module 8 explains why the journal is a lead-generator, not an exhibit).
- **What never to say:** anything you would not want quoted in a document the judge will read; anything admitting a violation of an existing order; anything about recording the other parent or the child (Module 9 — this is a hard stop); and anything you are guessing at. "I don't know" is a complete answer.
- **The child is not your instrument.** You do not coach, script, rehearse, or debrief the child for the investigator's interview. If the investigator interviews the child, that belongs to the investigator. (This is absolute — see the guardrail on the child, and Module 8.)

> ### 🛑 SAFETY OVERRIDE — the FOC interview in a domestic-violence context
> If there is domestic violence, a PPO, or a safety concern in your case, the investigation is a moment to protect, not just to prepare:
>
> - **Your address.** You are not required to expose a confidential residence to satisfy an intake form. Michigan's rules contemplate an FOC address of record rather than your physical location (see the address provisions discussed in Modules 12–13), and FOC records are confidential (MCR 3.218). Ask the FOC and, if you have one, a domestic-violence advocate how to handle location safely **before** the interview.
> - **Fleeing is not concealment.** A custodial parent's temporary residence with the child **in a domestic-violence shelter is not evidence of an intent to retain or conceal** the child — this is written into the parenting-time statute (MCL 722.27a(7), quoted in Module 2.5). State it plainly if it is raised against you.
> - **Safety planning comes first** (FM.5), and nothing in this module overrides a PPO, a no-contact order, or law-enforcement direction. If the investigation would require contact or disclosure that a safety order forbids, the safety order wins — raise the conflict with counsel and the FOC. (Cross-ref Module 17.)

## 15.4 The FOC report paradox

This is the section the whole module exists for. Read it slowly.

### The two rules that seem to conflict

**Rule one — the evidence rules do not apply to the court's *consideration* of the report.** This is why "Domestic Relations Matters" appears on the list of exceptions in MRE 1101(b):

> "(9) Domestic Relations Matters. The court's consideration of a report or recommendation submitted by the friend of the court under MCL 552.505(1)(g) or (h)."
> — MRE 1101(b)(9)

Read it narrowly. Only the court's **consideration of the FOC report** is outside the rules. Your contested hearing is not. (Module 5 §5.1–5.2 maps where the rules *do* apply — the referee hearing and the trial, in full.)

**Rule two — the report is not evidence unless both parties agree.** The leading case draws the line the rest of this module is built on:

> "The FOC's report and recommendation is not admissible as evidence unless both parties agree to admit it in evidence. However, the report may be considered by the trial court as an aid to understanding the issues to be resolved. The trial court's ultimate findings relative to custody must be based upon competent evidence adduced at the hearing. Thus, while the FOC's report and recommendation may not form the basis for the trial court's findings, it may be used to establish a background and context for the proceedings."
> — *Duperon v Duperon*, 175 Mich App 77; 437 NW2d 318 (1989)

And a second holding worth knowing: there is **no mandate that the court consider the report at all.** *Duperon* rejected the argument that earlier authority *required* the court to consider FOC reports — "We do not read *Hoffman* as establishing a blanket mandate that the trial court consider FOC reports."

`⚠ VERIFY` — the *Duperon* passages above are quoted as recorded in the verification ledger §4.2, where the operative language was read across three convergent secondary mirrors; the **official-reporter cross-check is still open.** Confirm against the bound reporter before quoting in a filing.

### How the two rules fit — the paradox in three prongs

They do not actually conflict. "Consider" and "admit as evidence" are different acts, and the distinction is the whole game:

1. **The report is not evidence** — not for you, not against you — *unless both parties agree to admit it.* So an **unfavorable** report is not proof against you, and a **favorable** report carries **none** of the burden you still have to meet with real evidence at the hearing.
2. **The court may still consider it** as **background, context, and an aid to understanding** the issues. That is how it coexists with MRE 1101(b)(9): *consideration* is permitted without the evidence rules; *consideration is not the same as evidence.*
3. **The findings must rest on competent evidence from the hearing.** A decision that simply **adopts** the report's findings, without independent hearing evidence to support them, is error.

### The teeth behind prong three

Prong three is not theoretical. Where parties did **not** stipulate the FOC report into evidence and the court nonetheless adopted the FOC's findings instead of reaching its own conclusion from the hearing, Michigan appellate authority has called that reversible:

> where the parties "did not stipulate to allowing the friend of the court's recommendations or report into evidence" and the trial court "reviewed and adopted the friend of the court's findings" rather than arriving at an independent conclusion — "This is a clear legal error which requires reversal and a new hearing."
> — *Truitt v Truitt*, quoting *Marshall v Beal*, 158 Mich App 582, 591 (1986)

The same principle appears in the support context: a court "may not accord evidentiary weight to the facts stated in the report of the friend of the court, unless otherwise stipulated to by the parties, although the report may be used as an evaluative aid." (*Pellar v Pellar*, 443 NW2d 427 (1989), citing *Jacobs v Jacobs*, 118 Mich App 16 (1982).)

`⚠ VERIFY` — *Truitt*, *Marshall v Beal*, *Pellar*, and *Jacobs* are quoted as recorded in the ledger §4.2 and were not independently read from the official reporters this pass. Carry the same cross-check as *Duperon*.

### The three practical rules

Everything above reduces to three instructions that discipline your conduct:

**1. A favorable report does not carry your burden. Put in your proofs anyway.** The recommendation may make you feel safe. It is not evidence, and the court's findings must rest on what is admitted at the hearing. Build the Best-Interest Evidence Matrix (Module 2) and prove your case as if the report did not exist.

**2. Do not stipulate an unfavorable report into evidence.** This is the default discipline of this guide, and the reason is exact: stipulating converts a *not-evidence* document into evidence **for and against you both** — and it surrenders the *Duperon* protections. Keep the report out, and it cannot form the basis of the findings; if a recommendation or order then tracks the report while the hearing record does not independently support it, that mismatch is an objection to raise and an error to preserve. **That preserved argument exists only if you did not agree to admit the report.**

**3. You have the right to review the report and object before the decision.** Michigan court rule guarantees it — the next subsection is entirely about that right and its clock.

> ### ⚠ The words "we'll stipulate to the FOC report" give away the paradox
> **Placement: read this before any hearing where the report might come up. Cross-referenced from Module 5 §5.4.**
>
> The report begins life as a *not-evidence* document. To **stipulate** is to agree, on the record, to admit it — and that agreement is the one thing that turns the report into evidence. Once it is evidence, it is evidence **against** you as much as **for** you, and the *Duperon* argument (findings must rest on hearing evidence, not on the report) is gone.
>
> There are situations where admitting a favorable report by agreement is a considered tactical choice — but it is a choice with a cost, and it is not the default. **The default is: do not stipulate.** If the other side proposes it, or the court asks whether the parties agree to admit the report, understand that you are being asked to change the rules of your own case. `⚠ TIME-CRITICAL` if asked at a hearing — you may have seconds to answer; know the answer now.

### The review-and-object right, and its clock

Your right to see the report and object to it before the judge rules is written into the court rule that governs custody hearings:

> "If a report has been submitted by the friend of the court, the court must give the parties an opportunity to review the report and to file objections before a decision is entered."
> — MCR 3.210(C)(6)

Read the clock precisely, because this is where parents get hurt. **The rule states no fixed number of days.** The deadline is functional: *before a decision is entered.* What bounds that window are the surrounding timing rules of the same subrule:

- In a contested matter that would change the established custodial environment, "the court may not enter an order resolving the contested matter that changes a child's established custodial environment without first holding an evidentiary hearing," and "a hearing on the matter must be held within 56 days" (MCR 3.210(C)(1));
- "The court must enter a decision within 28 days after the hearing" (MCR 3.210(C)(3)); and
- "The court may extend for good cause the time within which a hearing must be held and a decision rendered under this subrule" (MCR 3.210(C)(7)).

So the practical window to object can be **very short** — the report may arrive shortly before or even at the hearing, and the decision can follow within 28 days (sooner if the court rules from the bench). The safe posture is to treat the arrival of the report as the trigger and **object the moment you have grounds**, not on some assumed day-count.

> ### ⚠ Do NOT confuse this right with the two 21-day clocks near it
> Three FOC-adjacent objection tracks look alike and are **not** the same. Confusing them is a classic self-represented error:
>
> | What you received | Which right / clock | Owned by |
> |---|---|---|
> | An **FOC investigation report** feeding a judge's custody/PT decision | Review and object **"before a decision is entered"** — **no fixed statewide day-count** (MCR 3.210(C)(6)) | **This module (15)** |
> | A **referee's recommended order** after a referee *hearing* | Written objection within **21 days after the recommendation is served** (MCR 3.215(E)) → judicial de novo hearing | **Module 16** |
> | An **FOC-office motion to modify a parenting-time order** that arrives **with** a report and a boldface objection notice | Object within **21 days after the notice was sent** (MCL 552.517d) — see the trap box below | Below (15.4) |
>
> The review-and-object right in this module is **not** a 21-day clock. Do not wait 21 days on an investigation report because you read "21 days" somewhere — that number belongs to the other two tracks.
> `⚠ TIME-CRITICAL` — because the true window is "before the decision," which may be days. `ℹ PROVISIONAL` — how Genesee FOC actually delivers the report, and whether the report's own cover page states a local objection window, must be confirmed with the FOC/clerk; **read the notice on the report you received.**

### A companion trap — a single fact can slip in even without a full stipulation

The "not evidence unless both agree" rule protects the report as a **whole**. But in one specific situation, a **single statement of fact** from the report can be admitted if you let it — a waiver-by-silence trap worth knowing.

When the **FOC office itself files a motion to modify a parenting-time order** (available after a final judgment in an open FOC case with an unresolved parenting-time dispute), the statute both sets a hard 21-day clock and opens this narrow door:

> "(1) ... With a motion filed and each notice sent under this subsection, the office shall include the following: ... (b) ... a notice, in not less than 12-point, boldfaced type, that states substantially the following: 'A party may object to the office of the friend of the court's recommendation for modification of the parenting time order. If a party does not object to the recommendation within 21 days after this notice was sent to the party, the office of the friend of the court may submit to the court a parenting time order that incorporates the recommendation.'"
>
> "(4) At a hearing on a motion filed under this section, the judge or referee may admit a statement of fact in the office's report or recommendation as evidence to prove a fact relevant to the proceeding, but only if all parties stipulate to or no party objects to the admission of the statement of fact and no other evidence is presented concerning the fact to be proved."
> — MCL 552.517d(1)(b), (4)

Two lessons, one narrow and one general:

- **Narrow (this specific motion):** if the FOC files a §17d motion to modify your parenting time, you have **21 days from when the notice was sent** to object, or the office may submit an order adopting its recommendation (MCL 552.517d(3)). And at any hearing on that motion, a fact stated in the report **can** come in — but *only* if no one objects **and** no other evidence addresses that fact.
- **General (the caution it illustrates):** silence can admit what a stipulation would. Even outside §17d, do not assume that declining to stipulate the whole report protects every fact in it. When a specific fact from the report is offered, and it matters, **object and put on your own evidence about that fact** — do not let it stand unaddressed.

Dec. 1, 2002), fetched this pass; **to be archived to `sources/primary/mcl/` and confirmed against the current compilation before publication.** Note the scope: §17d governs an **FOC-initiated parenting-time-modification motion**, not the general custody investigation report.

### The same "review and object" structure reaches evaluator reports

If the court uses an outside professional — a custody evaluator or other community resource — the court rule gives you a parallel right to object before it decides:

> "If there is a dispute involving custody, visitation, or change of domicile, and the court uses a community resource to assist its determination, the court must assure that copies of the written findings and recommendations of the resource are provided to the friend of the court and to the attorneys of record for the parties, or the parties if they are not represented by counsel. The attorneys for the parties, or the parties if they are not represented by counsel, may file objections to the report before a decision is made."
> — MCR 3.219 (Dissemination of a Professional Report)

The same non-admissibility-without-agreement logic is generally understood to apply to a **Lawyer-Guardian ad Litem's written report** as well (the report is not evidence absent stipulation), a point the outline routes to MCL 722.24(3).
`ℹ PROVISIONAL` — the LGAL-report non-admissibility point is drawn from an SCAO checklist and was not verified from the statute this pass; MCR 3.219's text above **is** verified from the archived rules. Treat the professional-report right (MCR 3.219) as a companion to MCR 3.210(C)(6), not as a substitute for advice on any particular evaluator or LGAL report.

## 15.5 Objecting to the FOC report — and why it is not the same as objecting to a referee

The right in MCR 3.210(C)(6) is a right to **file objections to the report before the judge decides.** It is *not* the objection-to-a-referee's-recommendation procedure (that is the 21-day MCR 3.215 track in **Module 16**), and it is not a stipulation. It is your written statement to the judge saying: *here is why this report should not drive the decision, and here is what the hearing evidence actually shows.*

Because the report is not evidence, your objection is not really "the report is wrong" — it is **"the findings must rest on hearing evidence, and here is where the report departs from it."** Effective objections are **specific**. A vague "I object to the whole report" gives the court nothing to act on. Aim each objection at one of a few concrete defects:

- **Unsupported by competent evidence.** The report states a finding that no admissible hearing evidence supports. (This is the *Duperon* point in operation.)
- **Source problem.** The finding rests on hearsay, on one side's say-so, or on a source the investigator never tested — identify the source and its weakness.
- **Methodology problem.** The investigator did not interview a key person, ignored records you provided, or applied the wrong standard (for example, treating a parenting-time question as a custody question, or skipping the ECE analysis where the judge asked for it).
- **Omission.** The report leaves out dated, documented facts that bear directly on a best-interest factor — and you can prove them at the hearing.

For each objection, the discipline is the same three-part move you will use again in Module 16: **name the finding → state precisely why it is unsupported or wrong → point to the record or the exhibit that shows it.** The report-analysis matrix below is built to generate exactly these.

> ### The decision tree — "The FOC report just landed. What now?"
> Walk this the day you receive it. Do not improvise.
>
> - **Step 1 — Read it against the clock.** Is a hearing or decision imminent? The right to object runs *before the decision is entered* (MCR 3.210(C)(6)); the decision can come within 28 days of the hearing (C)(3)). Check the report's cover page for any stated objection window, and confirm with the FOC/clerk. `⚠ TIME-CRITICAL`
> - **Step 2 — Is it favorable or unfavorable?**
>   - *Favorable* → **still prove your case.** The report is not evidence and does not carry your burden. Do **not** offer to stipulate it in just because you like it, without weighing the cost (it becomes evidence against you too). Build your proofs (Module 2 matrix).
>   - *Unfavorable* → **do not stipulate it in.** Preserve the *Duperon* argument. Go to Step 3.
> - **Step 3 — Find the departures from competent evidence.** For each harmful finding, ask: *what admissible hearing evidence supports this?* Where the answer is "none," or "only the other parent's word," or "a record no one authenticated," that finding is an objection target.
> - **Step 4 — Draft specific written objections.** Name each finding, state why it is unsupported, and cite your rebuttal exhibit or the missing evidence. File before the decision.
> - **Step 5 — Prepare to prove it at the hearing.** The objection preserves the issue; the *hearing* is where the competent evidence goes in. Authentication (Module 7) and hearsay answers (Module 6) still govern there.
> - **If what you received is a *referee's recommended order* (not an investigation report), or an FOC §17d motion → different clocks (21 days). Go to Module 16 / §15.4's clock table.**

*Done when every harmful finding has a row, a defect type, and either a rebuttal exhibit with an admissibility route or an identified piece of missing evidence.* This feeds both your written objection (15.5) and your hearing proofs.

**4. Draft-objection aid.** For each objection: *(a)* the exact finding objected to; *(b)* the reason it is unsupported by competent evidence or otherwise wrong; *(c)* the record cite or exhibit that shows it; *(d)* the relief — that the court not rest its decision on that finding and instead decide on the hearing evidence. **Do not use if** what you actually received is a referee's recommended order (use Module 16's FOC 68 objection track and its 21-day clock) or an FOC §17d motion (21-day statutory clock).

**5. Opt-in vs. opt-out decision worksheet.** Two columns — *keep the FOC case* / *forgo or close it* — scored on: enforcement need, conflict level, safety, privacy, cost, and whether any statutory condition (public assistance, the other party's objection, DV/uneven bargaining) would defeat the motion anyway (MCL 552.505a). *Done when you can state, in one sentence, the concrete thing you gain and the concrete thing you give up.*

**6. FOC contact card.** Office, phone, hours, and which desk handles account/enforcement versus case questions. `ℹ LOCAL PRACTICE` / `ℹ PROVISIONAL` — fill from the FOC directly; treat anything older than 180 days as stale.

## Exercises

1. **Prepare for the FOC interview using the prep sheet *(fill-in / self-assessment).*** Complete the interview preparation sheet for your own case: for at least six best-interest or parenting-time factors, enter the dated conduct and the backing document, and fill the "things I will not say" box. *Done when every entry is a dated fact tied to a factor with a document behind it, and the sheet contains no diagnostic label about the other parent.*

2. **Critique a hypothetical report *(scenario exercise).*** Using the report-analysis matrix, take a sample FOC report (or the worked example below) and **identify three findings unsupported by competent evidence**, name the defect type for each, and state **the exhibit or testimony that rebuts each** and how it would get admitted. *Done when you have three findings, three defect types, and three admissibility-routed rebuttals — and none of your rebuttals depends on the report itself being treated as evidence.*

> **Worked example — critiquing a finding (fictional; assumes a postjudgment parenting-time dispute, FOC investigation ordered, hearing not yet held).**
> *Report finding, quoted:* "The child appears more bonded to the mother and the father's involvement has been inconsistent."
> - *Supported by competent hearing evidence?* **No** — the report attributes this to interviews with the mother and the maternal grandmother; neither has testified, and the statement is offered for its truth (hearsay, Module 6).
> - *Defect type:* **source** (untested hearsay) and **omission** (the report does not mention the father's documented exchanges).
> - *Rebuttal, with route:* the father's dated exchange log and the school pickup records for the same period (business/school records — foundation and hearsay route in Modules 6–7), plus his own testimony. *Objection to file:* the finding of "inconsistent involvement" is unsupported by competent evidence and omits the documented record; the court should decide involvement on the hearing evidence, not the report.
> - *Note the discipline:* the critique pleads **conduct and records**, never "the mother is alienating." And it does **not** ask the court to treat a *favorable* fact in the report as proof — it offers independent evidence instead.

## Stop and seek counsel 🛑

- If the FOC report, the interview, or the case crosses into **CPS, a police investigation, or a criminal allegation**, stop. The parallel-proceeding firewall applies, what you say to an investigator can matter in another forum, and this is outside the guide — Module 17. 🛑 `DO NOT USE THIS GUIDE ALONE`
- If there is **domestic violence, a PPO, or a safety concern**, the interview, your address, and disclosure all need handling that this module only points at — FM.5 safety planning, Module 17, and the safety override in FM.2. Nothing here overrides a safety order. 🛑 `SAFETY OVERRIDE`
- If a **decision looks imminent** and you have not yet seen or objected to the report, the review-and-object window (MCR 3.210(C)(6)) may be closing — the true deadline is "before the decision is entered," which can be days. Get same-day help confirming your window (FM.4 resources). `⚠ TIME-CRITICAL`
- If the other side, or the court, asks whether you will **stipulate to admitting the FOC report**, and you are unsure what that costs you, that is a moment to get advice before you answer — it changes the evidentiary rules of your case (15.4).
- If what you received is a **referee's recommended order** rather than an investigation report, you are on a different clock (21 days) and a different track — Module 16, now.
