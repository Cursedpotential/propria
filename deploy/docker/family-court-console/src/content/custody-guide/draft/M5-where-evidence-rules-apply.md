# Why this module opens the proof section

All three reviewers of this project reached the same conclusion: the largest gap in a self-represented custody case is not strategy — it is proof. And proof has a timing problem. **What a court is allowed to weigh determines what you must preserve and obtain months earlier**, before you ever walk into a hearing. That is why evidence is treated here as a spine that runs under every later module, not a single chapter you can skip.

This first evidence module asks the question that comes *before* "what evidence do I need?" — namely, **where will my proof actually be tested under the rules, and where won't it?** The answer is not the same for every step in your case. Some proceedings weigh your proof under the full Michigan Rules of Evidence. Others operate under relaxed or entirely different rules, or take no evidence at all. Get this wrong in either direction and you pay for it:

- You bring a binder of documents to a proceeding that will not weigh them — and mistake being *heard* for being *proven right*.
- Or you fail to preserve admissible proof for the one proceeding that *will* decide your contested facts under the rules — and stand at the podium holding papers the judge cannot consider.

The Michigan Rules of Evidence are cited throughout this book as **MRE**. This module maps where they bind.

## 5.1 The default: the rules apply almost everywhere

Start from the baseline. The Michigan Rules of Evidence are written to govern courtrooms broadly, and they say so:

> "These rules govern proceedings in Michigan courts. The specific courts and proceedings to which the rules apply, along with exceptions, are set out in Rule 1101."
> — MRE 101(a)

> "Except as otherwise provided in subrule (b) or in a rule prescribed by the Supreme Court, these rules apply to all Michigan court actions and proceedings."
> — MRE 1101(a)

So the **default is that the rules apply.** The exceptions are a short, closed list. MRE 1101(b) opens:

> "The rules—except for those on privilege—do not apply to the following:"
> — MRE 1101(b)

The full list of exceptions, by the rule's own labels, is only ten items:

1. Preliminary Questions of Fact
2. Grand Jury
3. Miscellaneous Criminal Proceedings
4. Contempt Proceedings
5. Small Claims
6. **In Camera Custody Hearings**
7. Proceedings Involving Juveniles
8. Preliminary Examinations — Property Matters
9. **Domestic Relations Matters**
10. Mental-Health Hearings — Opinion Testimony

Most of that list has nothing to do with a custody case. The two that do — **(6)** and **(9)** — are narrower than they look, and 5.3 and 5.4 below take them apart one at a time.

One trap to clear immediately: item **(7), "Proceedings Involving Juveniles,"** does **not** mean "any case in the family division." It is limited to the child-protective and delinquency world:

> "Proceedings in the family division of the circuit court whenever MCR subchapter 3.900 states that the Michigan Rules of Evidence do not apply."
> — MRE 1101(b)(7)

Your ordinary custody or parenting-time dispute is a family-division case, but it is **not** a subchapter 3.900 juvenile proceeding. The rules apply to it. (If your case *does* cross into CPS or a juvenile petition, that is a different rule set and a hard stop — see Module 17.)

## 5.2 Where the rules bind you: the referee hearing and the trial

The proceedings that decide your contested facts are the ones where the rules apply in full.

**The referee hearing.** In Genesee County, a domestic-relations referee often hears the contested motion before any judge does. And the rule is explicit:

> "The Michigan Rules of Evidence apply to referee hearings."
> — MCR 3.215(D)(1)

This is the single most important line in the module. In many custody cases the first — and sometimes the only — contested proceeding where evidence is actually taken is in front of a **referee**, not a judge. That can make the referee hearing your real trial. Whether your case is routed to a referee at all depends on local assignment practice, so confirm your own track using Module 4 (which explains the two-track system).
`ℹ LOCAL PRACTICE — may have changed` (whether a referee hears your motion; confirm with the FOC / clerk, per Module 4)

**The trial.** A bench trial before the judge is the paradigm case for MRE 1101(a): the rules apply, full stop.

**The judicial (de novo) hearing on an objection to a referee's recommendation.** If you object to a referee's recommendation and the matter goes before the judge, the judge may review the referee record — but is **required** to let you put on live evidence:

> "the court may conduct the judicial hearing by review of the record of the referee hearing, but the court must allow the parties to present live evidence at the judicial hearing."
> — MCR 3.215(F)(2) (as verified in the verification ledger §2.3)

So the rules apply there too. (The mechanics of objecting, and the 21-day clock, are Module 16 — this module only places the hearing on the map.)

**Why this is the whole reason to build proof first.** Because the rules apply at these hearings, every exhibit you hope to use has to get *in* under them — which means it needs a way to be authenticated (Module 7) and an answer to hearsay (Module 6). Those foundations are things you assemble in advance, from the moment you start documenting. A text thread, a school record, or a message from the other parent is not "evidence" simply because you printed it; it is evidence when it can be admitted at a proceeding where the rules apply. That is the work Modules 6 through 8 set up.

## 5.3 Where the rules step aside — and why that is usually a trap, not a relief

A reader who hears "the rules don't apply here" often relaxes. Usually the opposite is warranted: a proceeding where the rules of evidence are switched off is a proceeding that **cannot carry your contested facts to a finding.** Here are the ones that matter in a custody case.

### The in camera interview of the child

If the court speaks privately with your child about the child's preference, the rules of evidence do not apply to that conversation:

> "In camera proceedings in child-custody matters to determine a child's custodial preference."
> — MRE 1101(b)(6) (rules inapplicable)

But the same rule that switches the evidence rules off also fences the interview in. It is confined to one narrow question:

> "The court may interview the child privately to determine if the child is of sufficient age to express a preference regarding custody, and, if so, the reasonable preference of the child. The court shall focus the interview on these determinations, and the information received shall be applied only to the reasonable preference factor."
> — MCR 3.210(C)(5) (as verified in the verification ledger §2.4)

The Michigan Judicial Institute's Evidence Benchbook states the point in one line: "Although the judge is limited in his or her line of questioning, the rules of evidence do not apply." (MJI Evidence Benchbook, § 3.6(C).) Both halves travel together: **the interview exists, and it is narrow.** It is not a back channel for a child to narrate the other parent's conduct, and it is not a substitute for a hearing on anything other than the child's preference.

> ### ⚠ The interview cannot, by itself, decide custody
> The Evidence Benchbook reports a 2025 Court of Appeals decision (*Atherton v Atherton*, quoting *In re HRC*, 286 Mich App 444 (2009)) in which a trial court "committed clear legal error by suspending [a parent's] parenting time, based solely on information gathered at an *in camera* interview, without conducting an evidentiary hearing." The lesson mirrors the FOC-report rule in 5.4: a channel where the rules of evidence are switched off can *inform* the court, but a change in custody or parenting time still has to rest on evidence taken at a hearing where the rules apply.
> `ℹ PROVISIONAL` — *Atherton v Atherton* (2025) and *In re HRC* (2009) are used here only as quoted in the MJI Evidence Benchbook; the opinions were not independently read in this pass. Confirm before relying on them.

How a child's out-of-court statements can and cannot be used — and why the "tender-years" hearsay exception does not reach a custody case — is **Module 6's** subject, not this one. And under the project's rule on the child, nothing here asks you to interview, coach, record, or debrief your child; the interview belongs to the court alone.

### The judge deciding whether evidence comes in

When the judge rules on a preliminary question — is this witness qualified, does a privilege apply, is this exhibit admissible — the judge is not boxed in by the evidence rules while making that call:

> "The court must decide any preliminary question about whether a witness is qualified, a privilege exists, or evidence is admissible. In so deciding, the court is not bound by evidence rules, except those on privilege."
> — MRE 104(a)

Practical meaning: to *decide* whether your exhibit gets in, the judge may look at things that would not themselves be admissible. Privilege is the exception that still binds even here — which is why the privilege questions in Module 6 matter regardless of the setting.

### Settlement talks and mediation

What you say while trying to settle is generally protected from being turned into proof against you:

> "Evidence of the following is not admissible to either prove or disprove the liability for or the validity or amount of a disputed claim: (1) furnishing, promising, or offering—or accepting, promising to accept, or offering to accept—a valuable consideration in compromising or attempting to compromise the claim; and (2) conduct or a statement made during compromise negotiations."
> — MRE 408(a)

Mediation carries its own, broader confidentiality protection on top of MRE 408; that specific rule is covered with the mediation material in the Resolve stage. The takeaway for this module: a settlement or mediation session is a *different-rules* space, and an offer you make there is not an admission you have to fear seeing quoted back at a hearing.
`ℹ PROVISIONAL` — the exact mediation-confidentiality court rule is confirmed in the mediation module, not here.

## 5.4 The FOC report: considered, but not evidence

This is the exception that costs self-represented parents the most, and it is the reason "Domestic Relations Matters" appears on the MRE 1101(b) list. Read the exception precisely — it is **not** a general exemption for custody cases. It is narrow:

> "The court's consideration of a report or recommendation submitted by the friend of the court under MCL 552.505(1)(g) or (h)."
> — MRE 1101(b)(9)

Only the court's *consideration of the FOC report* is outside the rules. Your contested hearing is not.

Here is how the pieces fit. The **Friend of the Court investigation** is not a hearing. The investigator gathers information — interviews, records, statements from third parties — without being bound by the rules of evidence, and writes a report with a recommendation. The court is then permitted to *consider* that report without running it through the evidence rules (that is what MRE 1101(b)(9) allows). But "consider" is doing careful work in that sentence. The leading case draws the line:

> "The FOC's report and recommendation is not admissible as evidence unless both parties agree to admit it in evidence. However, the report may be considered by the trial court as an aid to understanding the issues to be resolved. The trial court's ultimate findings relative to custody must be based upon competent evidence adduced at the hearing. Thus, while the FOC's report and recommendation may not form the basis for the trial court's findings, it may be used to establish a background and context for the proceedings."
> — *Duperon v Duperon*, 175 Mich App 77 (1989) (quoted as verified in the verification ledger §4.2)

Three things follow, and they cut in **both** directions:

1. **The report is not evidence** unless *both* parties agree to admit it. An unfavorable report is therefore not proof against you — and a favorable one carries none of the burden you still have to meet with real evidence.
2. **The court may still consider it** as background and context. That is how it coexists with MRE 1101(b)(9): consideration is not the same as evidence.
3. **The findings must rest on competent evidence from the hearing.** A decision that simply adopts the report's findings, without independent hearing evidence to support them, is the kind of error a losing party can raise.

> ### ⚠ Agreeing to "put the FOC report into evidence" changes all of this
> The report starts life as a *not-evidence* document. Stipulating — agreeing on the record to admit it — converts it into evidence **for and against you both**. The strategic default this guide follows is therefore to **not** stipulate the report into evidence, precisely so the *Duperon* protections survive: the report cannot form the basis of the findings, and if a recommendation or order tracks the report while the hearing record does not independently support it, that mismatch is an objection to raise and an error to preserve. That preserved argument exists **only if you did not agree to admit the report.** The full paradox — including how to read, respond to, and object to an FOC report, and the review-and-object right before decision — is **Module 15**; objecting to a referee's recommendation is **Module 16**.
> `⚠ TIME-CRITICAL` where a review-and-object window is running (see Module 15). `⚠ VERIFY` — the *Duperon* text above is quoted from convergent secondary mirrors per the ledger; the official-reporter cross-check is still open.

## 5.5 The numbering trap

> ### ⚠ Old citations point at the wrong rule and the wrong subsection
> **The Michigan Rules of Evidence were restyled effective January 1, 2024** (ADM File No. 2021-10, adopted September 20, 2023, as verified in the verification ledger §2.1). A secondary source, form, or older brief written before that date may quote **superseded MRE wording and numbering.** Confirm any rule against the current MRE before you quote it.
>
> The same drift affects the statutes in this module. *Duperon* (1989) cites the FOC duty under the **old** number "MCL 552.505(d)"; the current provision is **MCL 552.505(1)(g)–(h)**, which is the number MRE 1101(b)(9) uses today. And the parenting-time statute discussed in Module 2 was renumbered — the factor list that older cases cite as **MCL 722.27a(6)** now lives at **MCL 722.27a(7)** (verification ledger §4.1). When you follow a case citation to a subsection, check that the subsection still says what the case says it says.

## Templates and tools

**The "Where do the rules apply?" matrix (one page — keep it with your hearing prep).**

| Proceeding | Do the Michigan Rules of Evidence apply? | Rule that says so | What it means for your proof |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bench trial before the judge | **Yes** | MRE 1101(a) (default — rules apply unless excepted) | Every exhibit needs authentication (M7) and a hearsay answer (M6). |
| **Referee hearing** | **Yes** | MCR 3.215(D)(1) | Often your first / only contested evidentiary hearing — prepare for it as your trial (M4). |
| Judicial (de novo) hearing on your objection to a referee recommendation | **Yes** — live evidence must be allowed | MCR 3.215(F)(2) | You may put on live testimony again; the rules govern what comes in (M16). |
| In camera interview of the child | **No** | MRE 1101(b)(6); confined by MCR 3.210(C)(5) | Limited to the child's preference; not a channel for other facts (M6). |
| Judge deciding whether a piece of evidence is admissible | **No** (except privilege) | MRE 104(a) / MRE 1101(b)(1) | The judge may look at otherwise-inadmissible material just to make the admissibility call. |
| The FOC investigation + the court's consideration of the FOC report | **No** | MRE 1101(b)(9); *Duperon* | The report is not evidence unless *both* parties agree; findings must rest on hearing evidence (M15). |
| Settlement talks / mediation | Compromise statements not admissible to prove the claim | MRE 408 (mediation adds broader confidentiality) | An offer to settle generally can't be used as proof against you. |
| A child-protective / juvenile (MCR subchapter 3.900) proceeding | Different rule set — outside this guide | MRE 1101(b)(7) | Hard stop; counsel (M17). |
