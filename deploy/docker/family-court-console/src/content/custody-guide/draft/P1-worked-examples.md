## Why a worked example, and how to use it

A blank template teaches you the shape of a document. It does not teach you the **register** — the flat, dated, unarguing voice that a court reads without flinching. Register is most of what separates a motion that gets heard from a motion that becomes the story.

So read these examples for three things:

1. **How little they say.** Almost every sentence is a date, a document, or a quotation from an order.
2. **What is missing.** No adjectives. No motives. No history. No characterizations of anyone.
3. **How the pieces lock together.** The same three letters appear in the motion, the affidavit, the brief, and the exhibit list, with the same dates each time.

> ### ⛔ Do not use this file as a template. Specifically, do not use it if:
> - You have not read Documents 1 through 4. This file assumes all of them.
> - Your facts are different — and they are. Every date, every schedule, and every ECE fact here is invented to make the shape visible.
> - **The schedule in the proposed order is built for a fictional three-year-old.** A different child needs a different schedule. Do not copy it.
> - You are actually asking to change custody rather than parenting time.
> - A PPO, no-contact order, CPS matter, or criminal matter touches your facts. **Stop.** `DO NOT USE THIS GUIDE ALONE`
> - Another state or country is involved. `DO NOT USE THIS GUIDE ALONE`
> - You would be copying any sentence here without being able to say, out loud, why it is true in your case.

### What to notice

- **Eleven paragraphs. Not one adjective about a person.** Paragraph 5(c) is the strongest fact in the motion and it is written in the flattest possible voice — arrival time, departure time, what did not happen. That flatness is what makes it land.
- **Paragraph 2 quotes the order.** Everything after it is measured against those seven words.
- **Paragraph 4 is a compliance paragraph disguised as a background paragraph.** It tells the court, without argument, that this parent has followed a restriction he is not asking to break.
- **Paragraphs 6 and 7 are the MCR 3.207(B)(1)(a) content**, and paragraph 6 concedes the established custodial environment openly. Conceding it is not a loss. It is what puts the motion in the lower-burden lane — see Document 2, Part A.2.
- **Paragraph 9 does the alternative pleading in one sentence.** It never claims MCL 722.27a(8) removes any threshold.
- **Paragraph 10 makes the concurrence certificate honest** by reciting what was actually done.
- **The history is absent.** Nothing about November 2025 beyond the fact that an order entered. Every window to revisit it closed long ago; the motion litigates forward.

# 2. The verified statement

MCR 3.207(B)(1)(a) speaks of "[a] verified pleading or an affidavit attached to a motion." A verified statement using the MCR 1.109(D)(3)(b) declaration is the simplest route and needs no notary.

```
                    STATE OF MICHIGAN
     IN THE CIRCUIT COURT FOR THE COUNTY OF GENESEE
                     FAMILY DIVISION

DANA MERROW,
     Plaintiff,                        Case No. 25-123456-XX
v.                                     HON. ________________
CHRIS VANTEL,
     Defendant.
______________________________________________________/

          VERIFIED STATEMENT OF CHRIS VANTEL

     I, Chris Vantel, state the following on my own personal
knowledge:

1.   I am the Defendant in this case.

2.   The minor child has lived primarily with Plaintiff since
     the order entered November 14, 2025. The child attends
     childcare from Plaintiff's residence. Plaintiff provides
     the child's day-to-day care.

3.   Based on the facts in paragraph 2, the minor child has an
     established custodial environment with Plaintiff.

4.   I do not ask this Court to change where the minor child
     primarily lives, who provides day-to-day care, or the
     child's childcare arrangement. I ask only that the times,
     dates, and locations of my parenting time be stated in
     the order.

5.   I wrote and personally mailed each of the letters
     attached as Exhibits A, B, and C, by certified mail with
     return receipt requested, on the dates those letters
     bear. The certified mail receipts and return receipts
     attached to each exhibit are the receipts I received from
     the United States Postal Service.

6.   I received the written responses attached as part of
     Exhibits B and C on or about the dates shown.

7.   On November 28, 2026 I arrived at the exchange location
     stated in my letter at 9:50 a.m. I remained until
     10:40 a.m. The minor child was not brought to that
     location. I then left.

     I declare under the penalties of perjury that this
verified statement has been examined by me and that its
contents are true to the best of my information, knowledge,
and belief.


Dated: December 14, 2026       ____________________________
                               CHRIS VANTEL
```

### What to notice

- **Paragraph 5 is the authentication paragraph.** It is what lets the letters and receipts come in — a witness with personal knowledge saying what each document is. Michigan has no self-authentication shortcut for these. Document 1, evidence table.
- **Paragraph 7 is personal knowledge only.** Where he was, when, what he saw. Nothing about why the child was not there, because he does not know why, and guessing would cost him more than the fact is worth.
- **Nothing in this statement came from the child.** The child is not a source, and there is no paragraph that could only have been written by asking one.
- **The declaration is copied exactly** from MCR 1.109(D)(3)(b). A knowingly false declaration under that subrule can be punished as contempt of court. *(VERIFIED.)*

# 3. The brief in support

> **FICTIONAL.** Roughly four pages as formatted. Quotations may be single-spaced; at least one-inch margins; 12- or 13-point body text — MCR 2.119(A)(2)(c), MCR 1.109(D)(1)(a).

```
     BRIEF IN SUPPORT OF DEFENDANT'S MOTION FOR ENTRY OF
     PARENTING TIME IN SPECIFIC TERMS

I.   INTRODUCTION

     The order entered November 14, 2025 states that parenting
time shall be "as the parties agree." It states no date, no
time, and no location. Defendant asks the Court to state
parenting time in specific terms and to set the conditions
that allow those terms to function.

II.  FACTS

     1. The order entered November 14, 2025 provides:
"Parenting time shall be as the parties agree."

     2. The parties communicate through Plaintiff's counsel.
Defendant has used that channel exclusively.

     3. Defendant requested parenting time in writing by
certified mail on October 6, 2026 (Exhibit A), October 29,
2026 (Exhibit B), and November 12, 2026 (Exhibit C). Each
letter proposed a specific date, specific hours, and a
specific exchange location, and requested a written response
by a stated date.

     4. The first request drew no response. The second drew a
written response dated November 10, 2026 stating the dates
were not available, with no alternative proposed. The third
drew a written response dated November 17, 2026 agreeing to
Saturday, November 28, 2026. On that date Defendant arrived
at the stated location at 9:50 a.m., remained until 10:40
a.m., and the minor child was not produced.

     5. In the fourteen months since the order entered, the
provision quoted in paragraph 1 has produced no parenting
time.

III. THE COURT SHOULD GRANT PARENTING TIME IN SPECIFIC TERMS
     UNDER MCL 722.27a(8)

     MCL 722.27a(8) provides:

          "Parenting time shall be granted in specific terms
          if requested by either party at any time."

     Defendant is a party. He requests specific terms. The
subsection contains no time limitation; it applies "at any
time."

     The Child Custody Act contemplates both forms of order.
MCL 722.27(1)(b) permits a court to "[p]rovide for reasonable
parenting time of the child by the parties involved . . . by
general or specific terms and conditions." MCL 722.27a(8) is
the provision by which either parent obtains the specific
form.

     Defendant does not contend that MCL 722.27a(8) displaces
any threshold this Court finds applicable to modification of
an existing order. Section IV addresses that question in the
alternative.

IV.  IN THE ALTERNATIVE, THE THRESHOLD IS SATISFIED

     A. The requested order does not change the established
        custodial environment.

     MCL 722.27(1)(c) provides that "[t]he custodial
environment of a child is established if over an appreciable
time the child naturally looks to the custodian in that
environment for guidance, discipline, the necessities of life,
and parental comfort."

     The minor child has an established custodial environment
with Plaintiff. The child lives primarily with Plaintiff,
attends childcare from Plaintiff's residence, and looks to
Plaintiff for day-to-day care. Defendant does not ask the
Court to disturb any of that. The proposed order changes no
residence, no childcare arrangement, and no day-to-day care.
It states times, dates, and locations that the current order
leaves to agreement.

     B. Because the established custodial environment is not
        changed, the parenting-time standard governs and the
        burden is a preponderance.

     In Shade v Wright, 291 Mich App 17 (2010), the Court of
Appeals held:

          "We discern nothing in the Vodvarka opinion that
          requires the standards used to determine the
          existence of proper cause or change of circumstances
          for custody determinations to apply to
          determinations regarding parenting time, absent a
          conclusion that a change in parenting time will
          result in a change in an established custodial
          environment. . . . If a change in parenting time
          results in a change in the established custodial
          environment, then the Vodvarka framework is
          appropriate."

     Shade limited its own holding. In footnote 4 the Court
stated: "With our holding today, we do not seek to precisely
define the proper cause or change of circumstances necessary
to change parenting time. Our holding is limited to our
conclusion that the normal life changes that occurred with the
minor child in this case are sufficient to modify parenting
time." Defendant cites Shade for the ECE-based distinction it
drew, not for a general test.

     In Pierron v Pierron, 486 Mich 81, 92-93 (2010), the
Supreme Court allocated the burden: where the proposed change
alters the established custodial environment, the standard is
clear and convincing evidence; where it does not, the standard
is a preponderance of the evidence, and the burden rests on
the parent proposing the change. Defendant accepts that burden
here.

     C. Proper cause and change of circumstances, if required.

     Vodvarka v Grasmeyer, 259 Mich App 499, 511, 513-514
(2003), defines the showing. "[P]roper cause means one or more
appropriate grounds that have or could have a significant
effect on the child's life to the extent that a reevaluation
of the child's custodial situation should be undertaken." A
change of circumstances requires proof that "since the entry
of the last custody order, the conditions surrounding custody
of the child, which have or could have a significant effect on
the child's well-being, have materially changed," and
"something more than the normal life changes."

     The facts are these. A provision that operates only on
agreement has, over fourteen months, produced no parenting
time at all. Three written requests, each proposing specific
dates and hours, produced no time — including one that was
agreed to in writing and then did not occur. The minor child
is three years old, an age at which the frequency and
regularity of contact bear directly on the relationship the
statute presumes to be in the child's interest.

V.   THE STATUTORY FRAMING SUPPORTS SPECIFIC TERMS

     MCL 722.27a(1) provides: "It is presumed to be in the
best interests of a child for the child to have a strong
relationship with both of his or her parents," and that
parenting time "shall be granted to a parent in a frequency,
duration, and type reasonably calculated to promote a strong
relationship between the child and the parent granted
parenting time."

     A provision that has produced no parenting time in
fourteen months is not, in operation, reasonably calculated to
promote that relationship.

     MCL 722.27a(3) provides: "A child has a right to
parenting time with a parent unless it is shown on the record
by clear and convincing evidence that it would endanger the
child's physical, mental, or emotional health." No such
showing has been made or attempted in this case.

VI.  THE PARENTING-TIME FACTORS

     MCL 722.27a(7) provides that "[t]he court may consider
the following factors when determining the frequency,
duration, and type of parenting time to be granted." Two are
directly engaged by this record.

     Factor (f) asks "[w]hether a parent can reasonably be
expected to exercise parenting time in accordance with the
court order." Defendant has communicated solely through the
channel the parties are directed to use, has proposed specific
dates and hours in writing, has offered to provide
transportation, and has appeared at the agreed location at the
agreed time.

     Factor (g) asks "[w]hether a parent has frequently failed
to exercise reasonable parenting time." Exhibits A through C
show three written requests over a seven-week period, each
proposing specific times, and the outcome of each.

     [Note for the reader, not part of the brief: older
     opinions cite these factors as MCL 722.27a(6)(a)-(i).
     The current statute places them at (7)(a)-(i). If you
     quote a case that cites subsection (6), add a bracketed
     note saying so.]

VII. THE REQUESTED CONDITIONS ARE AUTHORIZED BY STATUTE

     MCL 722.27a(9) provides that "[a] parenting time order
may contain any reasonable terms or conditions that facilitate
the orderly and meaningful exercise of parenting time by a
parent," including division of the responsibility and cost of
transporting the child, subsections (9)(a) and (b);
requirements that the child be ready at a specific time,
subsection (9)(d); requirements that the parent arrive and
return the child at specific times, subsection (9)(e);
requirements of reasonable notice when parenting time will not
occur, subsection (9)(h); and "[a]ny other reasonable
condition determined to be appropriate in the particular
case," subsection (9)(i).

     The proposed order tracks those subsections. Paragraphs 3
and 4 address transportation and cost. Paragraph 5 requires
the child to be ready at the stated time. Paragraph 6 requires
arrival and return at stated times. Paragraph 7 requires
written notice when a period will not occur. Paragraph 9
requests a written method of communication under subsection
(9)(i), so that the notice required by paragraph 7 can be
given and proved. Defendant does not request direct contact
with Plaintiff and does not ask the Court to relax the
existing communication directive.

     MCL 722.27a(10) requires that a parenting time order
"shall contain a prohibition on exercising parenting time in a
country that is not a party to the Hague Convention on the
Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction," absent both
parents' written consent. Paragraph 11 of the proposed order
contains that provision.

VIII. AN ENFORCEABLE ORDER IS THE OBJECT OF THIS MOTION

     MCL 552.641(2) permits the office of the friend of the
court to decline to respond to an alleged parenting time
violation where "[t]he custody or parenting time order does
not include an enforceable provision that is relevant to the
custody or parenting time order violation alleged in the
complaint." MCL 552.641(2)(c).

     An order providing that parenting time shall be "as the
parties agree" contains no term that can be violated. The
relief requested would give both parents terms that can be
followed, and, if not followed, enforced.

IX.  RELIEF REQUESTED

     Defendant requests entry of the attached proposed order.


Dated: December 14, 2026       ____________________________
                               CHRIS VANTEL
                               In propria persona
```

### What to notice

- **Section III is four short paragraphs and ends by giving something up.** "Defendant does not contend that MCL 722.27a(8) displaces any threshold" is the most important sentence in the brief. No case law on subsection (8) has been read by anyone in this project; claiming it settles the threshold question would be a claim nobody can back. Conceding the question openly and answering it in Section IV costs nothing and removes the easiest way to lose.
- **Section IV.B carries *Shade*'s footnote 4** and then says plainly what *Shade* is being cited for. That is how you use a narrow holding without overstating it.
- **Section VI walks two factors, not nine.** Walking all nine when only two are engaged reads as padding.
- **Section VII maps each statutory subsection to a numbered paragraph of the proposed order.** A judge can check the work in thirty seconds.
- **Section VII also disclaims what is not being asked for** — no direct contact, no relaxation of the communication directive. That sentence exists because a request to change a communication arrangement can be heard as a request to loosen a protection. Saying otherwise, in the motion, is cheaper than explaining it at the hearing.
- **No case is cited whose holding has not been read.** *Hayes v Hayes* is a natural fit for the ECE point and is deliberately absent, because its holding text has not been read for this project. *Pierron* appears because its burden allocation was confirmed through *Shade*'s own text — and Document 2's authority panel marks it PROVISIONAL for exactly that reason.
- **No unpublished opinion is cited.** MCR 7.215(C)(1) would require explaining why, where published authority exists. The simplest safe course is not to.

> ### ⚠ This schedule is built for a fictional three-year-old who has had no regular contact for over a year. It is not a model for any other child.
>

MCL 722.27a(7)(b) directs that a court may consider whether the child is nursing and under six months, or under one year with substantial nutrition through nursing — which is a different schedule question again.
>

```
County of Genesee, State of Michigan, on ________________.

2.   SCHEDULE — PHASE TWO. Beginning Saturday, March 20, 2027

3.   SCHEDULE — PHASE THREE. Beginning Friday, May 21, 2027
     and continuing thereafter, Defendant shall have parenting

     (a)  on alternating weekends, from Friday at 6:00 p.m.
          until Sunday at 6:00 p.m., beginning the weekend of
          Friday, May 21, 2027 and every other weekend
          thereafter; and
```

### What to notice

- **Every period has a start time and an end time.** There is no phrase anywhere reading "as agreed," "reasonable," or "mutually convenient."
- **Paragraph 7's second sentence is the quiet one.** A 30-minute forfeiture rule cuts against the parent who wrote it as readily as against anyone else. It is in the order because a rule that only binds one parent is a rule a court is right to be suspicious of — and because it removes the argument that would otherwise happen at 10:20 on a Saturday.
- **Paragraph 9 disclaims itself.** "Nothing in this paragraph authorizes any contact between the parents other than written communication through this method." That sentence is what keeps a communication-protocol request from reading as an attempt to loosen a restriction.
- **The blank in paragraph 9 is deliberate.** Naming a commercial application in a proposed order invites a fight about cost and access, and **treating any application as inherently court-favored is unsupported.** Leave the method to be filled in, and be ready for the court to choose something simpler.
- **Paragraph 11 is mandatory content** under MCL 722.27a(10), and it is one sentence. Leaving it out is a defect in a proposed order.
- **Paragraph 12 covers Mother's Day and Father's Day the same way in both columns**, because alternating them is a drafting error people make by pattern-matching the rest of the table.
- **Paragraph 13 leaves the rest of the existing order alone**, which is what makes this a parenting-time motion and not a custody motion.

> ### ⚠ What to do if the hearing date moves
>
> This proposed order names dates certain. If the hearing is adjourned past January 23, 2027, the Phase One start date has passed before the order can be signed. **Bring a corrected proposed order to the hearing** with the phase dates moved, and say so on the record. The alternative — writing "beginning the first Saturday after entry of this order" — avoids that problem but makes every later date depend on a calculation, which is exactly what specificity is supposed to eliminate. Dates certain plus a corrected copy is the better trade.

## The 7-day clock, worked

**The proposed recommended order arrived first.** MCR 3.215(E)(3)(d) lets a party draft it and serve it with notice that it goes to the referee for approval unless written objections to its **accuracy or completeness** are filed within 7 days of service.

In this fictional case the proposed recommended order omitted the exchange-location paragraph the referee had stated on the record. That is an accuracy-and-completeness problem, not a merits problem, and it has its own three-part response:

> **MCR 3.215(E)(3)(d)(ii):** "To object to the accuracy or completeness of a proposed recommended order, the party must within 7 days after service of the proposed order, **file written objections with the court clerk that state with specificity the inaccuracy or omission** in the proposed recommended order, and **serve the objections on all parties as required by MCR 2.107, together with a notice of hearing and an alternative proposed recommended order.**"

*(VERIFIED verbatim — `sources/primary/michigan-court-rules_2026-07-31.txt`.)*


## The recommendation, and deciding what to object to

**What the referee recommended** (fictional):

1. Parenting time every **other** Saturday, 11:00 a.m. to 3:00 p.m.
2. Exchanges **"at a location agreed upon by the parties."**
3. No provision requiring the child be ready at a stated time, and no notice provision for a missed period.
4. **No Hague provision.**
5. No communication method; the existing communication directive untouched.
6. A finding that "Defendant has not consistently sought parenting time."
7. The recommendation recites that the referee "reviewed and considered the Friend of the Court report and recommendation." That report was never stipulated into evidence.

**What this fictional parent objected to — and what he left alone.**

| Item | Objected? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Every-other-Saturday, 11–3 | **Indirectly** | Not "I wanted more." Objected through the **absence of findings** (item 8), which is a nameable defect. "I am unhappy with the amount" is not an objection; it is a mood, and MCR 3.215(F)(3) taxes moods. |
| "A location agreed upon by the parties" | **Yes** | This reinstates the exact defect the motion was brought to cure. Concrete, quotable, fixable. |
| No ready-time or notice provisions | **Yes** | Requested under MCL 722.27a(9)(d), (h) and simply not addressed. |
| **No Hague provision** | **Yes** | MCL 722.27a(10) says a parenting time order **"shall contain"** it. The cleanest objection in the set: a mandatory content omission, decidable without reweighing anything. |
| No communication method | **No** | Requested, not granted. That is a discretionary call the referee was entitled to make. Objecting to every unfavorable discretionary call is what a frivolous objection looks like. |
| The existing communication directive left in place | **No** | Same reason. Also, this parent is not asking to loosen it. |
| "Has not consistently sought parenting time" | **Yes** | Contradicted by Exhibits A–C, which were admitted. This is a finding against the record, and it is the finding that drives the schedule. |
| Reliance on the FOC report | **Yes** | See below. This is the strongest legal objection in the packet. |
| No findings, no stated standard | **Yes** | MCR 3.215(E)(1)(a) requires the referee to "find facts specially and state separately the law the referee applied." |

**Five grounds, each nameable in one sentence.** That is what "a clear and concise statement of the specific findings or application of law to which an objection is made" means — MCR 3.215(E)(4).

## The Friend of the Court report objection

This is the ground most self-represented parents never make, and it is available **only** because this parent did not stipulate the report into evidence.

> ***Duperon v Duperon*, 175 Mich App 77; 437 NW2d 318 (1989)** (published):
>
> "The FOC's report and recommendation is **not admissible as evidence unless both parties agree to admit it in evidence.** However, the report **may be considered by the trial court as an aid to understanding the issues** to be resolved. The trial court's **ultimate findings relative to custody must be based upon competent evidence adduced at the hearing.** Thus, while the FOC's report and recommendation **may not form the basis for the trial court's findings**, it may be used to establish a **background and context** for the proceedings."
>
> ***Marshall v Beal*, 158 Mich App 582, 591 (1986)**: where the parties "did not stipulate to allowing the friend of the court's recommendations or report into evidence" and the court "reviewed and adopted the friend of the court's findings" rather than arriving at an independent conclusion — "**This is a clear legal error which requires reversal** and a new hearing."

*(VERIFIED — `verification_ledger.md` §4.2; *Duperon* opinion text read across three independent mirrors with identical operative language. Official-reporter cross-check and a negative-treatment check remain open — these are 1980s Court of Appeals decisions and no citator was available to this project.)*

**Three things to hold together:**

1. **Consideration is not evidence.** MRE 1101(b)(9) says the evidence rules do not apply to the court's *consideration* of the report. *Duperon* says the report is not *admissible* absent both parties' agreement. Both are true. The report can inform; it cannot prove.
2. **Findings must rest on hearing evidence.** A recommendation whose findings track the report, where the hearing record does not independently support them, is the error *Marshall* calls reversible.
3. **You only have this argument if you did not stipulate.** Signing the report into evidence converts a not-evidence document into evidence — for you and against you. Document 4 says do not stipulate. This is why.

> **⚠ Citation-drift warning.** *Duperon* cites the friend-of-the-court duty as "MCL 552.505(d)" and the *Truitt* line cites the de novo hearing as "MCL 552.507(5)" — old numbering. The current provisions are **MCL 552.505(1)(g)–(h)** and **MCL 552.507(4)**. Same trap as MCL 722.27a(6)→(7). **Bracket-note any quotation you use.**
>
> **⚠ One citation is incomplete.** The ledger records *Truitt* as a Court of Appeals decision quoting *Marshall v Beal*, but without a reporter citation. **Do not put an incomplete citation in a filing.** The worked objection below cites *Duperon* and *Marshall*, both of which have complete citations, and leaves *Truitt* out. `⚠ PROVISIONAL — complete the Truitt citation from the official reporter before citing it.`

### What to notice

- **Every heading names the type of objection** — finding of fact, or application of law. MCR 3.215(E)(4) asks for "the specific findings or application of law to which an objection is made," and labeling them makes the objection self-auditing.
- **Every ground ends with a requested correction.** An objection that says only "this is wrong" leaves the court to guess what would be right.
- **Ground 4 quotes the record, not the grievance.** Exhibit numbers, mailing dates, delivery dates, the agreement, the times he arrived and left. Not "the referee ignored my evidence."
- **Ground 5 concedes the safe half in its own paragraph:** "Defendant does not object to the referee having read the report." That sentence is what keeps the objection accurate under *Duperon*, where consideration is permitted and reliance is not. Overstating it — "the referee was not allowed to look at the report" — would be wrong on the law and would cost credibility on the ground that matters most.
- **The penultimate paragraph lists what is NOT objected to.** This is the single most underrated paragraph in the document. It tells the court this is a targeted objection, not a re-argument of the whole hearing — which is the direct answer to MCR 3.215(F)(3)'s frivolous-or-for-delay standard.
- **Ground 2 is the easiest win in the packet** and takes eight lines. Mandatory order content, omitted, fixable without reweighing a single fact.
- **Nothing here re-argues the merits of the November 2025 order.** Fifteen months on, that is still not the case.

## What this objection buys, and what it does not

**Buys:** a judicial hearing at which, whatever restrictions the court imposes, **"the court must allow the parties to present live evidence"** — MCR 3.215(F)(2). *(VERIFIED.)*

**Does not buy:** a fresh start. MCL 552.507(6)(a) permits a de novo hearing to be "[a] new decision based entirely on the record of a previous hearing," and MCL 552.507(5)(a) conditions that on the parties having had "a full opportunity to present and preserve important evidence **at the referee hearing.**" MCR 3.215(F)(2)(c) permits the court to bar new evidence and new witnesses absent an adequate showing it was not available earlier. *(All VERIFIED.)*

**Which is the whole argument for Document 4's central instruction: everything goes in at the referee hearing.** In this fictional case that is exactly why Ground 4 works — Exhibits A, B, and C were admitted on January 15, so the objection can point at the record instead of asking for a second chance to build one.

**One more thing not to do:** do not sign anything that consents to immediate entry of the recommended order. MCR 3.215(E)(8) — "The parties may waive their right to object to the referee's recommendation for an order by consenting in writing to the immediate entry of the recommended order." *(VERIFIED.)* If the hallway conversation on February 3 had ended in a signature, none of Part Two exists.

>
> The response can arrive **four days before the hearing** and still be early. And then:
>
> > **MCR 2.119(A)(2)(b): "Except as permitted by the court or as otherwise provided in these rules, no reply briefs, additional briefs, or supplemental briefs may be filed."**
>
> *(VERIFIED verbatim — `sources/primary/michigan-court-rules_2026-07-31.txt`.)*

```
     MOTION FOR ENTRY OF PARENTING TIME IN SPECIFIC TERMS

I.   INTRODUCTION

     Defendant asks this Court to replace a parenting-time
provision entered fourteen months ago with a detailed
graduated schedule, a court-ordered communication platform,
and eight ancillary conditions. He does so without meeting the
threshold that governs modification of an existing order, and
on a record that shows he made no request for parenting time
of any kind between November 2025 and October 2026.

II.  MCL 722.27a(8) DOES NOT DISPLACE THE MODIFICATION
     STANDARD

     MCL 722.27a(8) provides that parenting time "shall be
granted in specific terms if requested by either party at any
time." Parenting time has already been granted in this case.
The order entered November 14, 2025 granted it, in the general
terms expressly authorized by MCL 722.27(1)(b), which permits
a court to provide for parenting time "by general or specific
terms and conditions."

     What Defendant seeks is not a grant. It is a modification
of an existing order, and modification is governed by
MCL 722.27(1)(c), which permits the court to "modify or amend
its previous judgments or orders for proper cause shown or
because of change of circumstances."

     Defendant identifies no published authority holding that
MCL 722.27a(8) removes that threshold, and Plaintiff is aware
of none.

III. DEFENDANT HAS NOT SHOWN PROPER CAUSE OR A CHANGE OF
     CIRCUMSTANCES

     Vodvarka v Grasmeyer, 259 Mich App 499, 513-514 (2003),
requires proof that "since the entry of the last custody
order, the conditions surrounding custody of the child . . .
have materially changed," and "something more than the normal
life changes."

     Nothing has materially changed. The order says what it
said in November 2025. The parties' circumstances are
substantially the same. What is new is that Defendant began
writing letters in October 2026 — three of them, over seven
weeks, fourteen months after the order entered. A party cannot
manufacture a change of circumstances by commencing
correspondence.

IV.  THE EXISTING PROVISION SERVES THIS CHILD

     The minor child is three years old. Both parents work
schedules that vary week to week. A provision permitting the
parents to arrange parenting time as they agree accommodates a
young child's routine, illness, and medical appointments in a
way a fixed calendar cannot.

     Defendant participated in the proceedings that produced
the November 14, 2025 order. He did not appeal it, move for
reconsideration of it, or move to modify it for fourteen
months.

V.   THE SPECIFIC DATES DEFENDANT IDENTIFIES

     Plaintiff does not dispute that Defendant sent the three
letters attached to his motion. Plaintiff states the
following:

     A.  October 24-25, 2026. Counsel received the request and
         conveyed it. Plaintiff was not able to confirm those

     B.  November 14-15, 2026. The minor child had a scheduled

     C.  November 28, 2026. The minor child became ill on the

VI.  DEFENDANT HAS NOT EXERCISED PARENTING TIME

     MCL 722.27a(7)(g) permits the Court to consider "[w]hether
a parent has frequently failed to exercise reasonable
parenting time." Between entry of the order on November 14,

     Plaintiff reserves the right to present evidence bearing

VIII. THE RELIEF REQUESTED IS OVERBROAD

     Defendant's proposed order runs to thirteen paragraphs,

     Plaintiff asks that the motion be denied. In the
```

### Why this is a good response

- **Section II is the strongest argument in the case, and it is textual.** "Parenting time has already been granted" is a real reading of MCL 722.27a(8), and it pairs with MCL 722.27(1)(b)'s express authorization of general terms. **This project has read no case law on subsection (8)** — which means neither side can say the question is settled, and the moving parent must not pretend otherwise.
- **Section III's last sentence is the sharp one.** "A party cannot manufacture a change of circumstances by commencing correspondence." It has to be answered, not ignored.
- **Section V does not deny anything.** It supplies reasons. That is far more effective than a denial, and it is the section most likely to be true.
- **Section VII is written the way a careful lawyer writes it** — a reservation, not an accusation. It does not allege anything. It reserves.
- **Section IX offers the court a middle.** Responses that only say "deny" give a court one thing to reject. This one gives the court somewhere to land.

## The walkthrough — what to actually say

> **Register rule for every line below: answer the argument, never the person.** The moment a sentence describes the other parent rather than the record, the hearing changes subject, and the new subject is you.

**On the threshold (their §II and §III).**

> "Your Honor, I'm not asking the Court to decide whether subsection 8 removes the threshold. I don't need that ruling. Section IV of my brief pleads proper cause and change of circumstances in the alternative, and I'm prepared to meet it today.
>
> On the change of circumstances: it isn't the letters. It's what the provision has produced. In fourteen months, a term that operates only on agreement has produced no parenting time. Exhibits A through C show three written requests with specific dates and hours, and what came of each.
>
> And on the burden — the order I'm asking for doesn't change where the child lives or who provides day-to-day care. Under *Shade* and *Pierron*, that puts this at a preponderance, and I accept that burden."

**On flexibility (their §IV).** This is the answer worth memorizing:

> "I don't disagree that flexibility has value. What I'm asking for doesn't remove it. A specific order is a floor, not a ceiling — if both parents agree to more time, or different time, nothing in my proposed order prevents that. Paragraph 13 leaves the rest of the existing order in place.
>
> What a floor does is give both of us something that works on a week when we don't agree."

**On the reasons for the missed dates (their §V).** Do not fight this. Take it.

> "I accept that each of those may be true. A medical appointment, a family obligation, and a child getting sick are all ordinary things.
>
> That's the argument, Your Honor. Under a term that operates only on agreement, an ordinary reason ends the parenting time entirely, and there's nothing to fall back on. Under a specific order, an illness on a Saturday means one period is missed and made up. The reasons aren't the problem. The absence of any term is the problem."

> ### Why this answer works
>
> It concedes every disputed fact and loses nothing. The other side's best factual section becomes the moving party's best argument, and no one has been called anything. **If you find yourself preparing to prove the child was not really ill, stop.** That fight is unwinnable, it needs evidence you do not have, and win or lose it makes the hearing about the other parent's honesty rather than the order's terms.

**On the eleven months (their §VI).** Concede, then reframe.

> "The Court is right that there's a gap, and I'm not asking anyone to overlook it. What I can show is what happened once I did start asking, in writing, through the channel I'm directed to use — three requests, specific dates and hours, transportation offered, in seven weeks. That's Exhibits A through C, and it's what factor (f) asks about: whether a parent can reasonably be expected to exercise parenting time in accordance with a court order."

**On the reservation (their §VII).** Say little. Say it carefully.

> "I'll only say this: no showing of endangerment has been made or attempted, and MCL 722.27a(3) requires that to be shown on the record by clear and convincing evidence. If Plaintiff intends to offer evidence on subsections 7(c) or 7(d) today, I'd ask that it be taken as testimony so I can respond to it.
>
> My motion doesn't depend on the Court finding anything against Plaintiff. It depends on the statute, the presumption in subsection 1, and my own request record."

> ### ⚠ The limiter, in the same breath
>
> **If there is a protective explanation and it is credited, MCL 722.23(j)'s second sentence means it cannot be scored against that parent** — "A court may not consider negatively for the purposes of this factor any reasonable action taken by a parent to protect a child or that parent from sexual assault or domestic violence by the child's other parent." *(VERIFIED.)*
>
> A motion built on factor (j) collapses at this moment. A motion built on MCL 722.27a(8), the subsection (1) presumption, the subsection (3) right, and a documented request record does not. That is why Part One's brief never argues factor (j) — **and this is the hearing where that choice pays.**

**On overbreadth (their §VIII).** Give ground on relief, not on principle.

> "If the Court finds the phased schedule is more than this record supports, I'd ask for paragraph 1 alone — Saturdays, 10:00 to 4:00, at a named location — and I'd accept a review date. What I'd ask the Court not to do is enter a term that operates only if we agree, because that's the provision that produced these fourteen months."

**And the one-sentence close.**

> "I'm asking for terms that can be put on a calendar and followed. Thank you, Your Honor."

## What escalation costs

| Escalating version | What the court hears | Flat version |
|---|---|---|
| "She's lying about the child being sick." | An accusation you cannot prove | "I accept that may be true. That's why the order needs a term." |
| "She has deliberately kept my child from me." | A characterization, and a motive claim | "No parenting time occurred on those dates." |
| "This is exactly the pattern I've been dealing with." | A pattern claim with no exhibit | "Exhibits A through C show the three requests and what followed." |
| "Her lawyer is playing games with the mail." | An attack on counsel | "The response was served January 8 and reached me January 11." |
| "I've been more than reasonable." | Self-characterization | "I offered transportation both ways in each letter." |

**Every left-hand sentence is one a person in this position genuinely feels.** The right-hand sentence says the same thing with a document behind it. `ℹ DRAFTING JUDGMENT — this is register advice, not law.`

## When the response asserts facts you dispute

Sections V and VI of the response assert facts. If a material fact is genuinely contested and the court must resolve it, a motion hearing on paper may not be the right vehicle:

> **MCR 2.119(E)(2):** "When a motion is based on facts not appearing of record, the court may hear the motion on affidavits presented by the parties, or **may direct that the motion be heard wholly or partly on oral testimony or deposition.**"

*(VERIFIED verbatim.)*

And in this subchapter specifically:

> **MCR 3.210(C)(1):** "When the custody, **parenting time**, change of domicile, or another motion regarding a minor is contested, the court may not enter an order resolving the contested matter **that changes a child's established custodial environment** without first holding an evidentiary hearing to determine whether clear and convincing evidence exists to support the order."

*(VERIFIED verbatim.)*

**Read that carefully before you rely on it.** The evidentiary-hearing requirement in (C)(1) is triggered by an order that **changes the ECE** — and this motion's whole position is that its relief does *not* change the ECE. **You cannot argue both.** If you want the (C)(1) hearing, you are arguing the relief changes the ECE, and you have just put yourself in the clear-and-convincing lane.

**So the right request here is the narrower one:** ask under MCR 2.119(E)(2) that the motion be heard on oral testimony as to the contested facts. That asks for testimony without conceding anything about the ECE.

> "Your Honor, Plaintiff's response asserts facts about November 14 and November 28 that aren't in the record. If the Court intends to rely on them, I'd ask under MCR 2.119(E)(2) that the motion be heard on oral testimony as to those facts."

## What not to answer

- **Anything about the November 2025 hearing.** Their §IV mentions that he did not appeal or move for reconsideration. It is true, every window closed long ago, and there is no answer that helps. Let it sit.
- **Anything about the other parent as a person.**
- **Anything about money, support, or the other parent's household.**
- **Any point they made that does not affect the order you want.** Answering everything is how a hearing runs out of time before you reach paragraph 1.

# PART FOUR — The judicial hearing, and the live-evidence floor

**Back to the objection timeline from Part Two.** The objection was filed Monday, February 15, 2027. The judicial hearing is Friday, March 5, 2027.

## The calendar, with both 7-day record clocks

| Date | Day | Event | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feb 15, 2027 | Monday | Objection, notice of hearing, **and notice of intent to offer evidence from the referee record** filed and served | MCR 3.215(E)(4), (E)(5), (D)(4)(c) |
| **Feb 22, 2027** | **Monday** | *(scenario)* The court, on its own motion, provides the referee record to the parties and indicates it may limit the hearing | MCR 3.215(D)(4)(d) |

> **The February 22 row is the one to notice.** MCR 3.215(D)(4)(d): "If the court on its own motion uses the record of the referee hearing to limit the judicial hearing under subrule (F), the court must make the record available to the parties and **must allow the parties to file supplemental objections within 7 days of the date the record is provided** to the parties." *(VERIFIED verbatim.)*
>
> **That clock starts when the record is provided — not when you notice.** And it is the second seven-day clock in this case that runs from a date on a document rather than a date you chose. Open the mail the day it arrives.
>
> Note the rule's own limit: (D)(4)(d) "does not apply when a party requests the court to limit the judicial hearing under subrule (F) or when the court orders a transcript to resolve a dispute concerning what occurred at the referee hearing." *(VERIFIED.)* The supplemental-objection right attaches to the court's **own-motion** use of the record.

## Before that: the same-day hearing trap

> **MCR 3.215(E)(7):** "The court may hear a party's objection to the referee's recommendation for an order **on the same day as the referee hearing**, provided that the notice scheduling the referee hearing advises the parties that a same-day judicial hearing will be available and **the parties have the option of refusing a same-day hearing if they have not yet decided whether they will object** to the referee's recommendation for an order."

*(VERIFIED verbatim.)*

**In this fictional case the notice for the January 15, 2027 referee hearing carried that advice.** At the end of the referee hearing, the parties were asked whether they wanted the judge to hear any objection that afternoon.

> ### ⚠ Why saying yes can cost you the objection you have not written yet
>
> A same-day judicial hearing happens **before the recommendation exists in writing.** You would be objecting to findings you have not read, on a record you have not reviewed, without the *Duperon* problem visible yet, and without the omitted Hague provision visible yet — because in this case none of those defects appeared until the recommendation was signed on February 1.
>
> Every one of the six grounds in Part Two's objection was invisible on January 15.
>
> **The rule gives you the option to refuse. Take it, unless you already know exactly what you would say.**

**What the fictional parent said, on the record:**

> "Thank you, Your Honor. I haven't decided whether I'll object, because I haven't seen the recommendation yet. Under MCR 3.215(E)(7) I'd like to keep the option of a later hearing, so I'm respectfully declining a same-day hearing today."

**Then write down that you declined, and the date.** If a later dispute arises about whether the objection right was preserved, that sentence is on the court's record — and MCR 3.215(D)(4) requires an electronic or stenographic record of all hearings. *(VERIFIED.)*

> **And the sibling trap, from Part Two:** MCR 3.215(E)(8) — "The parties may waive their right to object to the referee's recommendation for an order by consenting in writing to the immediate entry of the recommended order." *(VERIFIED.)* Same day, same hallway, same result. **Do not sign, and do not agree, on the day.**

## The floor, and its ceiling

Read both halves before you walk in. The reader who knows only the first half over-objects; the reader who knows only the second gives up too early.

> **MCR 3.215(F)(2):** "To the extent allowed by law, the court **may conduct the judicial hearing by review of the record of the referee hearing, but the court must allow the parties to present live evidence at the judicial hearing.** The court may, in its discretion:
> (a) prohibit a party from presenting evidence on findings of fact to which no objection was filed;
> (b) determine that the referee's finding was conclusive as to a fact to which no objection was filed;
> (c) prohibit a party from introducing new evidence or calling new witnesses unless there is an adequate showing that the evidence was not available at the referee hearing;
> (d) impose any other reasonable restrictions and conditions to conserve the resources of the parties and the court."

*(VERIFIED verbatim.)*

And the statute the rule sits under:

> **MCL 552.507(5):** "A hearing is de novo despite the court's imposition of reasonable restrictions and conditions to conserve the resources of the parties and the court if the following conditions are met: (a) The parties have been given **a full opportunity to present and preserve important evidence at the referee hearing.** (b) For findings of fact to which the parties have objected, **the parties are afforded a new opportunity to offer the same evidence to the court as was presented to the referee** and to supplement that evidence with evidence that could not have been presented to the referee."

*(VERIFIED verbatim.)*

**What the court may do to you, lawfully:**

| The court may | So do not object when it |
|---|---|
| Decide largely on the referee record | Says it has read the record and will work from it |
| Bar evidence on findings you did **not** object to | Excludes your point about something not in your six grounds |
| Treat unobjected findings as conclusive | Says a fact you never challenged is settled |
| Bar **new** evidence or **new** witnesses absent an adequate showing it was unavailable earlier | Refuses a witness you could have called on January 15 |
| Impose reasonable time limits | Gives you fifteen minutes |

**What it may not do:** refuse to allow you to present live evidence at all. That is the floor, and it is stated in mandatory terms — "**must** allow."

## The script

**The moment.** The court says some version of: *"I've reviewed the referee's recommendation and the record. I don't think we need testimony. Let me hear brief argument."*

**Do not treat that as a ruling.** It usually is not one yet. It is an intention, and it can be met with a request.

**Beat 1 — the ask. Short, respectful, rule-first.**

> "Your Honor, before argument — I understand the Court may conduct this hearing on the referee record. Under MCR 3.215(F)(2), I'd ask to present live evidence on the findings I objected to. I have a short offer, and I can keep it brief."

**Beat 2 — if the court says the referee already heard it.**

> "I understand, Your Honor. My objection is to findings of fact, and under MCL 552.507(5)(b) I'd ask for the opportunity to offer the same evidence that was presented to the referee on those specific findings. It's the three exhibits already admitted and my own testimony on two dates. I'd expect ten minutes."

**Beat 3 — narrow it before the court has to.**

> "If it would help, I'll limit it to Ground 4 — the finding that I have not consistently sought parenting time. That's Exhibits A, B, and C, which are already in the referee record, and about five minutes of testimony."

> **Narrowing early is the single most effective move in this script.** A judge weighing "a whole new hearing" against "five minutes on one finding" is being asked a much easier question. And it is honest: Ground 4 is the finding that drives the schedule.

**Beat 4 — say what you are not asking for.**

> "I'm not asking to call any new witness or offer anything that wasn't before the referee. I understand the Court can bar new evidence under subrule (F)(2)(c), and I'm not seeking that."

**Beat 5 — if the court still declines: the offer of proof.**

> "Thank you, Your Honor. May I make a brief offer of proof for the record?"

Then, if permitted:

> "If I were permitted to testify, I would testify that on November 28, 2026 I arrived at the exchange location at 9:50 a.m., remained until 10:40 a.m., and the minor child was not brought to that location. I would offer Exhibits A, B, and C, already admitted at the referee hearing, which show three written requests mailed October 6, October 29, and November 12, 2026, each proposing specific dates and hours, and the written response dated November 17, 2026 agreeing to November 28. That evidence bears directly on the finding that I have not consistently sought parenting time."

**Beat 6 — preserve it.**

> "Your Honor, may I have the Court's ruling on the record — that live evidence is not being permitted on that finding? Thank you."

## Why the offer of proof matters

> **MRE 103(a):** "A party may claim error in a ruling to admit or exclude evidence only if the error affects a substantial right of the party and: … **(2) if the ruling excludes evidence, a party informs the court of its substance by an offer of proof, unless the substance was apparent from the context.**"
>
> **MRE 103(b):** "**Once the court rules definitively on the record**—either before or at trial—**a party need not renew an objection or offer of proof** to preserve a claim of error for appeal."
>
> **MRE 103(c):** "The court may make any statement about the character or form of the evidence, the objection made, and the ruling. **The court may direct that an offer of proof be made in question-and-answer form.**"

*(VERIFIED verbatim — `sources/primary/michigan-rules-of-evidence_2026-01-28.txt`, updated with MSC orders effective 1/28/2026.)*

**Plain version.** If evidence is kept out and you say nothing about what it was, there may be nothing to review later. The offer of proof is how you put the substance on the record without being allowed to present it. **Three sentences is enough.** It does not need to be a performance, and MRE 103(c) means the court may ask you to do it as questions and answers instead.

**And under MRE 103(b), once the court has ruled definitively on the record, you do not have to raise it again.** Ask once, clearly, get the ruling, stop. Repeating it after a definitive ruling gains nothing and costs the room.

> ### ⚠ This section is about preserving a record, not about appealing
>
> Whether an order on this motion could be appealed, and on what clock, is **outside this document**. Parenting time is absent from the appeal-of-right list at MCR 7.202(6)(a)(iii), and appeal deadlines are jurisdictional under MCR 7.204(A). *(VERIFIED.)* **If you are thinking about an appeal, that is a `DO NOT USE THIS GUIDE ALONE` moment and the clock may already be running.**
>
> Preserve the record anyway. It costs three sentences, and the alternative is discovering later that the point is gone.

## The findings ask — and a rule that is stronger than most people know

Part Two's Ground 6 objected that the referee made no findings. At the judicial hearing, ask the court for its own — and there is a rule that makes this more than a courtesy:

> **MCR 3.210(D):** "The court must make findings of fact as provided in MCR 2.517, except that **(1) findings of fact and conclusions of law are required on contested postjudgment motions to modify a final judgment or order**…"
>
> **MCR 2.517(A)(1):** "…the court shall **find the facts specially, state separately its conclusions of law**, and direct entry of the appropriate judgment."
> **(A)(2):** "**Brief, definite, and pertinent findings and conclusions on the contested matters are sufficient**, without over elaboration of detail or particularization of facts."
> **(A)(3):** "The court may state the findings and conclusions **on the record** or include them in a written opinion."

*(VERIFIED verbatim — `sources/primary/michigan-court-rules_2026-07-31.txt`.)*

**This is a contested postjudgment motion to modify.** Under MCR 3.210(D)(1), findings of fact and conclusions of law are **required** — which is an exception to the general rule at MCR 2.517(A)(4) that findings are unnecessary on motions.

**What to say, at the close:**

> "Your Honor, one last thing — this is a contested postjudgment motion to modify, so under MCR 3.210(D)(1) I'd ask for findings of fact and conclusions of law, on the record is fine."

**And two things that take the pressure off:**

> **MCR 2.517(A)(6):** "**Requests for findings are not necessary for purposes of review.**"
> **MCR 2.517(A)(7):** "**No exception need be taken to a finding or decision.**"

*(VERIFIED verbatim.)*

So if you forget to ask, or the moment passes, you have not forfeited anything. **Ask anyway** — findings are what any later step attaches to, and a court that states them on the record has given you the clearest possible picture of what it decided and why.

**If the findings are incomplete:** MCR 2.517(B) — "On motion of a party made **within 21 days after entry of judgment**, the court may amend its findings or make additional findings, and may amend the judgment accordingly." *(VERIFIED.)* Another 21-day clock, triggered by entry. ⚠ **TIME-CRITICAL**

## What not to do at this hearing

- **Do not re-argue the motion.** You are here on six specific grounds. Everything else was decided.
- **Do not raise a ground you did not put in the objection.** MCR 3.215(F)(2)(a)–(b) lets the court bar evidence on unobjected findings and treat them as conclusive.
- **Do not ask to call a new witness** you could have called on January 15. MCR 3.215(F)(2)(c), and it makes every other request look less reasonable.
- **Do not argue with a ruling after it is definitive.** MRE 103(b) means you do not need to, and continuing costs you the room.
- **Do not describe the other parent.** Same rule as every other room in this guide.
- **Do not sign anything handed to you at the end of the hearing** without reading it away from the courthouse.
