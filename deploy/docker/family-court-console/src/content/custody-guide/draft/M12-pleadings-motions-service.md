# Why this module comes where it does

Module 2 answered *what makes a case* — the established custodial environment, the burdens, the gate, the factors. This module answers a narrower and more mechanical question: **how do you actually put a request in front of the court so it gets heard and can be enforced?**

That question has its own way of ending a case. A filing can be right on the law and still die on the machinery — filed on the wrong document, served on the wrong person, served two days late, or asking for relief no judge can write into an order. When that happens the merits are never reached. The Packet 1 service materials open with a real instance of it: *"The papers were prepared; the service failed; the merits were never reached."* That is the most common way a self-represented parent **loses without losing** — and it is the most preventable, because the machine is a checklist and a calendar, not a matter of judgment or luck.

This module is that checklist and that calendar, stated once, for every custody filing. It teaches:

- the difference between a **pleading** and a **motion**, and how to tell which one your situation needs;
- the **five parts** of a motion packet, and the two extra parts Genesee requires;
- how to write **numbered material facts** tied to the legal element each one proves;
- how to frame **proposed relief a court can place on a calendar and enforce** (Acceptance Test 11);
- how to **serve the party and the attorney correctly**, and the **two separate clocks** in MCR 2.119(C);
- what your **signature** on the document commits you to — including the sanctions it exposes you to.

## 12.1 Two families of documents: pleadings and motions

Almost everything filed in a custody case is one of two things, and they are not interchangeable.

**A pleading starts or defines the case.** The court rules define the word narrowly:

> "(A) Definition of 'Pleading.' The term 'pleading' includes only:
> (1) a complaint,
> (2) a cross-claim,
> (3) a counterclaim,
> (4) a third-party complaint,
> (5) an answer to a complaint, cross-claim, counterclaim, or third-party complaint, and
> (6) a reply to an answer.
> No other form of pleading is allowed."
> — MCR 2.110(A)

**A motion asks the court to *do something* in a case that already exists.**

> "(1) An application to the court for an order in a pending action must be by motion. Unless made during a hearing or trial, a motion must
> (a) be in writing,
> (b) state with particularity the grounds and authority on which it is based,
> (c) state the relief or order sought, and
> (d) be signed by the party or attorney as provided in MCR 1.109(D)(3) and (E)."
> — MCR 2.119(A)(1)

The practical test for which one you need is the same case-stage question from Module 1:

| Your situation | The document that carries your request | Where it is taught |
|---|---|---|
| There is **no case yet** — no divorce, paternity, or custody action filed | A **complaint** (a pleading) that starts the action | 12.2 below (basics only); Module 1 for the road; a lawyer for paternity/marriage gatekeeping |
| A case is **pending** and you were served | An **answer**, and possibly a **counterclaim** (pleadings); then **motions** for interim relief | 12.2, 12.3 |
| A **final order already exists** and you want it enforced, clarified, or changed | A **motion** in that same case — not a new complaint | 12.3 onward; Packet 1 for the worked instance |

> ### ⚠ Do not open a new case when a motion belongs in the old one
> Where a circuit court already has a case about these parties and this child — pending or resolved with continuing jurisdiction — a new request that was available in the original action is generally brought as a **motion in the earlier case**, not as a fresh complaint (MCR 3.204(A)(1)), and post-judgment domestic-relations motions run through the motion-practice rule, MCR 2.119 (MCR 3.213). Filing a new complaint when a motion was required can cost a filing fee and a hearing date, and it can land in front of a different judge. The Packet 1 motion draft works this routing in full (`P1-motion-packet-specific-parenting-time.md`, "Route tag").

Everything from 12.3 forward is about **motions**, because a motion is what a reader in a live custody dispute files far more often than a pleading. Section 12.2 covers the initial-action layer briefly, for the reader who is starting or answering a case.

## 12.2 If you are starting or answering a case (the initial-action layer)

Most readers arrive at this module needing a motion. If you are instead **starting** a case or **responding** to one just filed, a different set of rules governs first. This section is an orientation, not the full treatment — starting a divorce or a paternity case is its own body of law, and Module 1 routes it.

**Commencement and the summons.** "A civil action is commenced by filing a complaint with a court" (MCR 2.101(B)). On that filing, "the court clerk shall issue a summons" (MCR 2.102(A)) — you do not write your own; the clerk issues it, and it carries the deadline by which the other side must answer.

**The clock to answer.** A defendant served in Michigan "must serve and file an answer or take other action permitted by law or these rules **within 21 days** after being served with the summons and a copy of the complaint" (MCR 2.108(A)(1)); the period is **28 days** if service was outside Michigan or by registered mail (MCR 2.108(A)(2)). ⚠ TIME-CRITICAL — this is a forfeiture clock; a missed answer can lead to default (below).

**The answer, and the trap of silence.** A responsive pleading must, for each allegation, "state an explicit admission or denial," plead no contest, or state a lack of knowledge, which "has the effect of a denial" (MCR 2.111(C)). Silence is not neutral:

> "Allegations in a pleading that requires a responsive pleading, other than allegations of the amount of damage or the nature of the relief demanded, **are admitted if not denied** in the responsive pleading."
> — MCR 2.111(E)(1)

**The counterclaim.** Your own affirmative request — for custody, parenting time, support — can be combined with your answer as a counterclaim, "clearly designated as such" (MCR 2.110(C)). It is how a responding parent asks for relief instead of only resisting.

**What a domestic-relations case-initiating document must contain.** Beyond the general rules, family cases carry extra required content:

> "Except for matters considered confidential by statute or court rule, in all domestic relations actions, the complaint or other case initiating document must state (a) the allegations required by applicable statutes; (b) the residence information required by statute; and (c) the complete names of any minors involved in the action, including all minor children of the parties and all minor children born during the marriage…"
> — MCR 3.206(A)(2)

Three attachments ride along and are easy to miss:

- **Prior-case disclosure.** When any other family-division or tribal-court case involving these family members exists, "the filing party must complete and file a case inventory listing those cases." That inventory "is confidential, not subject to service requirements" (MCR 3.206(A)(3)).
- **The UCCJEA affidavit.** In any case where custody or parenting time "is to be determined or modified, the filing party shall file a Uniform Child Custody Jurisdiction Enforcement Act Affidavit" (MCR 3.206(B)). This is jurisdictional information, not a routine form — Module 1 and Module 3 own it. 🛑 If another state or country is or has been involved, that is a stop-and-get-counsel point.
- **The verified statement.** "In an action involving a minor, or if child support or spousal support is requested," the party seeking relief provides the Friend of the Court a verified statement of identifying, financial, and health-coverage information, served on the other party (MCR 3.206(C)(1)).

**Confidential identifiers — the child, above all.** Protected personal identifying information (dates of birth, Social Security numbers, financial-account numbers) is handled under a specific redaction regime: "The responsibility for excluding or redacting personal identifying information … rests **solely with the parties and their attorneys**. The clerk of the court is not required to review, redact, or screen documents at time of filing" (MCR 1.109(D)(10)(a)). Where a Social Security number must appear, "it shall be the last four digits only" (MCR 1.109(D)(9)(b)(ii)). This project's own rule is stricter and is not a balancing test: **the child's name and birthdate do not go into a public filing beyond what the rule affirmatively requires** (`GUARDRAILS.md` §8, §11a). The mechanics of address confidentiality for a party at risk are in FM.5 and in the Packet 1 service materials.

**The default safeguard — a shield if it happens to you, a duty if it happens to them.** A domestic-relations judgment cannot be rubber-stamped when a party does not show up:

> "A judgment of divorce, separate maintenance, or annulment may not be entered as a matter of course on the default of a party because of failure to appear at the hearing or by consent, and **the case must be heard in open court on proofs taken**, except as otherwise provided by statute or court rule."
> — MCR 3.210(B)(5)(a)

And where children are involved, the court "may take testimony and receive or consider relevant and material affidavits, testimony, documents, exhibits, or other evidence, as necessary, to make findings concerning the award of custody, parenting time, and support" (MCR 3.210(B)(5)(d)). A party moving for a default judgment must serve the motion, notice of hearing, and proposed judgment on the defaulted party "at least **14 days** before the hearing" in the situations listed in MCR 3.210(B)(4)(a). So a default in a custody case is not the automatic loss it is in an ordinary debt case — but it is dangerous, and it is a place to get help rather than to improvise. 🛑

> **When 12.2 is out of its depth.** Paternity establishment, the grounds and waiting periods for divorce, and service of the original summons and complaint (which follows MCR 2.105, *not* the later-document rules in 12.4) each have their own law. This section names them so you know they exist; it does not teach them. Module 1 routes the road; a limited-scope consult (FM.4) is worth its cost here.

## 12.3 Anatomy of a motion — the five parts, plus Genesee's two

Every contested custody motion is a **stack of documents that travel together**. Miss one and the packet is incomplete; the clerk may reject it (MCR 1.109(D)(6)), or the judge cannot act on it. Here is the whole stack, with the rule that requires each piece. Nothing here is Genesee-specific except the two rows so marked.

| # | Document | What it does | Required / permitted by |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The motion** | States, in writing, the grounds, the authority, and the relief sought; signed | MCR 2.119(A)(1) |
| 2 | **A brief in support** | Required when the motion "presents an issue of law"; carries the argument and the citations | MCR 2.119(A)(2) |
| 3 | **A notice of hearing** | Tells everyone the date, time, and place the motion will be heard | MCR 2.119(A)(3) — may be combined with the motion |
| 4 | **Affidavit(s) / verified statement** | Puts sworn *facts* in front of the court where the facts are not already of record | MCR 2.119(B) |
| 5 | **Proof of service** | Proves who was served, with what, how, and when | MCR 2.107(D) |
| **G1** | **Proposed order** ℹ | Genesee requires one "attached to and served with the motion" | **Genesee LCR 2.119(B)** |
| **G2** | **Concurrence certificate** ℹ | Genesee requires a signed certificate that you sought the other side's agreement first | **Genesee LCR 2.119(A)** |

Two administrative copies ride with the stack where the court is still on paper (Genesee is — FM.4): a **Friend of the Court copy** marked "friend of the court," handed to the clerk at filing when a child under 18 is involved or support is at issue (MCR 3.203(G)); and a **judge's copy** marked "JUDGE'S COPY" (MCR 2.119(A)(2)(d)). And a **fee, or a Fee Waiver Request** (MCR 2.119(G); MCR 2.002). The Packet 1 service materials carry the full "who gets what" table and the fee-waiver walkthrough; this module does not restate them.

### The brief, and what you do *not* get to file

> "(a) Except as permitted by the court, the combined length of any motion and brief … may not exceed **20 pages double spaced**, exclusive of attachments and exhibits.
> (b) Except as permitted by the court or as otherwise provided in these rules, **no reply briefs, additional briefs, or supplemental briefs may be filed.**
> (c) Quotations and footnotes may be single-spaced. At least one-inch margins must be used, and **printing shall not be smaller than 12-point type.**"
> — MCR 2.119(A)(2)

The consequence of (b) is strategic, not just formal: **there is normally no reply as of right.** Whatever you expect the other side to argue, the place to answer it is your opening brief — because you will not, absent the court's permission, get a second piece of paper. The Packet 1 worked example builds a response and answers it out loud at the hearing precisely because the paper record closes early.

### The notice of hearing — a date you cannot invent

> "A motion and notice of the hearing on it may be combined in the same document."
> — MCR 2.119(A)(3)

You may combine them, but you cannot **fill in a hearing date you made up.** How a motion gets a date — whether by contacting chambers, a scheduling clerk, a praecipe card, or a posted motion day — is a local-practice question. In Genesee, motion day is Monday, and the local rule (LCR 2.119) contains **no praecipe requirement**, though the counter may still use one; the current procedure is a `ℹ PROVISIONAL` item to confirm with Circuit Court Records (FM.4; Module 4.3; and the Packet 1 service materials, which list the questions to ask in one call).

## 12.4 The motion body: numbered material facts, tied to elements

The single most common defect in a self-drafted motion is that it reads like a letter — a narrative of everything the other parent did, in the order the writer remembers it, wrapped around adjectives. Courts do not read motions that way, and the rules do not let you write them that way.

> "(1) All allegations must be made in **numbered paragraphs** …
> (2) The content of each paragraph must be limited **as far as practicable to a single set of circumstances.**"
> — MCR 2.113(B)

That is the whole discipline in two lines: **one fact, one paragraph, one number.** But numbering is only the form. The content rule is that each numbered fact should be *material* — meaning it proves an element the court actually has to decide. This is where Module 2 and this module meet: Module 2 tells you which elements matter (the ECE, the threshold, the specific factor); this module tells you to give each element a dated fact.

**The rules for a paragraph that survives being read aloud in a flat voice** (adapted from the Packet 1 motion draft, which states them for the parenting-time instance):

- **One fact per paragraph, each with a date.** A paragraph without a date is a feeling, not a fact.
- **Quote an order; never characterize it.** Paste the provision word for word inside quotation marks. Let the court read what it says.
- **No adjectives about a person.** If a sentence would change meaning when read aloud by a stranger who does not know you, rewrite it.
- **The banned words appear nowhere.** "Narcissist," "alienator," "abuser," "gaslighting," "parental alienation," "PAS," "coercive control." Michigan reaches that conduct through pleaded *facts* mapped to a statutory factor, not through a label (`GUARDRAILS.md` §2; Module 2.4 factor (j); Module 11). A filing that carries those words becomes a filing about the person who wrote it.
- **Map each fact to the element it proves.** Beside each paragraph in your own notes, write the element: *this proves the ECE is unchanged; this proves failure to comply under MCL 722.27a(7)(g).* A fact that maps to no element is probably not material — cut it. Burying the material facts under every slight is its own defect (relevance and the court's patience, Module 6, MRE 401/403).

**Where the worked instance lives.** The Packet 1 motion draft (`P1-motion-packet-specific-parenting-time.md`, §C.2 and the fictional worked example) shows paragraphs 2–6 of a real-shaped motion doing exactly this — an order quoted, a dated request record, an ECE paragraph, a "will-not-change-the-ECE" paragraph — with a note on why the boring paragraphs are what make the one devastating paragraph land. Read it as the concrete example of the rule above; this module does not reproduce it.

> ### ⚠ A pleading-content rule specific to custody and parenting-time requests
> A verified pleading or affidavit that "requests a change of custody or parenting time" must include facts on **whether the child has an established custodial environment** and facts on **whether the requested order would change it** (MCR 3.207(B)(1)(a)). The rule sits under the ex-parte heading, and whether it binds an ordinary noticed motion is a fair question — but including those facts costs a paragraph, is exactly what the court must find anyway under MCL 722.27(1)(c), and is what puts a parenting-time request in the lower-burden lane. The Packet 1 motion draft (§B.2) works this in full; Module 2.1–2.3 owns the underlying standard.

## 12.5 Affidavits and exhibits — evidence, not argument by volume

A motion states what you want and why the law allows it. When the facts you rely on are **not already in the court's file**, you put them in front of the court as sworn evidence — an affidavit or a verified statement — with the documents attached. The rule sets the quality bar:

> "(1) If an affidavit is filed in support of or in opposition to a motion, it must:
> (a) be made on personal knowledge;
> (b) state with particularity facts admissible as evidence establishing or denying the grounds stated in the motion; and
> (c) show affirmatively that the affiant, if sworn as a witness, **can testify competently** to the facts stated in the affidavit.
> (2) Sworn or certified copies of all documents … referred to in an affidavit must be attached to the affidavit unless the documents [are already filed, are public record in the county, are in the adverse party's possession, or attaching them would be unreasonable]…"
> — MCR 2.119(B)

Three things follow, and each cuts against the instinct to file everything:

1. **Personal knowledge, admissible facts.** An affidavit is not a place to argue or to relay what someone told you. "She always keeps him from me" is not admissible fact; "On October 18, 2026, I arrived at the exchange location at 9:50 a.m. and no child was produced" is. If you could not say it as a witness on the stand, it does not belong in the affidavit. (What is admissible, and how a text or a screenshot gets in, is Modules 5–7. Michigan has **no self-authentication shortcut** for electronic records — Module 7 — so plan the affidavit around a fact you can actually prove.)
2. **Attach the document, do not describe it.** If the affidavit refers to a letter, a receipt, an order, attach a copy (MCR 2.119(B)(2)). A described-but-absent document is an argument, not evidence.
3. **Volume is not weight.** A three-hundred-page exhibit binder does not make a case stronger; it makes the material facts harder to find and the writer look unable to tell what matters. Over-documenting "buries the material facts and makes the writer look obsessive" (outline, Module 6; Module 11's strategy traps). Attach what proves an element. Nothing else.

**A verified pleading can do an affidavit's job.** Rather than a separate affidavit, a pleading or statement may be *verified* — signed under a declaration that its contents are true — using the exact language in MCR 1.109(D)(3)(b) (quoted and worked in the Packet 1 service materials, §D). A knowingly false verification carries contempt exposure on top of the sanctions in 12.7. Copy the declaration language exactly; do not paraphrase it.

## 12.6 Proposed relief the court can place on a calendar and enforce

This is the part the outline calls the operational-order test (Acceptance Test 11), and it is where good custody motions separate from doomed ones. The motion must "state the relief or order sought" (MCR 2.119(A)(1)(c)) — and in Genesee, that relief is not left to a paragraph of prose; it is written out as a **proposed order** that rides with the motion:

> **SEVENTH JUDICIAL CIRCUIT (GENESEE), Rule 2.119(B) Proposed Orders.** "A proposed order must be attached to and served with the motion."
> — Genesee LCR 2.119(B) (`sources/primary/local-court-rules-circuit_2025-06-01.md`; "Updated with MSC order(s) effective on 6/1/2025")

The proposed order is where "what I want" becomes "what a judge could sign today." The test for every line is the one the Packet 1 draft states for the parenting-time schedule and that generalizes to any relief:

> **Could a stranger, holding only this order and knowing nothing about your case, carry it out without asking a single question?**

If the answer is no — if a line says "reasonable," "as agreed," "as the parties arrange," "mutually convenient," or leaves a *who*, a *when*, or a *where* blank — that line is a future argument, not an enforceable term. Vague relief is worse than none: the Friend of the Court "may decline to respond" to an alleged violation where "the … order does not include an enforceable provision that is relevant to the … violation alleged" (MCL 552.641(2)(c), quoted in full in Packet 1). **Specific terms now are enforcement later.**

**The relief-precision checklist (works for any custody filing):**

- [ ] The relief names a **person**, an **action**, and — where it matters — a **time**, a **date**, and a **place**.
- [ ] No line uses "reasonable," "as agreed," "as appropriate," or "mutually convenient" to carry the operative term.
- [ ] Every recurring obligation says **who** does it and, where money is involved, **who pays**.
- [ ] The order says what happens to the **rest of the existing order** ("all other provisions … remain in effect").
- [ ] Any content the governing statute makes **mandatory** for that kind of order is present. (For a parenting-time order, MCL 722.27a(10)'s Hague-Convention paragraph is mandatory content — Module 2.5; Packet 1 §A.4. Different relief carries different mandatory content; check the governing statute.)
- [ ] The relief is the **narrowest** thing that solves the actual problem. Asking for more than you need can flip a winnable request into a harder one — the over-ask trap in Module 2.7 — and can raise your own burden.

**Match the ask to the standard.** How much you ask for decides which test you face. A request that would move a child's established custodial environment is a custody request carrying the clear-and-convincing burden, no matter what the caption says (Module 2.3, 2.7). Frame the relief precisely *and* size it honestly; the proposed order is where both disciplines show.

**The menu of conditions, for the parenting-time instance.** A parenting-time order "may contain any reasonable terms or conditions that facilitate the orderly and meaningful exercise of parenting time" — transportation, ready-times, arrival-and-return times, notice of a missed period, a communication channel, and more (MCL 722.27a(9), quoted verbatim in Packet 1 §A.4). That menu is the raw material for turning "he was late again" from a grievance into a breach of a written term. Other kinds of relief have their own statutory hooks; the principle — *write the term so it can be broken and proved broken* — is constant.

## 12.7 Signature, verification, and the sanctions you are signing up for

Every document is signed, and the signature is not a formality — it is a certification with teeth.

> "(2) Requirement. Every document of a party represented by an attorney shall be signed by at least one attorney of record. **A party who is not represented by an attorney must sign the document.**"
> — MCR 1.109(E)(2)

> "(5) Effect of Signature. The signature of a person filing a document, whether or not represented by an attorney, constitutes a certification by the signer that:
> (a) he or she has read the document;
> (b) to the best of his or her knowledge, information, and belief formed after reasonable inquiry, the document is **well grounded in fact and is warranted by existing law** or a good-faith argument for the extension, modification, or reversal of existing law; and
> (c) the document is **not interposed for any improper purpose, such as to harass or to cause unnecessary delay** or needless increase in the cost of litigation."
> — MCR 1.109(E)(5)

> "(6) Sanctions for Violation. If a document is signed in violation of this rule, the court, on the motion of a party or on its own initiative, **shall impose** upon the person who signed it … an appropriate sanction, which may include an order to pay to the other party … the amount of the reasonable expenses incurred because of the filing of the document, including reasonable attorney fees…
> (7) Sanctions for Frivolous Claims and Defenses. In addition to sanctions under this rule, a party pleading a frivolous claim or defense is subject to costs as provided in MCR 2.625(A)(2)."
> — MCR 1.109(E)(6)–(7)

Read those together, because they are what you commit to every time you sign. When you file without a lawyer, **you are the signer**, and the certification is yours: that you read it, that it is grounded in fact and law after reasonable inquiry, and that you did not file it to harass or delay. The sanction for a signature that violates the rule is not discretionary in whether it applies — the court "shall impose … an appropriate sanction." This is the concrete meaning of the Genesee concurrence certificate too: signing it certifies you actually made the contact it describes (12.8; Packet 1 §C.5).

## 12.8 The Genesee concurrence certificate — and it binds you

Genesee adds a requirement the statewide rule does not: before your motion reaches the counter, you must certify that you asked the other side to agree to the relief and were turned down (or that you tried in good faith to reach them).

> **SEVENTH JUDICIAL CIRCUIT (GENESEE), Rule 2.119(A) Motion Certification by Attorney.** "The following certificate signed by the attorney of record **or by the party in propria persona** shall be attached to or incorporated in the motion and notice of hearing filed with the clerk:
>
> *I hereby certify that I have made personal contact with ________________________[name] on ________________[date], requesting concurrence in the relief sought with this motion and that concurrence has been denied, or that I have made reasonable and diligent attempts to contact counsel requesting concurrence in the relief sought with this motion.*"
> — Genesee LCR 2.119(A) (`sources/primary/local-court-rules-circuit_2025-06-01.md`, eff. 6/1/2025)

Three things a self-represented parent should take from it:

1. **The heading says "by Attorney"; the text says "or by the party in propria persona." The text controls.** You are covered by this rule. Leaving the certificate off puts the motion out of compliance with a binding local rule.
2. **You have to do it before you can certify it.** The certificate has two branches — concurrence requested and denied, *or* reasonable and diligent attempts to contact counsel. Where communication in a case already runs through the other side's attorney, that contact is the second branch — which is convenient, because it is the same outreach the case may already require.
3. **Sign it honestly.** The certificate carries the full weight of the MCR 1.109(E)(5) certification in 12.7. A concurrence request sent as its own short letter (enclosing the proposed order, asking for agreement by a stated date) makes the certificate write itself and leaves a receipt that proves it.

`ℹ PROVISIONAL` — whether the assigned judge expects the certificate on its own page or incorporated into the motion, and whether the counter expects any scheduling card, is a local-practice item to confirm with Circuit Court Records (FM.4; Packet 1 service materials).

## 12.9 Serving it: the party, the attorney, and the two clocks

Service is where the last filing in the Packet 1 case failed. The full mechanics — permitted methods, the backward calendar, the proof of service, the fee waiver, the phone-call list — live in `P1-service-pack.md`, which this module does not duplicate. What every custody filing shares, and what you must carry away from here, is three ideas.

### Who gets served — the party *and* the attorney

The intuitive rule is "the other side has a lawyer, so I serve the lawyer." That is the opening clause of the service rule — and it has an exception that fits post-judgment custody cases:

> "(1) Service required or permitted to be made on a party for whom an attorney has appeared … must be made on the attorney except as follows: … **(c) After a final judgment or final order has been entered and the time for an appeal of right has passed, documents must be served on the party** unless the rule governing the particular postjudgment procedure specifically allows service on the attorney…"
> — MCR 2.107(B)(1)

Chapter 3 then requires, independently, that "copies of notices required to be given to the parties **also must be sent to the attorneys of record**," an obligation that continues "until 21 days after judgment is entered or until postjudgment matters are concluded, whichever is later" (MCR 3.203(I)). Reading these together produces one instruction that satisfies every version of the rule and violates none:

> **Serve the party. Also serve the attorney. Same papers, same day. Prove both on one proof of service.**

An extra copy breaks no rule; guessing wrong about who was the required recipient can undo the whole filing. Mail the party's copy to the **last known mailing address** as MCR 3.203(B) defines it (the address most recently given to the Friend of the Court, or the one in the most recent order) — not an address you happen to remember. If the party's address is confidential by order, the Friend of the Court supplies a service address; do not investigate it yourself. (Packet 1 service materials, Part A.)

### The two clocks — service and filing are different deadlines

This is the trap that is right on the county's own website. MCR 2.119(C) sets **two separate obligations**, and meeting one does not meet the other:

> "(1) … a written motion …, notice of the hearing on the motion, and any supporting brief or affidavits must be **served** as follows:
> (a) **at least 9 days** before the time set for the hearing, if served by first-class mail, or
> (b) **at least 7 days** before the time set for the hearing, if served by delivery…"
> — MCR 2.119(C)(1)

> "(4) Unless the court sets a different time, a motion must be **filed at least 7 days** before the hearing, and any response … must be filed at least 3 days before the hearing."
> — MCR 2.119(C)(4)

- **(C)(1) is about SERVICE** — 9 days if you mail it, 7 days if you deliver it.
- **(C)(4) is about FILING** — 7 days, however you serve.

Notice the mismatch: the filing minimum (7 days) is *shorter* than the mail-service minimum (9 days). If you file at the last legal moment, you cannot possibly serve by mail on time. The safe order is **file early, then serve the same day**, with both dates well inside the minimums.

> ### ⚠ The trap of omission on the county notice
> Genesee posts a 2019 "Notice Regarding Motion Practice" that states the **filing** rule (7 days) and is silent on the **service** rule. A reader who follows only that notice, mails the motion 7 days out, and thinks they are on time is **two days late on service**. Both deadlines apply. This is a documented gap in an official-looking source (`GUARDRAILS.md` §6; FM.4 carries the warning; the Packet 1 service materials work a full backward calendar, including a version that collides with a holiday week). ⚠ TIME-CRITICAL — every date here belongs in Appendix A and on a calendar you actually check.

A **response** is served at least 5 days (mail) or 3 days (delivery) before the hearing and filed at least 3 days before (MCR 2.119(C)(2), (C)(4)). And remember 12.3: there is normally **no reply** (MCR 2.119(A)(2)(b)) — plan to answer a response out loud at the hearing.

### Prove it, on paper

Service is proved by "a written statement by the individual who served the documents, verified under MCR 1.109(D)(3)," and the proof "must be filed promptly and at least at or before a hearing to which the document relates" (MCR 2.107(D)). It does not require a notary; it does require that the person who actually mailed the papers be the one who signs, and that the proof **name every document served** — including the proposed order (Genesee LCR 2.119(B)) and the concurrence certificate. The Packet 1 service materials carry the full proof-of-service aid and the rule for who may do the mailing where a communication restriction is in place.

## 12.10 ⚠ When *not* to file — the over-filing trap

Nothing above discourages filing, and a guide that only teaches how to file will produce a reader who files too much. That is its own way to lose. Every enforcement tool in this book can be aimed **at the reader** (`GUARDRAILS.md` §7), and the filing rules are no exception.

- **Frivolous filings draw mandatory sanctions.** The MCR 1.109(E)(6) sanction is not optional in *whether* it applies (12.7), and MCR 1.109(E)(7) routes frivolous claims to costs under MCR 2.625(A)(2), which provides that "if the court finds on motion of a party that an action or defense was frivolous, costs shall be awarded as provided by MCL 600.2591." (⚠ VERIFY — the verbatim text of MCL 600.2591 is **not in this project's primary archive** (`sources/primary/mcl/` is empty); it is cited here for its effect through the court rules that invoke it. Confirm the statutory text before quoting it in a filing. The fee-and-sanctions statute is Module 24's territory.)
- **Fees can shift — in either direction.** In a domestic-relations case, "a party may, at any time, request that the court order the other party to pay all or part of the attorney fees and expenses" (MCR 3.206(D)(1)), on facts showing inability to pay and the other's ability, **or** that "the attorney fees and expenses were incurred because the other party refused to comply with a previous court order … or engaged in discovery practices in violation of these rules" (MCR 3.206(D)(2)). The noncompliance branch points both ways — it is a hook against a stonewalling opponent and a cost if *you* are the one not complying.
- **A frivolous referee objection has its own sanction.** "If the court determines that an objection is frivolous or has been interposed for the purpose of delay, the court may assess reasonable costs and attorney fees" (MCR 3.215(F)(3)). (Referee objections are Module 16.)
- **There is a judicial-patience budget.** Repeated emergency motions, overbroad discovery, unsupported accusations, and bad-faith contempt cost credibility that no rule scores but every judge keeps.

**A pre-filing sanity check** (adapt from the Packet 1 "when not to file" list):

- Can you say, in one sentence and without adjectives, what you want the order to say? If not, the motion is not ready.
- Does every material fact have a **date and a document** behind it? A motion supported by three certified letters is a different motion from one supported by a story.
- Is this the **narrowest** relief that solves the problem, or an over-ask that raises your own burden (Module 2.7)?
- Would this be your **third or fourth** motion in a short span? As a rule of thumb, one well-built motion beats four thin ones; if you are filing often, ask whether clarification or a single comprehensive motion would do the work instead.
- Is there a **safety, CPS, criminal, PPO, or interstate** overlay? If so, stop — this is a get-help point, not a file-faster point. 🛑

## 12.11 Amendment, adjournment, and the proposed-order dance

**The uncontested-order route — try agreement before you notice a fight.** Before filing a contested motion, you may serve the other side a proposed order and a request to stipulate:

> "(1) Before filing a motion, a party may serve on the opposite party a copy of a proposed order and a request to stipulate to the court's entry of the proposed order. … A proposed order is **deemed rejected unless it is stipulated to or notice and hearing are waived within 7 days** after it is served."
> — MCR 2.119(D)

If the proposed order is rejected and you then file the contested motion, "a copy of the rejected order and an affidavit establishing the rejection must be filed with the motion" (MCR 2.119(A)(4)). This route can turn an agreed change into a signed order without a hearing — and it dovetails with the Genesee concurrence requirement, since the outreach it takes is the same.

**Amendment.** The time to answer or act can be extended by the court "if the request is made before the expiration of the period originally prescribed," and afterward "on motion" for excusable neglect (MCR 2.108(E)); pleadings are amended under MCR 2.118. Keep amendments clean and dated; do not use an amendment to smuggle in a new case.

**Adjournment.** Motions and stipulations to adjourn are governed by MCR 2.503 (MCR 2.119(D)(5)) — and a rescheduled hearing does **not** by itself reset the two service clocks for anything newly filed. A verbal assurance that a date moved is not enough: if the court changes a service or filing time, "its authorization must be endorsed in writing on the face of the notice of hearing or made by separate order" (MCR 2.119(C)(3)).

## 12.12 Motions for reconsideration — the cheap first move

If the court rules against you on a motion, there is a fast, inexpensive step before anyone talks about an appeal:

> "(1) … a motion for rehearing or reconsideration of the decision on a motion must be served and filed **not later than 21 days after entry of an order** deciding the motion.
> (2) No response to the motion may be filed, and there is no oral argument, unless the court otherwise directs.
> (3) … The moving party must demonstrate a **palpable error** by which the court and the parties have been misled and show that a different disposition of the motion must result from correction of the error."
> — MCR 2.119(F)

A motion that "merely presents the same issues ruled on by the court … will not be granted" (MCR 2.119(F)(3)) — reconsideration is for a genuine, demonstrable error, not a second argument of the same case. It is cheap, it is fast, and it can preserve an issue. ⚠ TIME-CRITICAL — the 21 days runs from **entry of the order**, and it is *not* the same clock as an appeal.

> ### 🛑 Reconsideration is not an appeal, and it does not pause a clock
> An **appeal of right** runs **21 days from entry** of a final order and is **jurisdictional** — no judge can give the time back (FM.0; Module 27). Filing for reconsideration does not, by itself, stop an adverse order from taking effect, and an appeal ordinarily does not either. If any appeal or stay clock is running, that is a **DO NOT USE THIS GUIDE ALONE** situation — get advice today, and do not let a reconsideration motion run out the appeal clock.

## Templates and tools

These are drafting aids. None is filing-ready; each assumes you have read the standard it rests on. The annotated model motion, brief, notice of hearing, and proof of service are collected for the parenting-time instance in the Packet 1 drafts (the future Appendix D); use those as the worked models.

**1. Motion architecture worksheet.** One page. Top: the one-sentence statement of relief. Then a row for each of the five parts (12.3) plus the two Genesee parts, with a checkbox and the governing rule. You cannot draft the packet until every row has an owner.

**2. Affidavit fact-quality checklist.** For each sentence in a draft affidavit: *Is it on my personal knowledge? Could I say it as a witness? Is it a fact with a date, not a characterization? Is the document it refers to attached?* Cut any sentence that fails a box (MCR 2.119(B)).

**3. Relief-precision checklist.** The 12.6 list. Run the proposed order against it line by line; the last line is the stranger test.

**4. Service-and-proof matrix.** Columns: *recipient (party / attorney / FOC / court / judge) · what they get · rule · method · date served/filed.* Fill it from the Packet 1 "who gets what" table; the point is that no row is blank and both clocks (12.9) are met.

**5. Filing-packet table of contents.** The 12.3 table, filled in for your motion, doubling as the cover checklist the day you go to the counter.

**6. The 10-point rejection checklist.** A motion should score 10/10 before it leaves your hands. Each point is a verified rule, and each is a reason a clerk may reject a filing (MCR 1.109(D)(6)) or a judge cannot act:

1. **Caption complete** — court, parties, case number with its year prefix and case-type suffix, and (self-represented) your name, address, and phone (MCR 1.109(D)(1)(b)).
2. **Format conforms** — 8½ × 11, legible, English, 12-point or larger, one-inch margins, motion-plus-brief no more than 20 pages double-spaced (MCR 2.119(A)(2)(c); MCR 1.109(D)(1)(a)).
3. **Signed** — by you, if self-represented; an unsigned document "shall be stricken" unless promptly signed (MCR 1.109(E)(2)–(3)).
4. **Grounds and authority stated with particularity** (MCR 2.119(A)(1)(b)).
5. **Relief clearly stated** — and mirrored in the proposed order (MCR 2.119(A)(1)(c)).
6. **Brief attached** if the motion presents an issue of law (MCR 2.119(A)(2)).
7. **Proposed order attached and set to be served** (Genesee LCR 2.119(B)).
8. **Concurrence certificate present, signed, and truthful** (Genesee LCR 2.119(A)).
9. **Notice of hearing with a real, court-given date** (MCR 2.119(A)(3); FM.4).
10. **Proof of service** names every document, shows service on the party *and* the attorney, and meets both clocks — 9-day mail service and 7-day filing (MCR 2.107(D), (B)(1); MCR 3.203(I); MCR 2.119(C)(1), (4)). Fee paid or Fee Waiver Request filed with the packet (MCR 2.119(G); MCR 2.002).

## Exercises

1. **Convert a narrative into eight numbered material facts *(scenario exercise).*** Take a page of "what happened" and rewrite it as eight numbered paragraphs, each a single dated fact, each mapped in your notes to the element it proves (12.4; MCR 2.113(B)). *Done when no paragraph contains an adjective about a person, every paragraph has a date, and every paragraph names the element it supports — and any paragraph that maps to no element has been cut.*
2. **Draft an enforceable prayer for relief and test it against a calendar *(fill-in).*** Write the relief as a proposed-order paragraph, then hand it to someone who knows nothing about your case and ask them to tell you exactly who does what, when, and where. *Done when the second reader can execute the requested order without asking you a question, and when the relief contains no "as agreed," "reasonable," or blank (12.6; Acceptance Test 11).*
3. **Score a draft motion against the 10-point rejection checklist *(self-assessment).*** *Done when the score is 10/10 and the service-and-proof matrix shows the party, the attorney, and the FOC each accounted for with both clocks met.*

## When to stop and seek counsel 🛑

- You are **starting or defending a paternity or divorce action**, or facing a **default judgment** in one — the initial-action layer (12.2) names these; it does not teach them. 🛑
- A **PPO, CPS, criminal, or safety** matter touches the facts you would plead. Filing does not suspend any of them, and what you write in a custody motion can be used against you elsewhere. 🛑 SAFETY OVERRIDE / DO NOT USE THIS GUIDE ALONE
- **Another state or country** is or has been involved, or the UCCJEA affidavit raises a jurisdiction question (12.2; Modules 1, 3). 🛑
- An **appeal, reconsideration, or objection clock** is running. Treat it as running **today** (12.12). 🛑
- The other side moves for **sanctions or fees**, or argues your **service was defective** — read it the day it arrives, note every date, and get advice (12.9, 12.10).
